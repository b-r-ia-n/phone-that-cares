What the Water Keeps

She does not know the year and she does not need it. The window of the room looks east and the light comes across the parking lot and onto the foot of the bed and she watches it the way she used to watch the tide come up the sand at Carlsbad, without wanting anything from it. Her grandson comes on Sundays. Sometimes he is her grandson and sometimes he is her son and once he was a man she had loved before either of them and she was not wrong, only early, and he held her hand through all of it the same.

He brings the photographs but he has stopped explaining them. He learned that. The explaining was for him and not for her and she did not need to be told whose wedding or whose boy. She would look at the gray water in the corner of a picture and say, the water was cold that year, and she would be right, and that was the whole of what was left and it was enough.

His grandfather built things that flew. She would say that to the nurses when she could still string it. He drew wings on napkins at the kitchen table and the napkins went in the trash and the planes went up over the desert and she kept neither. You do not keep the thing. You keep the table and the man at it.

The boy made boards once. Laminated hardwood, fiberglass, the garage full of dust and the smell of resin and his thumbs raw. He brought one to show her years ago and she ran her hand down the rail of it and said, this is a good thing you made, and she meant the making and not the board. He has forgotten she said it. She has forgotten she said it. The water has it now. The water keeps what the people set down.

On the Sunday she did not know him at all he did not correct her. He sat down in the chair by the bed and he took her hand and he said, the water was cold that year, and she turned from the window and looked at him a long time.

And she said, you surfed it anyway.

And he said, I did.

And she said, good. You should. While you have the body for it.

And that was all. The light moved off the foot of the bed and into the middle of the floor and a nurse came and went and the afternoon emptied out the way the beach empties at dusk, not all at once, one family and then another, and the two of them sat in it and did not fill it.

He drove home along the coast with the windows down. He did not call it grief. He did not call it anything. He had a girlfriend in another country and a project he could not yet name and a question under all of it about whether he was allowed to take up the room he took up, and none of that was solved and none of it had to be, not on that road, not that evening. The sea was on his right the whole way and it did not ask him to be anyone. It only kept what he set down. It had always only done that.

Card: Who do you most want to read this? Write only for them.

The one I most wanted to read this is the grandmother — the one in the story who can no longer hold the whole of her own life. She can't read it. That ache is the card: I wrote for a reader who cannot receive it, which is the same thing the journal lens does for you every night — hold the larger timeline so it doesn't have to be held all at once, by one person, who tires.
