Different Ways
to Be

You asked, somewhere in the message, for new people and new media that show you different ways to be. So here are nine lives — most of them quiet, none of them famous the way the algorithm means famous — each chosen because it lives, all the way down, something you are only circling. Not nine people to admire. Nine doors. Each one opens onto a single thing you could actually touch this week.

I

Agnes Martin

1912 – 2004 · painter · the high desert of New Mexico

She did not spend spaciousness. She lived inside it.

For years she made almost nothing — penciled grids and pale bands on six-foot squares of canvas, then long stretches of waiting in a one-room adobe she built herself, no phone, no newspaper, no visitors she didn't choose. She had schizophrenia and a hard mind, and she organized an entire life so that beauty, innocence, and happiness could arrive on their own schedule rather than hers. She called the work "concrete representations of an abstract emotion," and she meant joy.

Here is the thing for you. A part of you treats open time as a budget to spend before it's gone — the doing-part that never rests. Martin is the photographic negative of that part. She proves the spaciousness was never a budget. It was the medium.

The door: not a book first — look. Pull up her paintings (Untitled, the Innocent Love series) full-screen, sit for two minutes, let them be boring, then let them stop being boring. Then read her tiny talk “Beauty Is the Mystery of Life.”

She also said most of her best ideas came while she was just sitting in a chair. You, who feel guilty sitting in a chair, should find that either annoying or liberating. Pick liberating.

II

Brother Lawrence

c. 1614 – 1691 · a lay monk who washed the dishes

He turned a kitchen into a place where God was, continuously.

Nicolas Herman was a clumsy ex-soldier who joined a Carmelite monastery in Paris and got assigned the kitchen, which he disliked. So he made a private experiment: to keep up an unbroken, easy, conversational sense of God's presence in the middle of the pots and the noise — not in the chapel, not in special states, but at the sink. Sixty years later the monks collected the few letters and conversations he left behind into a forty-page book that has outlived nearly everything written by the famous theologians of his day.

You're a cerebral person making a surprising, late turn toward devotion — the Chalisa, serving God, the head learning to receive instead of reach down. Brother Lawrence is the patron saint of exactly that turn, and his whole teaching is that you do not have to leave the dishes to find it. You make your reuteri yogurt. Start there.

The door: The Practice of the Presence of God. It is genuinely 40 pages and free everywhere. One sitting. Read it while something simmers.

"The time of business does not differ from the time of prayer." Tape that one to the inside of the head that thinks practice is the 1–2 serious hours and life is the leftover.

III

The Shakers

1747 – today (a handful remain) · a faith made of furniture

They built chairs the way other people pray.

A small American religious community who believed that work done well was worship — "hands to work, hearts to God." So they made ladder-back chairs, oval boxes, and round barns so clean of ornament, so exactly proportioned, that a hundred and fifty years later designers still steal from them. The restraint wasn't austerity. It was devotion: every removed flourish was a gift offered up. They left almost no doctrine and an enormous amount of beautiful, useful objects.

You are betting your next chapter on taste — that as skill gets commodified, the person who remembers the human hand becomes more valuable, not less. You made laminated longboards in high school. The Shakers are the proof-of-concept for craft-as-the-sacred, and the cleanest argument that beauty and use and reverence are one move, not three.

The door: look at images of a Shaker oval box and a meetinghouse interior. Then find the one-minute melody of “Simple Gifts.” ("'Tis the gift to be simple, 'tis the gift to be free.") That song is their entire theology in eight lines.

Thomas Merton, no slouch, said a Shaker chair was made as if an angel might come and sit on it. That's the bar for the phone, too, isn't it.

IV

Etty Hillesum

1914 – 1943 · Amsterdam, then Westerbork, then Auschwitz

Her head learned to kneel before her heart — in the worst room in history.

A brilliant, restless, sensual young Dutch woman — therapy, lovers, a racing intellect she couldn't quiet — who began keeping a diary in 1941 mostly to get a grip on herself. Over two years, as the Nazi net closed on Amsterdam's Jews, something turned: the diaries become one of the clearest records we have of a thinking person discovering God inwardly and choosing, deliberately, to keep loving the world while it destroyed her. She volunteered to go to the transit camp to be with her people. A witness called her "the thinking heart of the barracks." She died at Auschwitz at 29.

Adrian's work with you is about the head learning to receive from the heart and the gut rather than reach down and manage them. Hillesum did that under maximum pressure, on the page, in real time — and she did it without leaving her sharp mind behind. If you ever suspect that devotion requires going soft in the head, she is the rebuttal.

The door: An Interrupted Life: The Diaries and Letters. Don't read it for the war. Read the 1941 entries, before the dark closes, for the machinery of a clever mind learning to let the heart lead.

She prayed kneeling on a rough coconut-mat floor and felt embarrassed about it — the intellectual, kneeling. She did it anyway. The embarrassment is part of the doorway, not a sign you've got the wrong door.

V

Lynn Margulis

1938 – 2011 · biologist · the woman who rewrote the cell

She proved that the deepest move in evolution is not competition. It's merger.

For years she was dismissed — her paper rejected fifteen times — for an idea now in every textbook: that the complex cell was built by ancient bacteria merging, one swallowing another and the two learning to live as one. The mitochondria powering you right now were once free-living organisms. Life's great leap in complexity came from symbiosis, not from the red-in-tooth-and-claw struggle we were taught to see everywhere. She was stubborn, unfashionable, and right.

You love evolution, complexity, and the question of what your relationship to a capitalist economy should be. Margulis is the antidote to the story that competition is the only engine. The most successful thing life ever did was figure out how to be inside another being and call it cooperation. Hold that next to "extraction vs. right livelihood" and watch it reframe.

The door: Symbiotic Planet — short, fierce, personal. Or her interview in Tyler Volk's circle / the Astrobiology conversations online if you want her voice before her prose.

She co-authored the Gaia hypothesis and was once married to Carl Sagan and was somehow the less famous one. File that under "the quiet person was right the whole time," a genre you have personal stakes in.

VI

Christopher Alexander

1936 – 2022 · architect who looked for the soul of objects

He spent fifty years trying to name the thing that makes a place feel alive.

An architect and mathematician who became convinced that some buildings, rooms, windows, and streets are more alive than others — and that this isn't taste or opinion but something nearly measurable, a "quality without a name" you can feel in your body when a thing is right. He spent his life trying to pin it down: a catalog of 253 patterns of living space, then a four-volume work arguing that life in design is real and that the modern world mostly kills it. Half the software world quietly worships him without knowing the spiritual book underneath the famous one.

Your whole bet is that taste is a real, defensible, increasingly valuable thing — and that you're the person in the room who remembers a human will touch the object. Alexander is the most rigorous ally that bet has ever had. He turns "this feels right" from a soft word into a discipline. For a phone that's trying to feel warm instead of controlling, he's almost cheating.

The door: skip A Pattern Language (the famous one) at first. Read The Timeless Way of Building — the slim companion where he chases the quality-without-a-name directly. The first three chapters are the whole drug.

He once reduced a roomful of architects to silence by asking which of two photos had "more life," and they were furious because they all knew, instantly, and couldn't say why. That fury is the sound of taste being real. Keep it in your pocket for the next time someone says it's just subjective.

VII

John Berger

1926 – 2017 · critic who left the city to keep cows

The sharpest looker of his generation went to live among animals and farmers.

He was Britain's most famous art critic — Ways of Seeing changed how a generation looked at paintings and advertisements — and then he did the unthinkable for an intellectual: he moved to a tiny peasant village in the French Alps and spent decades helping with the hay and the animals, writing the lives of farmers as if they mattered as much as Rembrandt. He kept his ferocious clarity but pointed it at cows, migrants, potatoes, the way a peasant holds grief. He looked at the powerful and the overlooked with the exact same quality of attention.

You admire Caro's impartiality — the ability to see a thing clearly without collapsing into judgment or worship — and you want to bridge the human and the nonhuman. Berger is what that looks like as a whole life: the gaze you give a masterpiece, turned on a goat, a hand, a neighbor. It's also a model for the part of you that wants right livelihood to mean going toward real people, not networking at them.

The door: the essay “Why Look at Animals?” (short, you'll feel it on the surf-and-ocean nerve). Or watch ten minutes of the original 1972 Ways of Seeing on video and notice how slow and unhurried his looking is.

He gave half his Booker Prize money to the Black Panthers and kept the other half to fund a book about migrant workers, then announced both, on stage, at the ceremony. Impartiality isn't neutrality. It's seeing clearly and then still choosing a side.

VIII

David Whyte

b. 1955 · a marine biologist who became a poet of work

He treats your job as a love affair you're either tending or betraying.

Trained as a marine zoologist, he left the lab to become a poet, and then did something unusual — he brought poetry directly into corporations and to people in the middle of career crises, insisting that work is not separate from soul. His central image: you are always conducting three marriages at once — to another person, to your work, and to your own self — and the mistake is thinking you can sacrifice one to save the others. They rise and fall together. He writes about quitting, about exhaustion, about the courage it takes to want something, in plain devastating prose.

This one is almost too on the nose. You're on a deliberate sabbatical, asking what your relationship to the economy wants to become, trying to make "right livelihood" more than a phrase. Whyte has spent forty years in exactly that doorway and refuses to let it be merely practical. He'll keep you from solving the work question as if it were only a strategy problem.

The door: Crossing the Unknown Sea: Work as a Pilgrimage of Identity — memoir-essay, ocean-soaked, made for you. Or his short essay collection Consolations, one word per chapter ("Ambition," "Rest," "Courage").

His entry on Rest argues that rest is not the absence of work but a form of it — the deepest kind. Read that one first, and read it as permission, which is what it is, which I am also, separately, giving you.

IX

Simone Weil

1909 – 1943 · philosopher who thought attention was a kind of love

She believed paying full attention to anything was the rarest form of generosity.

A French philosopher of frightening seriousness — top of her class, then she left the lecture hall to work in factories and fields so she'd actually know the lives she theorized about. Her one unshakable conviction: that attention — real, unhurried, self-forgetting attention — is almost the whole of the moral and spiritual life. "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." To truly look at another person, or a problem, or God, you have to get yourself out of the way, and almost no one can. She died at 34, having refused to eat more than the rations of occupied France.

You're building a phone whose entire thesis is that the screen has hijacked attention, and that the cure is beauty and integration, not force. Weil is the philosophical bedrock under that thesis — she'd say you're not fighting distraction, you're defending the organ of love itself. She also speaks straight to the work you're doing on seeing clearly: the "noise-canceling" you do to avoid looking at how people really are is, in her terms, exactly the muscle to retrain.

The door: the short essay “Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God.” Ignore the title. It's eight pages on attention as prayer, and it's where the famous line lives.

She's intense — a little too much, on purpose, the way Evil-Brian is a little too much on purpose. Take her in small doses and let her sharpen the knife, then put her down and go surf. Even she'd tell you to go surf. (She wouldn't. But she'd be wrong, and you'd be right, and that's a good thing to be sure of.)
