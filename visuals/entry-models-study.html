Entry models

Eight shapes for the same underlying move — making the user's intention into a structural part of how the phone is opened. A working document, not a spec.

The principle we're protecting

The act of opening the phone should externalize a piece of intent that would otherwise stay invisible. The four-direction swipe is one shape of that. The principle is the spine. Everything else is up for redesign.

The current prototype puts four named modes — Discover, Ask, Connect, Home — around a 160px circle, with a puck you drag toward one of them. It's honest about what it's doing, which is rare. But a small UI element is carrying a lot of conceptual weight, and the visual feel is mechanical rather than satisfying. The threshold-color reveal is the small magic; everything else is utility.

So: eight candidates below. Each is a shape for the same principle. Some are evolutions of the swipe; some are radically different. Each gets a name, a one-sentence gesture, what intent it surfaces, where it works, where it breaks, and a kinship — who's done a cousin of this. The honest verdicts at the end of each are mine, not consensus.

A research agent is currently pulling primary-source design history from the people who built the canonical phone entry surfaces (iPhone, Android, webOS, Light Phone, calm-tech writers). Findings will fold into the synthesis section once they're back.

02

Press and speak

Hold the power button, say a word — "scroll," "write," "music," "Marie." The phone opens into that.

Intent
Whatever you said. Most direct mapping of internal-to-external there is.
Strong
Lowest physical friction. Highest expressive range — not limited to four modes. Speech is naturally intentional in a way swipes aren't.
Weak
Social cost (you don't whisper "instagram" in a meeting). Reliability of on-device speech. No graceful fallback when you don't know what to say.
Kinship
Star Trek communicators · Rabbit R1 (failed-but-instructive) · Alexa/Siri but as an entry point not an app.

My read: Powerful as a secondary entry path, not the primary. Always-available power-button-speak alongside whatever the main visual gesture is. The social cost rules it out as the default.

Adjacent: the home screen

Once past the lock, what does the user land in? Brian floated the idea of scroll-apps living on the home screen with a different visual treatment — grayscale icons, dimmed corners — to mark them as "you're entering this differently." That's not an entry-model but a follow-through: how the chosen mode visibly inflects what you find on the other side.

Approach A — uniform homeSame home screen always. Mode lives in the act of entering, not in what you find. Cleaner but throws away the chance to reinforce the choice.
Approach B — mode-tinted homeThe mode you entered shows up in the home screen's palette, app order, or chrome. Reinforcement is constant but subtle. Risks looking busy.

After reading the research: this isn't actually adjacent anymore. The hinge insight makes it structural. If the home screen doesn't visibly carry the mode forward, the entry gesture is theatre. So Approach B is no longer "nice if we get to it" — it's part of the principle, and the open question is just how loud the carry-forward is. I'd lean toward a soft version (chrome/wallpaper/app-grouping shifts) over a loud one (entirely different app set per mode) because loud risks both visual exhaustion and discoverability collapse — but the design move that's no longer optional is "the home screen knows what mode you entered in."

Where I'd push next

Brian read the original eight and gave a sharp correction: most of them imagined intention without modeling the wall-clock cost. Slide-to-unlock takes ~0.3s; my "type what you're here for" takes 10× that plus model latency. I was generating shapes for intention but not pricing them. The bathroom-door-latch insight was about deliberateness without obstruction, and I let the second half drop.

The criterion I'd now use to pick between models is two-axis: (a) which entry gesture pairs with a persistent, visible mode-state on the home screen, and (b) which can do it without adding seconds to the most-frequent interaction on the device?

Top recommendation: prototype 09 (Four rooms with doorways). Same swipe people already do — no added friction at entry — but the mode lives in the home screen via icon opacity. Hinge built in at zero time cost. This is the model.

Plus one modifier: The frame (06) as the idle state. Ambient image when the phone is at rest; the four-room entry kicks in only when you wake it on purpose. Cheap, inherits existing PTC vocabulary, probably our biggest single move for "calm."

The other models, re-graded against the time-cost lens:

  • 04 Vertical asymmetric pull — still strong; passes the time test (a pull is a pull). Worth holding as the backup if four-rooms hits a wall.
  • 05 Type to enter — fails the time test as default. Keep as an optional secondary path reachable from any room (long-press anywhere, get a text field) for the rare moments where typing intent is faster than scanning a 12-icon grid. The principle was correct; the layer was wrong.
  • 02 Press-and-speak — same: an always-available secondary path on the power button, not the default.
  • 01 The wheel — kill. Two-step gesture costs seconds for skeuomorphic appeal we can't deliver on glass.
  • 03 Mode pages — superseded by 09, which is the better version of this idea.
  • 07 Pre-commit duration — right thought, wrong layer. Save for in-app moments of weakness, not unlock.
  • 08 Argos-mediated — defer to v2. Asking a question at entry pays a time-and-attention cost; worth it only when Argos's character is settled enough to land. Weiser/Case caveat applies.

The throughline from the design history: the strongest precedents (Sidekick hinge, slide-to-unlock, webOS cards) treat entry as a small physical commitment with consequences for system state. The weakest (Live Tiles, Light Phone menu) treat it as a surface to be configured. Our principle lives on the strong side, and the gesture should too.

What the design history says

Brief digest of what the people who built the canonical entry surfaces were actually trying to do. The full agent transcript is longer; this is the load-bearing condensed version.

Slide-to-unlock was a bathroom door latch

The original iPhone gesture (Ording / Anzures / Chaudhri under Christie) was modeled on the green→red sliding latch on an airplane bathroom door — a physical-world mechanism that cannot be triggered accidentally. The whole point was deliberateness. Our principle is the same idea, richer: not just "you meant to open the phone" but "you meant to open it for this." We are downstream of slide-to-unlock, not departing from it.

Holo and Material under Matias Duarte went the other way — entry was treated as friction to remove, replaced with delight (ripples, expressive transitions). The unlock was decorated, not loaded with meaning. Most of contemporary Android lives in this lineage. Our move is a deliberate return to the slide-to-unlock register — entry as a moment that asks something of you.

webOS's cards (also Duarte) were state-forward, not app-forward — the entry experience returned you to what you were doing, not to a launcher. The "throw a card up to dismiss" gesture had real committedness; closing was an act, not a back-button accident. Our inversion: the entry surface represents what you intend to do next, with the same committedness.

Windows Phone's Live Tiles is the closest historical precedent to our principle — and the instructive failure. Albert Shum's team bet on "glance and go": the surface would tell you what was happening so you wouldn't need to enter the app. Tiles failed for two reasons: developers never invested in the content, and Microsoft conflated information surfacing with intent externalization. The system spoke; it never asked what you came for. We have to not make that mistake — the entry surface listens before it speaks.

Sidekick had a hinge, and this turned out to be the agent's sharpest insight: the 180° swivel was a visible, single-axis state change that the device committed to. Closed = consumption; open = composition. The form factor was the intent gesture, and once the Sidekick was open, it was open. There was no way to pretend you hadn't committed.

The hinge question

Our current prototype is a momentary gesture that resolves into a regular Android home. Which means the mode effectively evaporates the instant you're past the lock. If we're serious about the principle, the mode has to live somewhere visible after entry, not just at the moment of entry. The home screen has to be a hinge — visibly in the chosen state, not a neutral launcher under a colored gesture.

This reframes Brian's mode-tinted home screen idea from "nice modifier" to "structural requirement." If we don't carry the mode forward, we don't actually have the principle — we have a fancier lock screen.

On tonal target: Daylight Computer's "warm-not-cold restraint" — paper-like surface, amber backlight, "comes alive when summoned" framing — is the closest precedent for being restrained without being ascetic. The device rests, it doesn't withhold. This vocabulary is ours to claim. Light Phone is the foil to learn from.

On Argos-mediated entry: Weiser/Case calm-tech principles explicitly frame the entry moment as the wrong place for the system to assert itself. A question on the lock screen is the system asserting. This doesn't kill option 08 but it sharpens the line — the question has to be something you welcome, never something that intercepts.
