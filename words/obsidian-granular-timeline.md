**Full Internal Timeline Narrative**

Spring/Summer 2024 — There's this intuition. It was a particular UI concept — a screen gradually turning grayscale. Obviously grayscale mode works, but people will forget to turn it on, or it won't be able to be turned on in a smart or flexible way. There was a little bit of speculative design work with Matthew, my younger brother, where he was exploring the possibility of having apps gradually go grayscale. And basically what we found out was that it wasn't possible — that Google would not let you do this. At the time, Comma. We don't imagine that this would be intentional on Google's part. We assumed it was an unfortunate coincidence of the way Android was set up. But in retrospect, this seems perhaps like less of a coincidence.

Fall 2024 — More thinking about theoretically what it would look like, trying to get less confused. There was an exploration of Freedom and various self-control type apps — opal, lightphone, palm mini, the one where you plant trees: for every half hour you set your mobile phone face down and don't move it, it plants a little tree in-game for you. A nice mechanic. But overall there was a process of exploring existing apps and software intended to help you with addiction, and finding that they generally did not work. To be honest, it was an intuition I already had — there are a lot of people who are kind of addicted to their phones and not having a good time, and generally if one of these actually worked, I would expect there to be a mimetic adaptation that spreads like wildfire. A lot of the framing in this space treats people as not agentic. I think this might be true of addiction in general — there are frames in which people aren't agentic, and that's obviously true. But there are also frames where, if there was a good solution that just felt better, people would absolutely just take it. They would get it on their phone, or they would stop using heroin, or whatever. People want to live harmoniously with themselves, with their substances or technology or whatever — they're just in one frame, and they don't have the right affordances available yet to do so.

November 20, 2024 — The first "Phone That Cares About You" brainstorm. Attended by @zencephalon, @Samswoora, @Tautologer, maybe one other person. Google Meet conference. A couple conversations around this time with Jane, @joespurpleshirt. This period was where it started to become more of a concrete project.

Spring 2025 — Applied for and did not receive an O'Shaughnessy Ventures grant to work on the project. The grant writing process was an opportunity to get down a much more concrete summary of what at the time felt like a kind of disparate project — able to concretize it more, and narrow down to a plan A/B/C type structure.

February 15, 2026 — Quit normal full-time work. Working on this project probably twenty hours a week since then.

Present/Near Future — The Scroll Lab Chrome extension: experiments proving out that UI-layer interventions can change how content feels. A custom Android OS build (ArgOS) targeting LineageOS on a Pixel 6. The first stage of the device wanting to sell for significantly more money than either a top-of-the-line Galaxy or an iPhone — because the story is this thing is more useful. It's actually going to give you hours back. It's going to be more valuable in your life than another phone. Both a negative side (there will be things it won't do) and a positive side: in some narrative formulations, the thing being added is agency — working like a bouncer, blocking certain content from appearing on your device, but also actively helping you: scraping your social media feeds or news for things relevant to your interests, helping you reach out to friends, that sort of thing.

Longer Future — The first phone manufacturer to ship with genuine care built in forces the others to follow or go obsolete. In retrospect, the system was fairly unstable. Hundreds of millions of people spending thirty hours a week on social media they did not especially value or identify as wanting to do was never going to be a business practice that lasted a hundred years. Much like the opium dens era, it's a sort of embarrassing thing that happened. The tacit, unspoken agreement by all mobile device providers to not try particularly hard to explore ways to make these devices less addictive made them a lot of money for a bit — but was an unstable equilibrium. As soon as any one device started to incorporate features that respected people's time and autonomy, the rest had to follow or go obsolete.
