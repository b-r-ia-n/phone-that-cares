## Visualization ideas

**From your original message:**

1. **Content pipeline showing who pays whom** — Creation → algorithmic sorting → delivery → display → consumption, with the phone/display highlighted as the _only_ link where the user is the customer rather than the product. The "paid link" in an otherwise unpaid chain.
2. **Exploded view of a phone UI** — Showing all the layers of an operating system: what's always on top, what's behind, what gets occluded by what. Making the implicit z-axis of an OS explicit. Specifically showing how things like Messenger pop-ups and WhatsApp notifications sit above everything else and can be dragged around.
3. **Comic strip: how "screen time" affordances exacerbate internal conflict** — "Screen time" in scare quotes. Showing the way current phone tools for managing usage actively pit parts of the user against each other rather than integrating them.
4. **The parts of a user as cartoon characters** — Rendered lovably and understandably, not pathologized:
    - The part that likes consuming content (for good reasons)
    - The part that values connection to people you'd otherwise never reach — the beauty, wonder, and magic of it
    - The part that feels the harm, the cost, the physical discomfort of long scrolling sessions Possibly shown in related/adjacent diagrams rather than one.

**Added since:**

5. **Perceptual layer between content and user** — A mutable layer that sits between the content pipeline and the eye, transforming how content is experienced without changing what's delivered. Reference: xkcd #3228 "Day Counter" reading modes. Possible direction: the same feed shown through 4–5 different "modes."

**Top-Level Project Overview** **Attempts**

**Attempt 1 — "This is absurdly tractable and no one's doing it"**

- Billions of people spend hours a day on their phones and don't enjoy it
- It's not an accident — it's the product of research in the lineage of Las Vegas slot machines, using everything we know about human psychology to get people to do things they don't, on reflection, want to do
- This could probably be solved by a project costing at most several million dollars
- The fact that no one has done it is absolutely nuts
- Concretely: spend $3M running experiments on how to make phones less addicting, then start a company implementing what you learn, and cut the time humans waste on TikTok by something like 20%
- Warning: if you are successful, you will make google facebook etc etc etc very mad

**Attempt 2 — Google at steady state**

- Google became a huge company by taking a small cut in exchange for helping people navigate a beautiful, fledgling, branching, unfurling internet
- Now we've hit steady state
- To keep growing, Google has to turn those same skills around — making it _harder_ to navigate, keeping people trapped in the same realms for longer and longer
- Now hundreds of millions of people are walking around confused by the dissociation machines we've built
- Part of why this persists: people working in tech have been lured into the false belief that what's happening is fine
- The core problem with phones is that they'll happily and freely do _some_ of the things we want — but not everything we want — and that asymmetry is weaponized against us under the pretense of neutrality

## Questions

**On the core reframe**

1. What's the thing you believe about phones and attention that you think almost no one else believes — or that people nod along to but don't actually operate as if they believe?
2. You've written that this isn't about addiction or willpower. What do you think the "addiction" frame gets _actively wrong_? Not just "it's incomplete" — where does it send people in the wrong direction?
3. The insight about the phone being "the only place in the pipeline where the user is the customer" feels like a load-bearing one. When did you first see it that way? What clicked?
4. If you had to name the single most underappreciated fact about how the current mobile software stack is structured, what would it be?

**On the pipeline and the intervention point**

5. Walk me through the content pipeline as you see it. Where are the links? And for each link, who's paying whom, and who's working for whom?
6. You've landed on the display / device layer as the leverage point. What are the _other_ points in the pipeline you considered and rejected, and why were they worse?
7. Is there a version of this project that intervenes at a different layer — and if so, why isn't that the one you're building?

**On what current solutions get wrong**

8. Screen time trackers, app blockers, grayscale modes, Light Phone, Opal, Freedom, Dumb Phone, the Apple Screen Time dashboard — which of these do you think is closest to right, and which is most misguided? Why?
9. What's the specific mechanism by which restriction-based tools _create_ the problem they're trying to solve? You've mentioned internal conflict — can you describe what's actually happening inside a user when they hit a Screen Time limit and tap "ignore"?
10. Is there anything the current tools get right that your framing risks losing?

**On the parts of a user**

11. You mentioned several "parts" of a user — the one that enjoys scrolling, the one that values the connection, the one aware of the harm. Are there other parts you'd add? (A part that wants to be informed? A part that's bored? A part that wants to avoid a harder task?)
12. Which part do you think current phones serve well, which do they serve badly, and which do they completely ignore?
13. When those parts conflict, what does a _good_ resolution look like — not in terms of phone features, but in terms of the internal experience you want the user to have?

**On what healthy looks like**

14. What's your image of a person who has a healthy relationship with their phone? Describe them concretely. What do they do differently from most people? What does it look like from outside, and what does it feel like from inside?
15. Is there a historical or cultural analog for what you're trying to build — a precedent where humans successfully domesticated a superstimulus or a powerful tool? What did that look like, and what can we learn from it?

**On the speculative phone itself**

16. If I opened the box on your theoretical phone, what's the first thing I'd notice? The first thing I'd notice after a day of use? After a month?
17. Name 2–3 specific interactions or moments where your phone would behave visibly differently from an iPhone, and what the user would feel in that moment.
18. Is there any existing product, app, or feature — in any category, not just phones — that gestures at the spirit of what you're building? Something where you've thought "that, but for phones"?

**On the business and collaborators**

19. You mentioned needing to get people to buy into the story before VC or technical co-founders come on board. What's the objection or skepticism you anticipate most, and what do you wish you had a better answer to?
20. Who's the ideal first collaborator or funder for this — not by name, but by type? What kind of person would read the website and immediately get it?
