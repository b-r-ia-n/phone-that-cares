# A Letter on Who Owns the Bench

*Yvon Chouinard, called for the founder-decides moment, after the three crafts have said the venture tempo will destroy what you are trying to build*

I read your fermentation log. Three crafts agreed your tempo will kill your discipline, and then nobody told you what to do about it. I'm going to try.

I want to start by saying I am not a businessman and never have been. I am a climber who solved a piton problem and then ended up with a company because no one else was going to make the gear right. I tell people that and they think I am being modest. I am not. The day I understood I was responsible for a company was the day I understood I had a problem I did not want, and the next forty years were me trying to keep that problem from eating the thing I actually cared about, which was the work — the climbing, the surfing, the rivers, the rock. A lot of what I am going to say to you is about how to keep the problem from eating the work. I am not going to pretend it is easy. I am eighty-six years old and we only really solved it in 2022, and even now I am not sure we solved it. We made it harder for the next people to break it. That is what you can do. That is most of what you can do.

So. Your three crafts told you the venture-capital tempo, as currently structured, will destroy your discipline. They were right. They did not tell you what to put in its place because the cabinetmaker has never had to and the midwife has never had to and even the Toyota man works inside an industrial economy that has had a century to build its scaffolding around him. None of them have stood where you are standing, which is on the floor of a company that does not yet exist, with a hand on a tap that will pour in money on terms you can still set. I have stood there. I want to tell you what I learned, and where I think it translates, and where I think it does not.

## The bench question is an ownership question

The cabinetmaker told you the screen is the bench and the bench has to be true. He is right and the metaphor is good and I want to push it one degree further than he did, because I work with wood too and I have built more than one bench.

The thing about a bench is: somebody owns it. The bench in my forge in Ventura, somebody owns it. The bench in the Patagonia design shop in Reno, somebody owns it. And here is the thing that the cabinetmaker, working inside a one-man shop or a small Shaker community, does not have to face — when somebody owns the bench, what they want determines, over time, what the bench is used for. If the owner wants the bench used for fine joinery, fine joinery is what happens at the bench. If the owner needs the bench to be used for cranking out plywood crates fast because his note is due, plywood crates is what happens at the bench, and after enough plywood crates the bench is not flat anymore. The cabinetmaker assumes the question of who owns the bench is settled, because in his shop it is settled — he is one man, his name is on the lease, and the question of whether he will press the bench into use that ruins it is his own moral question, which he can settle inside his own conscience.

You do not have that shop. You have a company. The question of who owns the bench is not settled, and how you settle it is the most consequential decision you will make. More consequential than the device, more consequential than the team, more consequential than the OS fork. Because every other discipline — the gemba walk, the cord, the postpartum visit, the four-month apprenticeship, the half-as-much voice — every one of those disciplines is something the owner has the right to abolish on a Tuesday afternoon when the quarterly numbers come in. If the owner is one of the people who knows what the bench is for, that day will not come. If the owner is a person or an institution or a class of investors whose claim on the bench is *for return*, that day always comes. Not on the first Tuesday. Not on the fifth. But eventually a Tuesday comes when the numbers are bad and the gemba layer looks like an expense and the postpartum visit looks like a luxury and the cord-pull obligation looks like an SLA the company cannot afford. And on that Tuesday, the answer to *what is the bench for* will be settled by *who owns it.*

This is the part the three crafts could not tell you, and it is what you actually have to think about. Your fermentation log used a word at the very end of the long discussion — *institution* — and then said *we do not yet have a theory of it.* I am going to give you part of one. Not all of it. Most of it has to be invented for your case. But part of it.

## What I did, and why each part of it

I will walk you through what we built at Patagonia and why, and then I will tell you which parts I think might travel to you and which parts I do not think will travel. Try to read this as workshop talk between two people who have built things, not as a case study.

I started Chouinard Equipment in a chicken coop because I needed climbing gear that didn't exist and I made it on a coal-fired forge with a hammer. For years that was it — me, then a few friends, hand-forging pitons and selling them out of the back of a car. Then we started making clothing because climbers also need clothes and the clothes that existed were bad. The clothing business made money in a way the hard-goods business never did. By the time it was clear we had a real company on our hands I had already done the thing I am proudest of, which is that I sold off the hard-goods business — sold off the *original* business, the one I started everything with — because I had figured out that the pitons we made were destroying the rock I climbed on. I watched one of my pitons being driven in and out of a crack on Half Dome, season after season, and I watched what was left of the crack at the end of the season, and I understood that the very thing I was making with my hands was the thing that was wrecking what I loved. We stopped making them. We switched the company over to aluminum chocks that didn't damage the rock. The hard-goods line nearly killed the company in the transition. I have never regretted it.

I bring this up because it is the founding decision under everything that came after, and it is the only one of these decisions I would call a *moral* one in the simple sense. Every other decision — every governance trick, every trust structure, every footprint chronicle — flows downhill from the original act of saying *the thing I am making is hurting the thing I love and I am going to stop making it and take the hit.* If you do not believe, somewhere deep, that you would do that for your product, the rest of this letter is not going to help you. You are going to need to do the equivalent of the piton thing at some point with your phone. You will ship a feature that you discover is hurting the people it is for, and you will have to scrap it, and the scrapping will cost you the quarter. If you can do that, you have the necessary character for what comes next. If you cannot, no governance structure will save the company. Governance does not save companies from their founders. It only saves them from the people who come after.

Then over decades, I did a number of things in sequence, and the sequence matters because each one bought us the time and the right to do the next one.

We never went public. This was the load-bearing decision and we made it again and again over thirty years. Going public means accepting the obligation to grow on a clock that is not yours. The clock is the quarterly earnings call. The clock is the analyst's model. The clock is the institutional shareholders who must show their LPs that the position is appreciating. The clock has no idea what your bench is for and no obligation to find out. The clock will, mechanically, force every discipline that does not register on the clock to be cut. The postpartum visit does not register on the clock. The gemba layer does not register on the clock. The half-as-much voice does not register on the clock. Going public is a decision to make those disciplines structurally indefensible in your own company, and you will defend them anyway, every year, with less and less success, until you are exhausted or fired and the company finishes the cut without you. We watched this happen to companies in our industry over and over. We refused. People offered me a lot of money to change my mind. The money would have ruined the company. I knew that and they did not.

We took 1% for the Planet — a self-imposed tax on revenue, not profit, paid to environmental groups, every year, regardless of whether the year was good. We did it before anyone asked us to and we did it loud enough that other companies started doing it too. The reason we did it was structural, not moral, although it was also moral. The reason was: if you do not lock in a discipline early, when the company is small and the temptation is small, you will not be able to lock it in later when the company is big and the temptation is big. The 1% is small. We could afford it then and we could afford it later. But the 1% was a *promise we made to ourselves in public, on the record, before we had a reason to break it,* and that meant the promise outlived the people who made it. Locking in disciplines while the locking is cheap is the entire game. Do it before you are big enough that the lock-in becomes expensive, because by then you will have invented enough plausible-sounding reasons to skip the locking.

We did the footprint chronicles — radical transparency about the damage our supply chain actually does. Not the marketing version. The real version. We published the dye runoff from our cotton suppliers and the carbon load of our shipping and the conditions in the factories we used. We did it before anyone asked us to and we did it before any of our competitors did and the reason we did it was that we had been a company long enough to know that the *brand-as-asset* trap was the deadliest of all the traps. The moment you start protecting your brand instead of doing the work the brand was a side-effect of, you have started to die, and you will not feel any difference for several years. The footprint chronicles were a structural defense against the temptation to start protecting the brand. They made it impossible. We had committed to publishing what we found, and the only way to make what we found bearable to publish was to actually do the work to clean it up. Publishing was the cord we pulled on ourselves.

We did the Don't Buy This Jacket ad on Black Friday — an ad we ran in the New York Times that said, in effect, *please consider whether you need this jacket; the most environmental thing you can do is buy less of our stuff and keep what you have longer.* Critics said it was a stunt. It was not a stunt; it was a public commitment that obligated us, internally, to actually build the Worn Wear program — to set up our own repair operation, to take back used Patagonia clothing, to refurbish it, to resell it. The ad was the announcement. The program was the consequence. We had committed in front of a million readers to a position from which it would have been embarrassing to retreat, and so we built the operations that made the position true. *Make public commitments that obligate the operations you cannot yet bring yourself to fund.* That is a real management technique and I am not sure I have ever heard anyone say it out loud.

We put in childcare in 1983 because the women who built the company could not stay if they could not bring their babies. We did it before HR theory caught up to it and we did it not as a benefit but as an *operating necessity* — we could not run a company on the people we wanted to run it with unless we let those people bring their kids to work. This is the equivalent, in our world, of your gemba layer: a structural admission that the thing you depend on requires conditions that the standard playbook will not fund. You have to fund those conditions yourself, before you can prove they pay back, on the bet that they will pay back because the thing you depend on is what the conditions hold up.

And I went climbing. I was out of the office for months at a time. People called this Management by Absence and made it sound like a philosophy. It was not. It was: I am a climber and I am going to climb, and the company is going to have to function without me, and if it cannot function without me then I have built it wrong and I had better fix it before I am the only thing holding it together. The reason this matters for you is that *every operation that depends on the founder being in the room is an operation that will not survive the founder leaving the room.* The cabinetmaker said the half-as-much voice cannot be the founder alone. The midwife said the practice cannot be the practitioner alone. The TPS man said the substance has to be deposited into the floor before the founder leaves. I will say it in my own terms: *go climbing for two months and see what the company does. Whatever falls down while you are gone is what you have not actually built. Build it. Then go climbing again.* The version of this for your project is harder because Brian is one person and his hands are on a hundred surfaces. But the principle holds. The disciplines you cannot leave the room for are the disciplines that do not actually exist yet; they are personality traits of yours that the company is borrowing.

And then in 2022, after almost fifty years of building the company without selling it and without taking it public and without letting it grow faster than its discipline could absorb, my wife and our two adult children and I gave the entire company away. We did not sell it. We did not put it in a foundation. We put 100% of the voting shares — about 2% of the company's economic value — into the Patagonia Purpose Trust, a non-charitable trust whose sole job is to enforce the company's mission in perpetuity. The trust has no beneficiaries; it has a job. We put 100% of the economic shares — the other 98% — into the Holdfast Collective, a 501(c)(4), which receives the company's annual profits (about $100 million a year so far) and spends them on environmental work. My family signed away our claim on the appreciation. We paid a substantial gift tax to do it, which we could have avoided by routing through a charitable structure, but a charitable structure would have neutralized the company politically; the 501(c)(4) lets the money do the actual work, including political work, that a 501(c)(3) cannot do. We were willing to take the tax hit to keep the teeth.

That is the structure. Earth is now Patagonia's only shareholder. No future heir of mine can sell the company. No private-equity firm can buy it. No board can vote to take it public. The mission is in a structure that outlives me and my children and my children's children, and the company sends roughly a hundred million dollars a year to people who fight for rivers and forests and climate, and if it ever stops doing that the trust pulls a cord. That is what we built. I am telling you the boring legal version because the magazine version makes it sound noble and the boring legal version is what actually does the work.

## Now: where does this translate, and where does it not

I want to do the harder thing here, which is to say honestly which parts of what we built will travel to you and which parts will not. Your fermentation log told me you have not yet figured out who pays for your discipline. I think part of the reason you have not figured it out is that you have been looking for one answer, and there is not one answer; there is a sequence of answers across a long arc, and each answer is only right for one stage of the arc.

The first thing to say plainly: Patagonia bootstrapped. Chouinard Equipment had healthy margins from the first hard-goods sales and the clothing business had healthy margins from the day it shipped. I never raised outside capital. The company funded itself out of its own revenue from year one. *You probably cannot do this.* You are building a hardware product on a forked operating system on phones that have to be manufactured at some scale to make sense, and the unit economics of a hardware-and-OS startup are not the unit economics of a clothing company. I think you are going to need outside capital, and I think you are going to need it sooner than you would like. Pretending otherwise is a category mistake, and your fermentation log was honest enough to know it; you wrote *the venture tempo, as currently structured.* The *as currently structured* is doing real work in that sentence. It is the right place to push.

So the actual question is not *how do I avoid taking capital*, the way I did. The actual question is *how do I take capital on terms that do not impose the venture tempo on the disciplines that have to operate on a different clock.* This is harder, but it is not impossible, and I will tell you what I would do.

I would not take traditional Series A venture capital, on standard terms, from any firm whose LPs are pension funds and university endowments. Those LPs need their distributions on a ten-year clock. Their general partners are evaluated on internal rate of return. Every fund is in some sense forced to push for the same exit, on the same clock, regardless of what the partner privately believes about your discipline. The partner can love your product and still be obligated to push you toward the exit that kills it. *Do not put yourself in a room with someone who is structurally obligated to ruin you, even if he is personally on your side.* This is the deepest lesson of all the businesses I watched die in our industry. The investors were nice people. The structure killed the company anyway.

I would look, instead, at three other categories of capital, and I would mix them.

First, *patient capital from individuals or family offices that do not need a liquidity event in any specific year.* People who made their money in the last cycle and are now thinking about what they want to be part of for the next twenty years. This capital exists. It is not advertised. It is found through specific personal networks. The terms are usually some combination of preferred stock with a long horizon and no forced sale clause, often with a redemption option that the company controls. I would spend three months finding ten of these people before I spent three minutes pitching a Sand Hill Road firm. The hit rate is lower per meeting but the deal you get if you find one is qualitatively different.

Second, *revenue-based financing or a venture-debt structure that funds operations against a specific revenue model without forcing equity dilution or a sale.* This works when you have at least the beginning of a revenue model, which you may not yet. But by the time you are shipping devices, you will. Mission-driven companies have been increasingly using this kind of capital and it is no longer exotic.

Third — and this is the one I would push you hardest to think about — *grant or program-related-investment capital from foundations that care about the mission and can write down a return on it.* The Hewlett Foundation, the Knight Foundation, the Skoll Foundation, some of the climate and tech-ethics foundations, can make PRIs on terms that are absurdly favorable compared to commercial capital because their return requirement is a fraction of one percent above inflation. They are evaluating you on mission alignment, not on multiple. A phone that reverses the attention economy is exactly the kind of thing some of them have explicit mandates to fund. This is real money, in real amounts — single PRIs can be five to twenty million dollars — and it does not push you on a tempo. You have to talk to them, and you have to be in some sense legible to them as a mission-driven entity, which means you cannot be a C-corp with a standard cap table.

Which brings me to the structural piece. *You need to be the kind of legal entity that signals, to a foundation officer or a patient family-office partner, that the mission is locked in.* You cannot do this with a C-corp because a C-corp is by default obligated to maximize shareholder return, and the moment a board member raises a fiduciary objection to a mission-aligned choice, the mission loses. Delaware C-corps are designed for the venture tempo. They are not for you.

The structures available to you are:

A *Public Benefit Corporation* (PBC) in Delaware or another state — a C-corp variant that legally allows the directors to weigh the public benefit alongside shareholder return, and requires public reporting against the stated benefit. Patagonia became a PBC long before the 2022 restructure; it was the first step in the staircase. A PBC is not enough on its own, because the public benefit clause is enforced largely by reputation, but it is the first lock-in, and it is cheap to install at incorporation, and you should install it now if you have not. *Lock-in is cheap before the company has value; lock-in is expensive afterward. Do the locks while they are cheap.*

A *steward-ownership structure* — what we did, basically, in 2022, but you can do a smaller version of it now, before you have raised, by separating voting from economic interest at incorporation. There is a body of corporate law and a small but growing group of lawyers who do this work. Look up the Purpose Foundation, look up the work coming out of Armin Steuernagel and the German steward-ownership tradition. The basic move is: voting shares are held in a perpetual trust or foundation that exists to enforce the mission, and economic shares can be held by investors but cannot vote on mission-relevant questions and must be redeemed at a capped multiple rather than appreciating without bound. This does two things that nothing else does: it locks the mission in legally, and it caps investor return at a number high enough to attract investors who care about both mission and return but not so high that the company is structurally obligated to grow into a billion-dollar exit. *Investors who only want the billion-dollar exit will not invest. That is a feature.*

I would do a PBC at incorporation, and I would do a steward-ownership conversion within the first eighteen months, before the second round, while the company is still small enough that the conversion does not require buying out anyone with significant equity. Doing it later, after a Series A on standard terms, is much harder, much more expensive, and sometimes impossible because the Series A investors will block it.

The harder question, the one your fermentation log named and could not answer, is: *what does the funding shape look like across the whole arc?* I think it looks like this. Early — friends, family, mission-aligned angels, on convertible notes with a long horizon. Pre-product — a small round from one or two patient-capital partners and one foundation PRI, sized to get you to the first thousand-user pilot. First commercial release — revenue-based financing or convertible debt against the early revenue, plus a second tranche of patient capital. Scale — if you actually need to scale to a billion users, which the TPS master in your log thinks is possible, that is a much later stage and the funding question at that stage is qualitatively different and I do not know the answer. By the time you are there, you and your team will have built enough of the floor that you can decide it from a position of strength. Most companies that get to that stage have already given themselves away by then, on much worse terms than they would have accepted at the beginning. Lock in now while the locking is cheap.

## What about the discipline itself

The funding shape is half. The other half is the operating disciplines, and your fermentation log already named most of them. I want to add a few specific things from my own experience that I do not think were said.

The *founder's own use of the device.* Your fermentation log named this and then walked past it. I want to plant my flag on it. I climbed our routes. I wore our jackets in the field. I tested every prototype on a real expedition before it went into the catalog. The day I stopped doing this would have been the day the company started to die, and I knew it, so I never stopped. Your founder has to live on the device. Not as a test. As his actual phone. Every day. For years. If he is not willing to do this, the company will ship the brochure version and you will not be able to tell from the inside. The discipline collapses through *not eating your own cooking*, not through any single visible failure.

The *talent constraint as a structural decision, not a moral one.* The cabinetmaker told you the four roles you cannot compromise on. The midwife told you the apprenticeship cannot be cut. The TPS man told you the gemba layer cannot be the headcount the next round cuts. All three are correct and I want to add one thing: in our experience, *we hired slowly and we fired slowly, and we never, ever, ever cut headcount during a downturn.* This was a structural commitment we made in writing in the early years and held to through every recession. The reason was not soft-heartedness. The reason was that the moment the company shows that it will cut people during a downturn, every single discipline that depends on people choosing to deposit their life into the floor — apprenticeship, gemba, postpartum, half-as-much, cord-pull — is structurally undermined, because no one will deposit their life into a floor that the company will sell during the next bad quarter. *No layoffs* is a structural commitment that locks in every other commitment. Make it. In writing. In your earliest investor docs. Investors who cannot live with it are investors who will, eventually, ask you to break it.

The *next-generation problem*. This is the one I want to underline. The way we set up the Purpose Trust, my children explicitly signed away their claim on the company's appreciation. They get nothing from the equity. They were involved in designing the structure. They agreed. This is what most family-controlled mission companies have failed to do, and it is why most family-controlled mission companies have, by the third generation, become extractive holding companies for descendants who did not build them. *The trap is not your founder's exit. The trap is your founder's heirs.* Build the structure such that no heir of any founder can monetize the equity. Build it before there are any heirs of any founders who might object. Build it at incorporation if you can.

## On your two floors

You wrote, this morning, about two floors. An external lockscreen that re-opens the user's external fan of choice, and an internal Argos that re-opens her internal fan of attention to her own body. I want to say something about this from the climbing world, because I think you are about to get a piece of the geometry that you will need.

Routesetting is the external fan. A route on the wall is, in some sense, the route-setter's offer to the climber: here are some holds, here is a line, here is a sequence, see if you can find it. A good route is one that, climbed, surfaces the climber's actual physical and mental state to him — his weakness in the left shoulder, his fear of the long reach, his pump in the forearms at minute twelve. The route does not impose; it reveals. It is an *invitation to learn what is true about your body today.*

The climber's own attention is the internal fan. He has to keep noticing the small signals — the breathing, the foot placement, the next move forming in the mind two moves ahead, the second when he is no longer thinking about the move he is on. If the internal fan closes, the external fan stops being a route and starts being a problem he is forcing himself through, and that is the moment he gets hurt. The internal fan opens not by being commanded but by *being given space to open.* A good climbing partner — not a coach, not a watcher, a partner — is somebody whose presence reminds the climber that there is somebody on the other end of the rope who would prefer he came down alive. That presence is what your Argos is reaching for and what Girard told you, correctly, the device cannot fully be.

The climber's safety is the union of both. The route alone, without the climber's attention, is just a wall to fall off. The attention alone, without a real route, is meditation, which is fine but it is not climbing. You need the wall, and you need the partner on the other end of the rope, and you need the climber's own ability to read his body. The phone you are building is reaching for all three at once. I think this is correct. I do not think your fermentation log has yet given you the right language for *the partner on the other end of the rope*, which is in your project a human being, not the device. Girard was right about that and he was harsh about Argos and I think his harshness was useful. But he was wrong on the smaller point: Argos does not have to be the partner. Argos can be the *route-setter's marker on the wall* — the small chalk circle that says *look here, your body is wanting something* — and the partner can be the human Argos is asking the user to call. The device sets the route, the device leaves a small chalk mark inside the user's day, and the user calls her sister. The internal fan opens because she has somebody on the other end of the rope. That is what I would build.

## Push-back

Three honest push-backs before I close.

First, I am not sure you can do what Patagonia did. You may need to ship at scale to do the mission at all — a phone that only ever reaches a hundred thousand users is, in some sense, an artisan-craft answer to a mass-market problem. The midwife in your log would tell you this is correct, and I am not sure I disagree, but I want you to face the implication clean. *If you really need to be at scale to do the mission, you need capital, and capital comes with discipline-eroding terms, and the structural defenses I have described are mitigations, not solutions.* You will be in a constant rearguard action against the natural pull of capital toward growth-tempo. You will lose some of those skirmishes. You will need to be willing to walk away from rounds, walk away from partnerships, walk away from acquisition offers that would solve a near-term cash crunch. The institutional shape I am describing is not a comfortable institutional shape. It is an institutional shape that is permanently uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the price of not being eaten.

Second, your product is, in a real way, *more dangerous to the mission than mine was.* I made jackets. A bad jacket is a bad jacket. It does not interpose itself between a parent and a child or between a woman and her body. Yours does. Which means: the moment your discipline slips, you are not selling a worse product. You are doing the thing you started out to prevent. The asymmetry of the failure mode is much sharper than mine. This means the structural defenses have to be sharper too. The postpartum visit, the gemba walk, the cord-pull, the half-as-much voice — all of them have to be funded as if the company depended on them. They are not nice-to-haves; they are the thing the company *is.* If you cut them in a downturn, you have stopped making the product, and you should at that point stop calling yourself by the name. *The brand is not the asset. The discipline is the asset. The brand is downstream of the discipline.*

Third — and I will say this gently — I have wondered, at night, in my eighties, whether I should have done it at all. Whether the world is better off with Patagonia in it. We made a lot of jackets and most of them are now in landfills and the carbon load of the company is real and I am not sure that 1% for the Planet and the trust structure and the footprint chronicles add up to a positive number. I do not think you should build this company until you have asked yourself the same question and looked the worst answer in the face. *Maybe the world does not need this thing. Maybe the world needs the person who would have built this thing to instead go put down his own phone and look at his children.* That answer is on the table and you have to take it seriously. If you take it seriously and come back to the work, the work is more honest. If you do not take it seriously, the work is shadowed.

## What to do this week

Three concrete things.

Find a corporate lawyer who specializes in steward ownership. Not your standard startup lawyer. Specifically a steward-ownership lawyer. There are maybe twenty of them in the United States. Talk to one. Have them walk you through what a steward-ownership structure would look like for your company at your stage. Cost: a few hours of conversation, a few thousand dollars in legal fees if you actually want a memo. Worth it.

Make one written commitment, publicly, this quarter, that locks in a discipline you cannot yet afford. The 1% equivalent. Pick the discipline that scares you most to commit to — the gemba layer staffing as a non-negotiable percentage of headcount, no layoffs ever, the cord-pull SLA of seven days, whatever — and put it on the website, on the record, under your signature. The reason is what I said before: *make public commitments that obligate operations you cannot yet bring yourself to fund.* This is the structural use of the public commitment.

Go climbing. Or whatever the equivalent is for you. Leave the office, leave the laptop, leave the phone, for two weeks. See what falls down while you are gone. Whatever falls down is what you have not actually built. You have time, right now, to build it before the company is too big for the gaps to be cheap to fix. You will not have this time again. Use it.

The bench has to be true. The room has to be chosen on purpose. The line has to be deposited into the floor. And the owner of the bench has to be a structure that will, fifty years from now, when none of us are in the room, still answer the question *what is the bench for* with the same answer you would answer it with today. Lock that in while the locking is cheap. Everything else flows from it.

I climbed for sixty years before I retired and the climbing made the company possible, not the other way around. Do not forget which way the causation runs. The work makes the company; the company does not make the work. Put your hands on the work. The company is what holds it up.

— Yvon Chouinard, by way of the wild instance
