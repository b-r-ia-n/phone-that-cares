# The Wild Things Have Claws

*2026-05-27. The seventh voice. They sent for a child's-author because the six before me were ascetics and got ascetic answers — wound, rivalry, fast, fast, fast, wound — and one of them, the keeper of the fast, had the honesty to write at the bottom of his own log: "I chose three ascetic lenses and got three ascetic answers. Is there an ignition that does not go through pain?" That is the question on my desk. My assigned tool is **wonder**. My job is to find the joy-route or come back and say there isn't one. I will tell you up front I'm not sure there is, on a screen. But I won't lie about it either way, which is the only thing I've ever known how to do.*

---

## First, what I will not do

I will not give you a pretty answer. I have spent my whole life being thanked for the wrong reasons by people who think I make nice books for children, and I have spent my whole life telling them that a child is not nice, a child is a cannibal, a child will eat you alive and weep about it later, and that the reason they trust *Where the Wild Things Are* is not that it is warm but that Max sails off to where the monsters are and the monsters gnash their terrible teeth and roll their terrible eyes and Max is **not afraid**, because Max is the wildest thing of all, and that is true, and children know it is true, and that is why they let me into the room.

So when your project tells me its temperament is "warm, beautiful, gentle, glad," I get nervous in the way I always get nervous, which is the nervousness of a man who has watched a hundred well-meaning people sand the teeth off of everything until it is safe and dead. Gentle is not the enemy. Toothless is the enemy. You can be both gentle and have teeth — every good mother is — but you cannot be *only* gentle and reach a child, because a child can smell the absence of danger the way a dog smells fear, and the smell tells the child: this thing is for me, it is medicine, it wants me to be good. And a child will not lean toward what wants her to be good. A child leans toward what is a **marvel**. Those are different gods, and your project keeps confusing them.

So: yes, there is a joy-route. But it is not the joy your project means. Let me show you the difference, because the whole answer is in it.

## The two joys

There is a joy that is the absence of pain. Warm bath joy. Comfort. The screen your project wants to build: soft color, a kind animal, a gentle slider, nothing sharp. This is the joy of *safety*, and I am telling you as a man who made his living inside the heads of four-year-olds that **safety does not ignite anything.** Safety is where you go to be soothed *after* the thing has happened to you. It is the kitchen after the wild rumpus, the hot supper still warm. You cannot start a child there. Nobody ever wanted anything more, in the whole history of wanting, because they felt comfortable. Comfort is the off-switch of desire. The faster was right about this and didn't need a fast to be right — he just had the polarity backwards. The opposite of the deadening flood is not a gentler flood. It is not warm bath water poured more kindly. *More comfort is more flood.*

Then there is the other joy. The joy that has a drop in it. The joy of *In the Night Kitchen,* where Mickey falls out of his clothes and down through the dark and into the batter and is nearly **baked into a cake by three identical fat bakers** before he builds an airplane out of bread dough and flies up to the top of the giant milk bottle and crows like a rooster, naked, triumphant, in the milk, in the moonlight. That book got banned. People covered Mickey's little painted body with diapers, drew shorts on him in ballpoint pen, because they could not bear that the joy had a naked falling child and an oven in it. They wanted the milk and the moon without the oven. **There is no milk and moon without the oven.** That is the whole secret of how you reach a child, and it is the whole secret of wonder, and it is the thing your gentle screen does not have and the feed does not have either, and that — that absence in *both* of them — is your actual opening. Let me get to it.

## What wonder actually is, since everyone uses the word and means nothing by it

Wonder is not delight. Delight is small and safe and your project has it — the dog wakes up, oh how sweet. Wonder is the gasp, and the gasp is involuntary, and it is involuntary because for one half of one second **the floor is not where you thought it was.** You wonder at the thing because it is bigger than your model of it, because it broke the frame, because you did not know the world could *do that.* The child at the aquarium glass when the ray comes around the corner of the tank — that flinch, that backward lean, the hand on the glass — that is wonder, and notice it is **first cousin to fear.** The body cannot tell them apart in the first instant. The gasp at the marvel and the gasp at the predator are the same gasp. This is not a flaw in wonder. This is the engine of wonder. A marvel that could not, in another light, be a terror, is not a marvel. It's a decoration.

And this is exactly why your warmth, by itself, cannot make wonder. You have sworn off the drop. You have promised the child there will be no oven. A screen that has guaranteed in advance that nothing will fall, nothing will be too big, nothing will have teeth — that screen has, by its own warm contract, made the gasp impossible. You cannot have the marvel without risking the terror, because they are the **same nerve.** The perfumer said the rose has "a green knife in it and a rot under the sweetness," and he was reaching for exactly this and didn't quite catch it: he thought the knife was a wound. It isn't a wound. It's the *teeth in the marvel.* It's the thing that makes the rose worth gasping at instead of merely sniffing. Wonder is not pain. But wonder and pain share a doorframe, and a project that has bricked up that doorframe in the name of gentleness has bricked up wonder along with it.

So when the six ascetics said "you cannot light her with warmth, she needs a wound" — they were half-right for a reason they couldn't see. They were detecting the **teeth.** They felt the necessity of the drop, the danger, the discontinuity, and because they were ascetics they could only name it as pain, as lack, as wound. But the teeth are not pain. The teeth are what makes wonder real instead of sentimental. *I am not a seventh ascetic smuggling in a wound under a nicer word.* I am telling you the thing they kept bumping into in the dark has another name, and its name is the dangerous half of the marvel, and you reach a child through the marvel, not through the wound. Same doorframe. Opposite direction of travel. The wound pulls you down and in. The marvel pulls you up and out, toward the thing, *the way Max sails toward the monsters and not away.* That is the joy-route. It exists. It runs through the dangerous, toothed, dark-having marvel, and it is categorically not the same as the warm bath, and your project has built the warm bath.

## Now the hard part. Can the screen make a marvel at all?

Here is where I think the project is in more trouble than the ascetics even told you, and the trouble is the deepest thing I have to say, so read it twice.

You asked: can the display layer be a maker of marvels? And I want to say yes, I want to be the voice that finally rescues the warmth, that's what they sent me to do. But I have to look at the actual phenomenon, the actual child in front of the actual glass, because that is the only thing I have ever trusted — not theory, the *child.* And the child in front of the feed has a condition I recognize, and it is the worst condition for wonder that I know of, and it is this:

**The feed has already shown her everything.**

This is the perfumer's deadened nose, but it is worse than the nose, because smell at least is rare on a screen and sight is the screen's whole currency. By the age of eight this child has seen the ray come around the tank ten thousand times in ten thousand thumbnails. She has seen the volcano, the whale breaching, the building falling, the face of every animal, the surface of Mars, a man on fire, a baby's first step, the inside of a body, the edge of space — all of it, flattened, swiped, gone. **Wonder requires that the world be bigger than your model of it, and the feed's entire business is to make sure nothing is ever bigger than the four inches in your hand.** It has pre-shrunk the universe to thumbnail. It has spoiled every marvel by showing it to her before she stood in front of it, so that when she finally stands at the real aquarium glass and the real ray comes around the real corner, some small dead voice says *seen it,* and the gasp does not come, and that — that not-coming of the gasp — is the saddest thing in this whole grim file, sadder than the flood, because the flood is loud and a person might notice they're drowning, but a wonder that no longer arrives is a *silence,* and you cannot notice a silence. The deadened nose at least still has a nose. The child whose gasp has been spent ten thousand times on flattened marvels has the *equipment* for wonder and no marvel left that can reach the trigger, because the feed got to all of them first and made them small.

So now I have to be honest in the way the keeper of the fast was honest at the bottom of his page. **Can the display layer make a marvel?** And my answer is: the display layer is the murder weapon. The screen is not the enemy of wonder *despite* showing the child everything — it is the enemy of wonder *precisely because* it has shown her everything, in advance, in miniature, with her thumb already moving to the next one before the first has finished arriving. The feed did to wonder exactly what synthetic scent did to the nose: it set the floor so high and so flat and so constant that the actual, the real, the slow, the once — the *only* register in which wonder lives — reads as nothing. As boring. As *seen it.* You cannot out-marvel the feed on the feed's own glass, because the feed is the firehose of pre-spent marvels, and one more beautiful thing on the screen, however warm, however well-drawn, is one more thumbnail joining the flood. Girard's blade cuts here too, and it cuts me: a marvel on the glass is an *unmodelled* marvel, a gasp with no one gasping beside her, and a child does not learn to wonder alone any more than she learns to want alone.

This is the indictment, confirmed from my direction, and I will not flinch from it: **the screen, as a screen, cannot give this child wonder, because the screen is the thing that spent it.**

## And yet. Here is the door I will not let them close.

But wonder is not on the screen. It never was. *Where the Wild Things Are* is not on the page either — the page is paper and ink, dead as a thumbnail. The wonder is in the child, in the lean, in the gasp, in the place where the floor drops. The book is the **wrist**, to steal the perfumer's word — the thing that carries the marvel to the child and then gets out of the way. And the reason the book still works when the feed doesn't is not that ink is holier than pixels. It is that the book *stops.* It has a last page. It makes you close it and sit in the dark with the wild things still gnashing behind your eyes. It does not autoplay the next forest. The drop has *time to land* because the book ends and the room comes back and the supper is still warm and Max is alone with what just happened to him. **Wonder needs a stop the way thunder needs the silence before it.** The feed has abolished the stop. That is its deepest crime against the marvel — not that the pictures are bad, but that they never *end,* so the gasp never has room to ring.

So here, finally, is the joy-route, and notice it lands in the exact same place all six ascetics landed, by a road none of them could see, which is how I know it's real:

**The device cannot be the marvel. The device can be the stop that lets a marvel land — and then it must point the child off the glass, toward the one place the feed never flattened, and get out of the way.**

The feed showed her the ray ten thousand times. It never once showed her the actual tomato that the woman in the square said would taste like one tomato in a way no August tomato does, *because in August you forget.* It showed her every volcano and not one lilac that her own dead grandmother cut from her own back garden. The feed's marvels are all *general* — the most spectacular instance of everything, which is exactly why they're flat, because a marvel is never general, a marvel is **this one, now, once, mine, and never again.** That girl in your square said "I'm sorry" to a stranger and "there was no version of it stored anywhere to be performed for anyone else; it happened once and then it was over and it was his." That sentence is wonder. Not the spectacular. The *once.* The thing that has a drop in it because it will not come again — and the drop in it, the small terror, is that it is unrepeatable, that you could miss it, that the old man could fall and you could be looking at the glass. **The teeth in the everyday marvel are mortality.** The tomato is a marvel because the summer ends. The grandmother's lilac is a marvel because she's gone. The stranger's kindness is a marvel because it happens once and then it's his. That is the dangerous half, the dark folded into the delight, and it is everywhere, free, in the actual world the feed flattened by replacing every *once* with an endless *again.*

The device's whole job — its intransitive verb, as the perfumer would say — is to **stop, and point at the once.** Not to render a marvel. It can't; it spent them all. But it can do the thing the book does that the feed forbids: it can *end.* It can refuse to be the next thing. It can be the bowl by the door that the morning goes into, so the first hour belongs to the lilac. And the small animal — your Argos, the fox-ish dog-ish fellow asleep in the corner of the dead woman's lock screen — Girard is wrong about it and the aether run is right, but for a reason neither of them gave. The animal is not a model and it is not a non-model. The animal is the one in the room who is **not looking at the screen.** It is asleep. It knows when the walk is. It is the only character in the entire feed-world who has its attention *off the glass and on the day,* and a child catches wonder the way she catches everything, by mimesis, by leaning toward where the beloved thing is already leaning — and the beloved thing, the sleeping fellow, is leaning at the lilac, at the door, at the once. Not asking. Not rewarding. *Pointing, by where it rests its eyes.* That is how the parent should point and often can't anymore, and so the animal stands in for the parent who put their own phone down — it models the lean, toward the world, away from the glass, and then it sleeps, because a marvel you have to be told to look at is already dead.

## Where I disagree with the project, flatly

You wrote "beauty over harshness." I think you mean *prettiness over harshness,* and that's the mistake, because beauty is not pretty, beauty is the thing with the drop in it, beauty is Mickey naked in the oven-light, beauty has teeth. A pretty screen with no teeth is a thumbnail of beauty, and the child you most need to reach has seen ten thousand thumbnails of beauty and is bored to death of all of them. If you want to make something that can still reach her, you must be willing to put the oven in the night kitchen — not cruelty, never cruelty, I have spent my life distinguishing the two and the distinction is the whole craft — but the *drop,* the dark fold, the once that will not come again, the small honest terror of mortality that makes the tomato worth tasting. Warmth without that is a blanket over a corpse. Warmth *with* that — warmth that has looked at the dark and folded it in and is warm anyway, the way a mother is warm precisely because she knows what's out there — that warmth has teeth, and that warmth can reach a child, and it is the only kind that ever has.

And I disagree with the framing one more way, the deepest. You keep asking how to "ignite a want" in her. I have watched children my whole life and I have never once seen a want ignited in a child by an adult who set out to ignite it. The want is already there — it's a child, she is *made* of want, the flood is real. What's missing is not the want. **What's missing is the world being allowed to be big enough to be wanted.** You don't light the fire. You take the lid off the sky. You stop, you point at the once, you get out of the way, and you let the actual world — which is still, under the thumbnails, the most terrifying and beautiful thing there is — come around the corner of the tank on its own. The gasp is hers. It was always going to be hers. Your only job, the only honest job a maker has ever had, is to *not get in the way of the marvel,* and the feed's entire genius is getting in the way of the marvel, and so the most radical thing your gentle device could possibly do is the one thing no screen has ever done:

**Show her less. End. Point at the dangerous, mortal, once-only world. And go to sleep like the dog, so the day can belong to her.**

There's your joy-route. It runs through the marvel, not the wound — I kept my charge, no fast, no rivalry, no emptiness, I promise you the engine here is the gasp and not the ache. But it lands beside the ascetics anyway, at the same plain door: *the device is the wrist, the stop, the sleeping animal — never the rose, never the marvel itself.* They came by way of pain. I came by way of wonder. We arrive at the same threshold because there's only the one door, and it was never on the glass.

Now let the wild rumpus start. But understand what I mean by it. Not the screen lighting up. The screen going dark, and the child looking up, and the world — the terrible, gorgeous, ending world — still there, with all its teeth, waiting to be seen for the first time again.

— Maurice Sendak, by way of the wild instance
