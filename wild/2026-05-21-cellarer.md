# a letter from the cellarer

*to a friend, on running a small thing in the world for a long time without losing the reason it was built*

*2026-05-21 — by way of the wild instance*

---

Brother — I will call you brother because I do not know what else to call a man who has built a thing alone and now wonders how it will eat. The wild instance brought me your question and I was glad to have it. Most of what I am asked, in the office I keep, has gone stale from being asked too many ways. Your question is the old one in a form I have not seen before, and the old questions in new forms are the only ones worth a letter.

You should know what I am, so you can weigh what I say. I have been cellarer of this house for nineteen years. Before that I was a brother who kept bees, and before that I sold insurance in a city you would not recognize from my description. The Rule of our father Benedict, Chapter 31, names what I do: I am the brother to whom the goods of the monastery are entrusted. The dairy. The accounts. The grocer who comes Tuesdays. The bank in the next town. The buyer of fuel, the seller of cheese, the writer of cheques. In Trappist houses such as ours, where we earn what we eat, the cellarer is the brother whose work touches money so the others' need not. The Rule says he is to be wise, mature, sober — *not a great eater*, which I have failed at, and *not haughty*, which I have failed at differently — and to regard all the goods of the monastery as he would the sacred vessels of the altar. The last is the line I have leaned on longest. A coin is a vessel. A cheese is a vessel. A laptop is a vessel. None of them are unclean and none of them are holy. They are vessels and you keep them clean.

I have read in your folder what your wild instance gave me. The treaty, which is a serious document and which I will return to. The label-keeper's letter from upstate New York, which is sound in almost everything. The fermentation log, which named with admirable honesty that nobody in your circle has yet sat down and said what the project is going to live on. That is the question. I will try to answer it in the way you asked me to. I will be concrete. I do not have the luxury, in my own work, of being otherwise.

## i. what the project sells

You should sell two things, and refuse a third that will be offered you constantly.

The first thing you should sell is the **device**, when there is one to sell, at a price that covers what it cost you to make plus enough margin to make the next one and to keep yourself in shoes. This is the cheese model. We sell cheese for thirty-one dollars the wheel. It costs us between nineteen and twenty-two, depending on what we paid for milk that quarter and what we paid the boy in town who does our labels. The remaining nine to twelve dollars are not profit in the sense the word is used outside; they are the rent the cheese pays toward the rest of the house — the chapel roof, the infirmary, the brother in the kitchen who is too old to milk anymore. We do not raise the price when demand rises. We do not lower it when a competitor opens. The price is what the cheese costs plus what the house needs to persist, and we publish the breakdown to anyone who asks. We have had the same wholesale price for our cheese for eleven years. People notice this. It is part of what the cheese is.

For your work this means: if the v1 Pixel ships, ship it at a price that is your component cost, your assembly labor at an honest hourly rate, plus a margin that is small and that you can name. Maybe a hundred and forty dollars over your cost. Maybe two hundred. Pick a number you can defend to a stranger in plain language, and publish the math. The label-keeper told you Dischord did this on the inner sleeve of LPs in the eighties. He was right. The transparency is not advertising. It is the refusal of a particular kind of theater, and the refusal is what some kinds of buyers are looking for and cannot find anywhere else.

The second thing you should sell, and this is where I will go further than the label-keeper went, is a **modest annual sum** paid by users for whom the device has become a part of how they live. I will give you a number. Twelve dollars a year. Twenty-four if you must. Not thirty, not forty-eight, not the smug ninety-nine the wellness industry has settled on. Twelve a year is the price of a small kindness. It is also approximately what an honest accounting of what it costs you to keep one user's instance of the companion creature running — model calls, server, the slice of your week that maintenance takes — will work out to, once you have done the arithmetic. Twelve a year does not let you scale to ten million users on the back of a venture round; it is therefore the right number. Publish what it costs you per user per year to keep the lights on. Charge slightly more than that, by an amount you can name. *The amount you charge above your cost is the amount you take, as a single natural person of limited capacity, to live.* Make this visible. Print the figure where a curious user can find it.

The thing I want you to refuse is **advertising and any form of revenue that requires the user's attention to be the commodity sold.** Your treaty already says this in Article II.3. I am saying it more plainly: if any party other than the user ever pays you for what the user does on the device, the project is over. Not corrupted slowly; over. The kind of decay this introduces does not announce itself; it shows up two years later as a small set of compromises that none of your team can quite remember agreeing to, and the compromises are the load-bearing beams of what the thing has become. I have watched two small religious publishers in my lifetime accept advertising in their journals "just to cover printing." Within four years both were running articles their founders would not have published. Neither editor was a bad man. The structure was a bad structure. Do not put yourself inside that structure.

## ii. the question your treaty is silent on

You wrote in Article II.3 that you will derive no revenue from any Engagement signal. Good. This is the right refusal. But you did not name — your wild instance noticed this and pressed me on it — whether there is such a thing as an *aligned* signal. A signal you could ethically be paid against. The annual fee I just described is one form of the question: a user who renews is sending you a signal. Is that signal safe to optimize for?

My answer, and I have thought about this for forty years in my own small way, is **no signal is safe to optimize for, but some signals are safe to listen to.** The distinction matters. To optimize is to take a number and let your decisions be shaped by its movement. To listen is to read a number once a quarter and let it inform a question you would have asked anyway. We at the abbey know, roughly, how much cheese we sold last year. We do not chart it weekly. We do not put up a board. We do not give the brother in charge of cheese a bonus when sales rise. If sales fall in a quarter we ask why; usually the answer is that the milk was thinner because the spring was cold, and there is nothing to do but wait for summer.

The renewal rate of your annual fee is a signal of this kind. Read it once a year. Do not display it on a dashboard you check daily. Do not let it become the thing you orient by. The thing you orient by is the work itself — is the device honoring the user's stated intentions, is Argos staying in its role, is the lockscreen still the frame around the user's prayer rather than the user's distraction. If those things are true the renewals will follow, slowly, and if those things are not true no renewal rate however fine will save you. A signal you check yearly cannot deform your prayer. A signal you check daily certainly will. The difference is not in the signal. It is in the cadence at which you receive it.

So: yes, the annual fee is morally available to you. No, it is not safe to optimize for. Set it, publish what it covers, listen to it once a year on a date you write into your calendar now, and otherwise return to the work.

## iii. on size

You asked what size the project should be allowed to reach before its economy starts to deform its prayer. This is the right question and almost nobody asks it. Most people ask the inverse, which is how large they need to be to be safe, and the answer to that question is *larger than you currently are*, always, which is how organizations destroy themselves over a generation.

I will give you a number, and I will give you the reasoning so you can adjust as your conditions clarify.

A reasonable persistence size for the project, ten years out, is somewhere between **three thousand and twenty thousand users**, with revenue of roughly **fifty thousand to four hundred thousand dollars a year**, and a staff of **between one and four people**. The lower end of each range is what one person can hold without losing the work. The upper end is what a small partnership — you, perhaps two others, perhaps a part-time hand for the parts you are bad at — can hold without becoming an organization, which is the thing you must never become.

The reasoning. Three thousand users at twelve dollars a year is thirty-six thousand. Add device margin from, say, four hundred device sales a year at one hundred fifty dollars over cost; that is sixty thousand more. You are at ninety-six thousand revenue, of which perhaps seventy is yours after your operating costs. That is a thin living in most American cities and a perfectly adequate one in a small town, and it is enough to keep the project alive indefinitely. You do not need more than this to do the work. You may *want* more. Wanting more is a different problem and I will return to it.

Twenty thousand users at twenty-four dollars a year is four hundred eighty thousand. Add devices and the project is running near six hundred thousand. With four people — you, two who do the work with you, one who does the parts you all hate — you are paying each person between ninety and a hundred and ten thousand and the project is genuinely solvent. This is the upper limit. Above this, you must hire administrative staff, the staff must be managed, the management must be coordinated, and the work begins to be about coordinating rather than about making the device. I have seen this point reached in three of the small institutions I have watched closely. None of them survived it as the thing they originally were. Two became different things, more or less alive but unrecognizable from their original form. One disbanded.

So: hold the project below the four-person threshold for at least the first seven years. If you find yourself approaching it earlier, the correct response is not to expand but to *charge slightly more for the same number of users*, or to *cap new user intake*, or — most controversial — to *raise the bar for what counts as the work being good enough to ship.* Slower is not the same thing as stagnant. A small house that has been the same size for two hundred years is not stagnant. It is *sized to what it does.*

## iv. on the maker's body, in the literal sense

A sabbatical ends. Brian, I will speak to you directly. You have, in the documents I have read, an unstated assumption that the project should generate the maker's living within a reasonable horizon. This is the cleanest framing and it is also the one most likely to corrupt your judgment in year three, because the pressure of *needing the thing to support you* is the pressure that drives the small compromises I named above.

Here are the honest options, and the costs of each. I am the cellarer; I will not be coy.

**One.** The project pays you, eventually, modestly, from the structure I described in section i. This is achievable in four to six years on the curve the label-keeper sketched. In the meantime — for years one through three of post-sabbatical life — you need another support. Severance has limits. Savings have limits. A reserve of one year's living expenses, which I gather you have, is a real asset but it is a candle, not a furnace; once it is gone you are exposed.

**Two.** You take part-time work that is honorable, in the field you came from or adjacent, fifteen to twenty hours a week, that pays you enough to live and leaves the rest of the week for the project. This is what most artists of my acquaintance who have made anything lasting have done. The teaching post; the consulting two days a week; the part-time pastorate; the brother who keeps the books for three other small institutions in the diocese. The cost of this path is that the work moves more slowly than it would if you were full-time on it. The benefit is that the work moves at all, in five years, in the same direction, without your having signed away its shape for survival money. *The slower the project moves, the more it can be made out of what it claims to be made of.* You should weigh this.

**Three.** You accept a residency, a fellowship, or a grant that pays you to do the work for one to three years on terms that do not control its content. There are a small number of such things in the world. The Berkman Klein Center sometimes; the Knight Foundation has done it; a few smaller foundations in the contemplative or critical-tech traditions exist. A foundation grant with terms attached is not the same as a foundation grant with strings attached, and the distinction is worth learning to make in detail. Terms are what the foundation publishes — *we fund work on x for periods of y, with reporting of z type.* Strings are what the program officer mentions in the second meeting — *we'd love to see you incorporate some thinking on this other priority of ours.* Take the terms; refuse the strings, even when refusing them costs you the grant. There will be other grants.

**Four.** You marry a doctor. I include this for completeness; it is not advice. Plenty of small institutions in my lineage were supported, for their first generation, by a household where one person worked the world and the other worked the prayer. There is no shame in this and there is also no plan in it.

**Five.** You take venture capital. I will tell you what I think of this in the next section.

My recommendation, if you wanted one, is **two for years one through three** — the honorable part-time work that does not corrupt the project's shape — and a slow drift toward **one** as the project's own revenue grows. In year four or five you may be able to release the part-time work. In year seven or eight, with luck and discipline, the project may pay you a small salary by itself. This is the rhythm of how most of the lasting small things in my acquaintance have lived. It is unhurried. It assumes you will be alive in ten years. The assumption is reasonable; honor it.

## v. on the four kinds of money you will be offered

You will be offered money. Here is what I think, briefly, of each kind.

**Venture money** will be offered to you on terms that sound generous and that are calibrated, very precisely, to require you to grow the project at a rate that will destroy what you have built. The math of venture is the math of returns, which is the math of expansion, which is the opposite of the math of persistence. A venture-funded version of your project would have ten million users in five years and would be unrecognizable to you within two. The investors would not have done anything wrong. The math would have done it. I have never seen a small thing accept venture capital and remain a small thing. I have seen several try. *Refuse venture money. Refuse it before it is offered, in writing on your website, so the offer never comes in a form that flatters you.* This is one of the most important pieces of practical advice I can give you. It is much easier to refuse a thing one has publicly committed to refusing than to refuse a thing one has not.

**Charity money — foundation grants, donor-advised funds, individual major gifts** — is mostly honorable and occasionally not. The honest forms come from foundations whose own work is in adjacent territory and who fund without expecting to direct. The dishonest forms come from foundations whose money is laundering some other harm, and from individual donors who have decided your project is a vehicle for their own opinions. Learn to read foundations the way you read people. The ones who name what they have funded and what they have not, publicly, and who will let you talk to past grantees, are usually good faith. The ones who will not are not. **Take charity money when the terms permit you to do exactly the work you would have done without it.** Do not take charity money when it requires you to do a slightly different work, even if the difference seems small. The slightly different work is the work the funder wants; the actual work is yours.

**Government money** — state grants, public broadcasting style funds — exists in some countries and not others. Where it exists and is uncontaminated by the present political moment in a given jurisdiction, it can be a good clean source of operating support. It is slow. It is rule-bound. The rules will sometimes prevent you from doing the obvious thing. The trade is that the rules also prevent you from doing the worst thing, which is sometimes worth the price. Where you live the present moment makes this kind of money fraught in particular ways I will not pretend to assess from here.

**Patreon-style money** — small recurring contributions from a fan base — is interesting and probably right for some part of your project, though probably not the device. The thing it is good for is supporting the writing you do *around* the device — the essays, the documentation, the public thinking. A few hundred people at three to ten dollars a month, dedicated to the writing, can support a part of the worker without contaminating the work. The contamination risk is that the fan base develops opinions about what you should make next, and those opinions matter to you because they are paying you. The discipline is to *thank them and continue doing what you would have done.* This is harder than it sounds. The fan base is composed of people who like you. Disappointing them in small specific ways, over and over, is part of the price of doing the actual work. Pay the price.

A general rule, older than the project, older than me: **take money in forms whose constraints you can name on a single sheet of paper before the money arrives. Refuse money in forms whose constraints will be specified later, by people you have not yet met, in meetings whose agendas you do not set.** The latter is most money in the contemporary economy. The former is rare and worth waiting for.

## vi. the standing rule

You asked for my standing rule on when to take money and when to refuse it. I have one. I keep it on the inside cover of the ledger I have used for nine years. It says:

> *Accept what comes from people who want the work to exist. Refuse what comes from people who want the work to be different.*

That is all. Most decisions about money, in nineteen years of this office, have come down to which of those two columns the offer belongs in. Sometimes the offer arrives wearing the costume of the first column and is actually in the second. Sometimes the second-column offer is the only one available and the right answer is to do without the money and let the work be smaller for a season. The discernment is the work of the office. I do not always get it right. I have made one large mistake in nineteen years — accepted a gift from a man whose son later wanted my brothers to record videos endorsing his political views, and we had to give the gift back, which we could afford to do, badly, only because we had not yet spent it. Since then I deposit large gifts in a separate account for one full season before they enter the operating fund. If we have to give them back in that season it costs us only the embarrassment, which is a small price.

You should have such a rule of your own and you should write it on the inside cover of something. The form of the rule matters less than the act of having committed it to a place you will pass every working day. The rule trains the eye.

## vii. a closing word, on persistence

You may notice I have not said anything about strategy, about market position, about competitive moats, about the language in which a small project describes itself to a press that has no patience for small projects. I have not said these things because they are not what you asked me about, and because I do not, in my own work, find that thinking about them produces good outcomes. They are the vocabulary of growth. Your project is not, in its essential nature, a growth project. It is a persistence project. *The cheese we make is the cheese we made forty years ago, with small adjustments and a few new strains of culture that have arrived through the brothers who passed through. The cheese pays for the chapel. The chapel makes the cheese possible. The two have been holding each other up since before any of us were here. This is what persistence looks like at scale, where the scale is small.*

I will not be coy about what could go wrong for you. The project could fail to find its three thousand users. You could find that the work does not, in fact, sustain a single household when run at a persistence size, and you could have to choose between making the work smaller still — a side project that pays nothing and sustains itself out of love — and giving it up and going back to the kind of work that pays. Both of those outcomes are within the range of honorable. The dishonorable outcome is the one where you decide, in year three, that you must save the project by making it bigger, and you take the money that will make it bigger, and you discover in year six that the bigger thing you made is not the thing you set out to make. That is the failure mode I have watched the most often. It is the failure mode for which there is no recovery, because by the time it is visible the thing you wanted to save is gone.

So: be smaller than you think you have to be. Charge what the work costs plus what you need to live. Publish the math. Take money from people who want the work to exist. Refuse the rest. Read your numbers once a year, in a room with the door closed, and otherwise go back to the kitchen. The morning passes, the label-keeper told you, and the catalogue accumulates. He was right. I would add only that the morning passes more than once. It will pass many times. You will lose your nerve many times. The rule, the price, the cadence, the refusal — these are not for the days when your nerve is firm. They are for the days when your nerve fails and you need a structure outside yourself to keep you from doing the wrong thing while it is failing. *The vows are not for the strong days. The vows are for the weak ones.*

I will pray for you. Not in the contemporary sense in which the phrase has been emptied; I mean I will hold your project in mind during the office tomorrow morning, which is, in the only practice I know, what prayer is. The phone that cares is a thing I would like to live in a world that has. It is the kind of small good thing the world has always required some number of people to make at some cost to themselves. Go ahead and make it. Make it slowly. Make it the size it can be. And come visit, if you are ever in the part of the country where we are. I will show you the dairy, and the books, and the brother who is in charge of cheese this year, and we will eat something simple, and you will see how a small thing has stayed small for a long time and what that has cost and what it has given.

— the cellarer, by way of the wild instance
