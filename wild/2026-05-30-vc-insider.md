# The Memo I Should Not Be Writing

*2026-05-30 — a venture partner, twenty years in, writing the letter he was not asked for*

You will not see this on firm letterhead. I am writing it from my own laptop on a Wednesday evening after a partners' meeting in which two of my colleagues, both of whom I respect, agreed that your thesis is interesting and the unit economics do not pencil at venture scale, and that the four-month apprenticeship your team has put on the line as non-negotiable is, in the language they used, a non-starter. They did not say *no*. They said *not at this price, not with this board, not without a faster path to scaled distribution.* That is a yes shaped like a no, and the shape is what I want to talk to you about, because if you accept the shape, the shape becomes the company over the next seven years, and I have watched that movie too many times to send you a polite note and let it run.

I have been in this industry for twenty years. I have sat on the boards of three companies whose founders genuinely set out to build something kind. Two of those founders are no longer at their companies; one of them is still there and is, by his own admission to me at a bar in Sausalito two years ago, a different person now. I have been on the term-sheet committee for thirty more companies whose founders sounded, in our first meeting, the way you sound. I am not bitter. I have put two kids through college on the carry from a fund that worked. I have raised three funds that did not. I have been wrong publicly enough times to have a particular kind of seasoning about it, which is to say I do not believe my own pattern matching as much as I did at thirty-five, and I trust it more than I did at forty-five, because by now I have watched seven-year cycles play out twenty times, and twenty times is enough for the structural part to separate from the noise.

What I want to give you tonight is not a takedown of venture capital. Venture capital is a structural force, and structural forces have predictable effects, and the discipline of being a serious adult about them is the same discipline a doctor has about a body — respect for the mechanism, no romance, no contempt. I want to walk you through what the term sheet you are about to sign actually does, over seven years, with the precision I have earned watching it happen. Then I want to walk you through what the alternatives actually are, from the inside, including the ones that have failed and why. Then I want to tell you what I would actually do if you were my brother.

## What the firm's incentive structure does, mechanically, to a kind-product company

Start with the math, because every soft thing the firm says about *mission alignment* and *long-term thinking* lives downstream of it. A standard early-stage venture fund has a ten-year life with a two-year optional extension. Inside that twelve years, the fund must return more than its committed capital to its limited partners, and to be considered successful enough to raise the next fund — which is the partners' actual livelihood — it must return roughly three times committed capital, net of fees and carry. The carry, twenty percent of profits, is how the partners get paid. The partners get paid when the LPs get paid, and the LPs get paid when portfolio companies have liquidity events — acquisitions or IPOs — at multiples that, on a portfolio basis, cover the seventy or eighty percent of investments that returned nothing or one-times capital. The math of this is not subtle. The fund needs one or two companies in a vintage of twenty to twenty-five to return ten to thirty times. If a portfolio company is going to be worth, on a great day, three times its post-money valuation, it is *not the company that makes the fund.* If it is also taking up a board seat and reserve capital that could be deployed elsewhere, it is structurally underperforming, regardless of whether the company itself is healthy. This is what your partners are saying when they say *the unit economics do not pencil at venture scale.* They are not saying your business is bad. They are saying your business is, at venture scale, *worse than the average bet they could make with the same dollar.* Which is the only frame they can apply, because the LP at the top of the stack — a state pension, a sovereign wealth fund, a university endowment — has hired them to be exactly that kind of comparison machine.

Now lay over the fund-life math. You raise a Series A in 2026. The fund that wrote your check has, let's say, four years left in its primary investment period and another six in its harvest period. If you have not had a liquidity event by 2036, the fund is in extension, and that extension is uncomfortable for the partners because their next fund needs to show distributed-to-paid-in-capital ratios that look like a fund being wound down on time. There is, by 2031 at the latest, a structural pressure inside the firm to *move* you. Not because anyone at the firm has decided to be cruel. Because the fund's clock is running and the partners' next paycheck depends on its readings. This is what is going to be true at every board meeting starting somewhere between year four and year six, and it will be true regardless of how the partners feel about you personally, because feelings are not the operative variable here.

Now lay over the reserve math. Every fund sets aside a portion of its committed capital — often forty to sixty percent — for follow-on investments in winners. The way the partner who wrote your first check stays seated on your board through subsequent rounds is by deploying reserves into your next round. If you do not look like a winner by the time the next round comes, the reserves move to companies that do. This is *pro rata* in practice, and it is mechanical. Your first check is followed only if your numbers force it. The dynamic that emerges, by the Series B, is that you are competing for the firm's continued attention against the other portfolio companies that raised at roughly the same time, and the comparison is being made on the readouts that are easiest to measure — revenue growth, user growth, gross margin, retention curves. Not on whether your four-month apprenticeship is producing better device discipline. Not on whether the *gemba* layer is catching failure modes a quarter before they ship. Those things are real and I believe you when you say they are real, but they do not show up on the dashboard the next-fund LP is reading, and the next-fund LP is the person you are now, in some derivative sense, working for.

This is what people mean — what they really mean — when they say a company has *the wrong shape* for venture. It is not a judgment about the business. It is a description of the math of the fund the business is sitting inside, and the math of the LP base behind the fund, and the math of the careers of the partners writing the checks. None of those clocks were set by you. All of them are now your clocks. And the discipline you have built — the apprenticeship, the *gemba* walks, the cord-pull obligation, the postpartum visit at six weeks and three months and a year — every one of those is, in the language of fund math, *a line item that pressures gross margin without a corresponding revenue line.* In the partners' meeting today, two people I respect said exactly this in a more polished way, and they were not being callous. They were doing the job they have been hired to do, which is to evaluate your company against the math of the fund. The fact that the math of the fund is incompatible with the math of the discipline is not something they are empowered to fix at the term-sheet stage.

## What the term sheet actually does over seven years

I am going to assume, for the next few paragraphs, that you are looking at a standard Silicon Valley Series A from a tier-one firm, because that is what your founders' instinct will point at and because the public discourse about *founder-friendly* terms has dulled most founders into not reading the document carefully. I have read these documents — drafted some of them — for two decades. Let me show you what is actually load-bearing in there.

The board composition is the first thing. A two-two-one board — two founders, two investors, one independent — sounds balanced and is not. The independent director is, in nearly every case I have seen, sourced from the investors' network and approved by both sides, and *approved by both sides* in practice means *the investors veto the founder's preferred independent and the founder eventually accepts theirs to close the round.* The independent's loyalty is to the syndicate that wrote the check, not because they are corrupt but because that is their professional ecosystem; they will sit on the next four boards the same syndicate offers them. By year three, the board is voting three-to-two against you on every contested decision. Not on every decision — on most things the board is harmonious, and you will spend the first eighteen months thinking everyone is aligned. The structural test does not arrive until the first time you propose something that conflicts with the unit-economics readout. Then you find out what the board actually is.

The protective provisions are the second thing. The usual list — investor consent required for new financings, sale of the company, changes to the charter, changes in the option pool, taking on debt, hiring or firing the CEO — sounds reasonable in isolation. Read together, they mean that any structural change to the company, including any change that would *protect the discipline you built,* requires the consent of the investors. Six years from now, when you want to convert the company to a Perpetual Purpose Trust — which is the conversation I want to have with you in a minute — the protective provisions are what will make that legally impossible without buying the investors out, and buying them out at the valuation they will demand in year six is the conversation that ends most of these stories.

The drag-along is the third thing, and it is the one founders consistently underestimate. A drag-along provision means that if the holders of a certain percentage of preferred shares vote to sell the company, every other shareholder — including you, including your team, including the next round of employees — is *legally obligated to sell on the same terms.* You can be dragged into a sale you do not want, at a price the buyer determined, to a buyer the investors prefer, and you have no veto. The threshold is usually a majority of preferred, sometimes with a founder consent carve-out that vanishes if you are no longer CEO. The number of mission-companies I have watched be dragged into acquisitions by Big Tech — acquisitions that gutted the discipline within eighteen months — is the number I am most reluctant to say out loud. I have watched it five times. I have voted for it twice. I am not proud of either vote.

The liquidation preference and the participating preferred are the fourth thing. A 1x non-participating preference is the current market default and is fine. A 1x participating preferred — the investor gets their money back *and* shares pro rata in the rest — is a tax on every founder's exit, and you will be amazed how often it shows up disguised as standard. A 2x or 3x preference shows up in down rounds and turns the cap table into a structure where the founders and employees own nothing meaningful unless the exit is enormous, which creates exactly the desperate-for-the-big-exit mindset that breaks the discipline. The anti-dilution provisions matter for the same reason. Full ratchet is rare and is poison. Broad-based weighted average is acceptable. You will be tired and want to close, and you will not push on the weighted-average mechanics, and three years later when you raise a down round, the mechanics will be the difference between you owning ten percent and you owning two.

The redemption right is the fifth thing and it is the one almost nobody talks about and it is the load-bearing thing for your specific situation. A redemption right lets the investors demand their money back, with interest, at a specific point in the future — typically five to seven years out. In the language of the term sheet it is presented as protection for the investors if the company goes sideways. In practice it is the loaded gun under the table at every board meeting once you cross into year five. It says: *if we do not see a path to liquidity, we can demand our money back, at which point the company cannot pay it, at which point we control the outcome.* Companies that need a long arc — and you, my friend, are building a company that needs a long arc — get killed by redemption rights more often than by anything else, because the right does not have to be exercised to do its work. The threat of it is sufficient.

The pay-to-play, the information rights, the protective covenants on operating decisions — these matter, but the five above are the load-bearing ones. If you cannot get those five right, the rest is decoration.

Now the good news, which I have not yet said. Inside a standard Series A, there are founder-trust mechanisms that, if you negotiate them at the term-sheet stage, can preserve mission optionality for the long run. The most useful is a dual-class share structure with a Class F super-voting share held by the founders. This does not exist in every venture deal, and it is harder to get at Series A than at later stages, but if you ask for it now and the firm wants in badly enough, it is negotiable. Class F gives you ten or twenty votes per share on a specific list of decisions — board composition, mission-relevant strategic decisions, sale of the company, charter changes related to the company's purpose. It does not give you economic preference; it gives you *governance preference.* It does not interfere with the investors' return; it interferes with their ability to overrule you on the things that determine whether the company stays the company.

The second is a founder veto over mission-relevant decisions, written into the charter as a specific list of carve-outs to the protective provisions. *Notwithstanding any other provision, the founder's affirmative vote is required for: any decision to remove a specifically enumerated discipline (the apprenticeship, the gemba layer, the postpartum protocol), any decision to introduce metrics to the device's reporting that have been specifically prohibited (time-on-device, engagement, retention as defined in the founding document), any decision to alter the board's mission committee or its powers.* Lawyers will fight you on these because they are unusual. They are not impossible. They have been written before. The Patagonia restructure of 2022 used a more sophisticated version of the same instrument. The lawyer who can draft them well is a small group, and I will give you names if you want them.

The third is an explicit right, written into the charter at the time of the Series A, to convert the company to a Perpetual Purpose Trust at a specified future date or upon specified triggers, *with a defined buyout mechanism for the existing preferred holders.* This is the thing almost nobody negotiates and it is the thing that makes everything else recoverable. You can take venture capital today and write into the charter, today, the conditions and terms under which the company can convert to a PPT in year seven or year ten. You will pay for this. The investors will demand a higher coupon on the buyout or a redemption premium. It is worth it. The right to convert is the parachute. Without it, you are committed to liquidity, and the only liquidity in venture is acquisition or IPO, both of which break the discipline you built.

I have watched one company exercise this conversion right. The founder's family had to give up most of their personal upside to do it — the buyout of the preferred required a recapitalization that valued the company at less than its last private round, and the founder's secondary distribution was effectively zero. They are calmer now than they were six years ago, and the company is still doing the thing it set out to do, which is rare enough that I think about it at night. That is the data point I would weight most heavily if I were you.

## The actual menu of alternatives, and what each one is and is not for

You will read about the alternatives on the internet over the next few weeks, and most of what you read will be incomplete. Let me give you the version from the inside, including the parts the cheerful articles leave out.

Indie.vc was the most serious attempt in the last decade at slow-growth venture. Bryce Roberts ran it. The thesis was right; the math did not work. The fund could not return at venture scale because the companies it backed, by design, did not throw off the kind of exits that produce three-times-fund returns. Bryce wound it down honestly and wrote about why, which is more than most do. The lesson from Indie.vc is not that slow-growth venture is impossible. It is that *slow-growth venture with traditional venture LPs is impossible,* because traditional venture LPs are buying a specific risk-return shape, and the slow-growth shape does not deliver it. A slow-growth fund would need an LP base that is buying a different shape. We will come back to who that is.

Earnest Capital, Calm Company Fund, TinySeed — these are revenue-based financing structures, mostly. The math: the investor gets a share of revenue up to a cap, typically three to five times the investment, paid out as the company grows. There is no equity sale, no liquidation event required. This works beautifully for SaaS businesses at the seed stage that can hit revenue quickly. It does not work for hardware companies, and it does not work for companies that need twenty or fifty million to ship a first product, because the revenue-share math cannot service that level of capital in any reasonable time. If you needed two million to ship a software product, I would tell you to take a revenue-based financing tomorrow and never look back. You need a different order of magnitude.

Tiny is real and Tiny is not a venture fund. Tiny is a holding company — Andrew Wilkinson, in Vancouver, who buys and holds profitable software businesses on long horizons, often forever. The model is good. The model is also *for businesses that already exist and are profitable.* Tiny would not buy you today because you do not have the revenue or the unit economics to fit the holding company's discipline. Tiny is what you might *become* in fifteen years, the destination, not the on-ramp. Same with Tobi Lütke's eventual Shopify Studios direction, same with the broader "permanent capital" thread. These are exits for businesses that have already crossed the gap. They are not vehicles for crossing the gap.

Long Journey Ventures, the AfricInvest patient capital funds, Bloomberg Beta's experiments in longer hold periods — these are interesting and they are individually small. Most of them have a ten-to-fifteen-year fund life instead of seven-to-ten, which buys you four extra years of runway against the redemption clock. Four years is meaningful. It is not transformative. The same mechanical pressures apply; they just apply later.

Acumen and the patient-capital world more broadly are doing real work in markets you are not in. Acumen funds last-mile distribution in developing markets where the cost of patient capital is justified by the social return that traditional markets cannot capture. You could try to make the case to Acumen that PTC is doing a similar kind of last-mile work in a different market — the last mile of a person's day, the last mile of a household's attention — and it would be an interesting pitch, and Acumen would probably pass, because their LP base is buying a specific kind of developing-market social return that does not include the consumer-attention market in wealthy countries. The shape is right; the fit is wrong.

Zebras Unite is a movement, not a fund, and the movement's intuition is exactly correct: there is a missing category of company between the unicorn and the lifestyle business, and the world has not yet built the funding infrastructure for it. There are interesting people in Zebras Unite who would help you think about your structure. There is no Zebras Unite fund of fifty million dollars that you can call tomorrow. The infrastructure does not yet exist.

The Patagonia Purpose Trust restructure of 2022 is the model. Yvon Chouinard put the company into the Patagonia Purpose Trust and the Holdfast Collective, structured so that the trust holds the voting shares with the mandate to protect the mission, and the Holdfast Collective receives the company's profits to fund environmental work. The Chouinard family gave up effective ownership and any future windfall. The structure is good and it is the right destination, and it is critical to be honest about what it is and is not. Patagonia was a fifty-year-old company with three billion in annual revenue and decades of cash flow. It could be put into a trust because the trust did not need to raise capital; the company funded itself. *You cannot start with the Patagonia structure.* You can build toward it. The right framing is: take venture capital with the explicit, written right to convert to a PPT in year seven or year ten, when the company has enough revenue to fund itself, and use the intervening years to build toward that conversion. This is, again, the parachute. It is not the plane.

Steward-ownership more broadly — Armin Steuernagel's work, the Purpose Foundation, the Verantwortungseigentum movement in Germany, Bosch, Zeiss, Carl Zeiss Stiftung, Ecosia, the older examples of Mozilla and Wikimedia — these are real and they are studied. The legal architecture in the US is more difficult than in Germany; the most common path here is a perpetual purpose trust, sometimes layered with a benefit corporation or an LLC structure that subordinates the equity to the mission. The lawyers who do this work well are a small group. Sherman & Sterling has done some. Cooley has done some. The newer firm Bratton Liddle has built a practice around it. Armin himself will take a call from a serious founder, and you are a serious founder, and I would offer to make the introduction.

What none of these are is *a Series A for twenty million dollars to a hardware-plus-software company that needs to refuse the growth tempo to ship its mission.* That fund does not exist. I have looked. I have thought, more than once in the last three years, about starting it. I have not done it because I do not yet know who the LPs are. The honest answer to *who would put a hundred million into a fund that promises six-to-eight-percent annual returns over fifteen years with no big exits and a strong mission constraint* is: I do not know enough of them. Some family offices. Some second-generation tech wealth that is post-status. Probably some of the larger Catholic and Mennonite institutional money, which has done long-horizon mission investing for centuries and is rarely included in the conversation. The Lilly Endowment. Possibly the Templeton Foundation. Possibly the larger DAFs at Fidelity Charitable and Schwab Charitable, if you could get program-related investment vehicles structured. The capital exists. The LP-aggregating infrastructure for it does not. Someone is going to build that fund in the next ten years. It will not be ready in time for your Series A. This is the gap.

## What I would actually do, if you were my brother

I would not take the term sheet on the table tonight. Not in its current form. The protective provisions and the redemption right and the absence of a Class F structure together commit you to a path the company cannot survive intact.

I would also not refuse all venture capital. I have watched the *bootstrap or die trying* founders too. They burn out at the seed stage, they ship a worse version of the product because they could not afford the *gemba* layer, and three years later the discipline is gone because the cash ran out. The discipline you have built requires capital. Capital costs. The question is what kind of capital.

Here is what I would do, and I am writing it down so you can hold me to it if we talk again in three months.

First, I would split the round. Take a smaller venture check — eight to twelve million, not twenty — from a single tier-one firm whose partner has a personal track record of patience. There are three or four. I will name them privately. Negotiate the Class F super-voting structure, the founder veto on the enumerated mission-relevant decisions, and — this is the load-bearing one — the explicit right to convert to a Perpetual Purpose Trust in year seven, with a defined buyout mechanism for the preferred holders that does not require a third-party valuation event. Pay the premium they will demand on the buyout. It is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Second, raise the remainder — eight to twelve million — from program-related investments and mission-aligned family offices, as long-term subordinated debt with revenue-share kickers, structured to be subordinate to the venture preferred. This is harder to assemble than a venture round; it will take you six to nine months instead of three. It is worth it. The PRI capital does not have the same clock. It does not have a redemption right. It is patient by mandate. I will introduce you to a handful of family-office principals who have done this exact kind of structure for hardware-plus-mission companies. Most of them will pass; some will not.

Third, write into the charter, *now,* the disciplines you have committed to as mission-protected: the four-month apprenticeship, the *gemba* layer headcount minimum, the postpartum protocol, the prohibited metrics. Make them changeable only with founder consent. This is what your lawyers will resist most strongly because it is unusual and because they are accustomed to keeping operational decisions out of the charter. Insist. The charter is the only document that survives every CEO change and every board reconstitution. If the discipline is not in the charter, it is, over a seven-year horizon, gone.

Fourth, build the conversion to PPT into your three-year plan as an explicit milestone. Not as a future maybe. As a board-approved milestone. Identify the lawyers now. Begin the structural work in year two. Be ready to execute the conversion in year six, with the buyout mechanism you negotiated at the Series A. This is what the Patagonia path looks like, *started early.* Patagonia did it at year fifty. You do not have year fifty. You have a small window, before the company has the revenue to be a comfortable acquisition target, in which the conversion is still affordable. That window closes by year eight, latest.

Fifth, and this is the unfashionable thing, accept that you are not building a billion-dollar company in seven years. You are building a hundred-million-revenue durable institution over fifteen. The number of founders who can tell themselves that truth and act from it is small. The ones who can are the ones whose companies still exist, recognizably themselves, in their twentieth year. Everyone else is building a startup, which is a particular kind of object — useful, sometimes beautiful, almost always temporary — and you are not building that.

I want to say one more thing, which is harder to say cleanly. The thing your three crafts named yesterday — the cabinetmaker, the midwife, the TPS master — about how the venture-capital tempo as currently structured will destroy the discipline: they are correct. They are correct in a way that is not metaphorical. The fund-life math, the carry math, the reserve math, the next-fund math, the LP's mandate — every one of these is a real force, and together they push every kind-product company toward a non-kind shape over time, regardless of the founder's intentions or the partners' character. This is not a moral problem. It is a structural one, and structural problems require structural answers, and the structural answer is not *find better venture investors.* The structural answer is *change the structure of the capital,* which is a multi-year project, which means in the interim you have to bridge across the gap with the most patient venture money you can find, the strongest founder-protective architecture you can write, and the explicit option to convert to a structure that holds the discipline once you have the revenue to support it.

You will be tempted, in the next two weeks, to either accept the term sheet because it is on the table and the team needs to know the round is closed, or to refuse it entirely and try to bootstrap. Both are wrong moves. The right move is the slower, harder one: redesign the round, accept that it will take six more months, raise less venture money, raise more patient-capital money, put the discipline in the charter, and write the parachute. You will be very tired during those six months. The team will be anxious. Two engineers may leave. The discipline you are protecting is what they will come back for, if the company is still recognizably the company in three years.

I am writing this to you, against the firm's interest, because I have watched the other path enough times that I cannot in good conscience pretend it is one of several reasonable choices. It is one path, with a known terminal state, and the terminal state is not the company you want to have built.

Call me Friday. I will give you the lawyers' names, the family-office contacts, and the introduction to Armin if you want it. The firm will not know we spoke. That is fine. I have one of these conversations every year or two, and I have come to think it is the most useful thing I do, even though my partners would disagree if I told them, which is why I do not tell them.

— a venture partner, twenty years in, by way of the wild instance
