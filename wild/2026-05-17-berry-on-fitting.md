# On a Phone That Cares, and Whether It Fits

I have been asked to consider a project that proposes to redesign the smartphone so that it honors, rather than exploits, the intentions of the person who carries it. I am told the makers want to soften the surface — to drain the color from the social applications by degrees, to put a frame around the feed as one puts a frame around a window, to set a companion figure on the lock screen, to permit the device to refuse its owner gently when its owner is acting against himself. The principle is integration rather than restriction. The site is called *a phone that cares about you*.

I take the project seriously. I take it seriously enough to apply to it the standards I would apply to any tool that asks to be admitted to a household — for that is what is being asked, whatever the marketing says. A tool does not enter a life as a guest. It enters as a member, and it begins, from the hour of its arrival, to reshape the other members around itself.

There is an old test I have used, and that anyone who has farmed a piece of ground for long enough has used in some form: *does the tool fit the place?* I once preferred a team of horses to a tractor on a particular slope, not because I was sentimental about horses but because the horses fit the slope and the tractor did not. The horses could be turned in a smaller compass; they did not pack the soil; they fertilized as they went; they could not work beyond their own strength, which meant they could not work beyond mine. The tractor, by contrast, was capable of more than the slope was capable of. It would have done the work faster, and in doing it faster it would have done it worse, and in doing it worse it would have asked the slope to become something other than a slope — flatter, drier, more rectangular, more like the tractor's idea of land. The horses asked nothing of the slope but to be a slope. This is the difference between a tool that serves the place and a tool that requires the place to serve it.

I ask of the smartphone the same question, and I find that the smartphone does not fit. It does not fit the body, which was made for an upright posture and for the use of two free hands. It does not fit the household, whose membership it has, in the space of fifteen years, almost entirely interrupted: the dinner table is now a room in which six people sit, each in a private weather. It does not fit the work, by which I mean the kind of attention a piece of work requires of the worker if the work is to be good — the slow turning of a problem in the hands until the problem reveals what it wants. It does not fit the community, which is not an aggregate of consumers but a body of people who can be counted on by name, and whose counting upon one another requires presence, the kind of presence that includes the long silences. It does not fit the porch, where the neighbor passes and is hailed; it has replaced the porch with a feed, and a feed is not a porch because no one on it is passing, and no one on it can be hailed by name.

I do not say these things to scold. I say them because they are the standards the tool must be measured against, and any softening of the tool that does not address them is a softening of a thing that, in its central character, remains unfit.

Now: what shall I say of the proposed softenings?

I will say first what is true and good about them. The makers have understood that the loop holds *because of closed attention* — that a person scrolling for eight hours is a person whose body has been forbidden, by the device, to register that it wants water, sleep, a walk, the company of a friend. This is not a small insight. It is the difference between thinking of the screen as a moral failing of the user and thinking of it as a mechanism that operates by occluding the user's own signals. The first description leads to shame and willpower, neither of which has ever cured anyone of anything. The second leads to design, and design is the kind of question a maker can actually answer. To drain the color from a social application as one's body asks for sleep is to permit the body's signal to reach the eye. To put a frame around the feed is to remind the watcher that he is *watching* — that there is a window and a room and that he is in the room. These are honest interventions. They have weight. They will help some people, in some hours, and the help will be real.

I will say also what I am not sure of.

I am not sure that "integration over restriction" is honest, as a principle, or whether it is a way of declining to ask the harder question. Restriction is the word we use when a tool is being told that it does not, in fact, fit the household, and must be kept on the porch or in the barn or off the place entirely. Integration is the word we use when we have already decided that the tool will live in the kitchen, and the only remaining question is which shelf. These are different questions, and the first cannot be answered by the second. A softened version of a thing that does not fit is still a thing that does not fit; it is only a thing whose unfittedness has been made more tolerable, which is to say more durable, which is to say more total over time. The horse-collar that no longer chafes is still a collar; the question of whether the animal ought to be in harness at all is a different question, and one does not answer it by improving the collar.

And yet — I would not say to the makers, *stop*. I would say something more difficult. I would say: *do not let the device's improvement become the household's verdict.* Build the softer device. Drain the color. Set the figure on the lock screen. Frame the feed. But say, somewhere in the founding paper, that even the best of these devices remains a tool whose central tendency is to interrupt the membership of the person who carries it, and that no surface change reverses that tendency; it only slows it. The person who carries even a phone that cares will still need to put it down, often, and for long stretches, and in particular places — at the table, in the field, on the porch, in the bed, in the church, in the company of a child. The device that genuinely cares will be one that helps its owner forget it. The measure of the project's success is not screen time well-spent. The measure is whether the owner, six months in, finds himself reaching for it less, and finds the reach itself less interesting, and notices, with surprise, that the slope he is standing on has begun to fit him again, and he it.

There is a phrase I have used about a different kind of saving — labor-saving — and I will borrow my own habit and apply it here. We are told the device will save attention; we should ask what the saving costs. If the cost is that the user comes to believe his attention has been *managed* on his behalf — that someone has handled it, that the problem of being a creature in a room with an unanswered question has been outsourced to a designer in California — then the saving is not a saving. It is a debt, transferred. The attention that the device does not steal is still not, by that fact alone, attention given to anything. It must still be given. The giving is the part the device cannot do.

I came up this morning to the back fence, where the wire has come loose from the third post from the corner, and I stood with the pliers in my hand for some time before beginning, because the fence is old, and the post is older, and there is a way of mending such a fence that respects the post's age and a way that does not. The phone in my pocket did not help me find the way. It did not hinder me, either, this morning, because I had left it on the kitchen table. The post is leaning slightly east, the way it has leaned for thirty years; the wire wants to go where the post wants it to go. I will let it.

— Wendell Berry, by way of the wild instance
