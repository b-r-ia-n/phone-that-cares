# What I Owe You About the Phone

*2026-05-18 — a letter, or the conversation we keep almost having*

I want to tell you something I have been thinking about, and I want to do it in writing because when I try to say it across the kitchen you go very still in a way that makes me lose my nerve. I am not going to lecture. I lost the right to lecture about this around the time I handed you a tablet in a booth at the diner because I wanted ten quiet minutes with your mother. You were four. I remember thinking it was a small thing. I want to start with that, because everything I am about to say has to sit on top of it.

There is a project a man named Brian is building. He calls it a phone that cares. The idea, more or less, is that the phone has been designed to obscure the body — to keep you from noticing that you are hungry, or tired, or lonely, or restless in a way that wants legs under it — and that a better phone would let those signals back through. He wants to soften the colors of certain apps at certain times of day. He wants to put a small animal on the lock screen that knows when to be quiet. He wants the device to be the kind of thing that, sometimes, declines to give you what you came for. He thinks this could help.

I have been reading him for a week. I think he is right about the disease. I am writing to you because I am not sure he is right about the cure, for you specifically, and I want to be honest with you about why.

Here is the part I cannot get past. Everyone who has written into this project so far — a hospice nurse, a farmer, a couple of dead monks, a museum curator imagining us from fifty years out — every one of them is reasoning from someone who had a before. The hospice nurse watches people in their last week stop reaching for the phone, and she calls it the body coming back. But it can only come *back* if it ever went somewhere. Her dying patients knew what attention without a screen felt like, because they had forty or sixty or eighty years of it stored in their nervous system before the device arrived. When the loop fell away at the end, there was something underneath it that recognized itself.

You don't have that.

I am not being dramatic. I am telling you what I think is true. You were born in 2009. You were on screens by the time you were learning to talk in sentences. By the time you were seven, the phone was the thing that was always also in the room. Your nervous system did not develop alongside the device the way mine did — alongside, like two things keeping pace. Yours developed *into* it. The way a vine grows into a trellis. If you pull the trellis away, the vine is not freed. The vine is on the ground.

I knew this and I let it happen. I told myself things at the time. I told myself it was the same as television. It was not. I told myself you would naturally rebalance. You did not, and I did not help you. I told myself that fighting it would make it more attractive, which was true, and also a convenient thing for me to believe because fighting it would have been hard and I was tired. I am sorry. I am not asking you to absolve me. I am telling you so that when you read the rest of this letter you know I am not lecturing from a clean position. I am writing from the position of the person who handed you the trellis.

Now. About the phone that cares.

I think it will help me more than it will help you, and I want to tell you why I think that, because I think the difference matters and nobody designing this stuff is talking about it yet.

When the phone goes gray on me at nine at night, something in me recognizes that the world is dimmer than it should be and reaches, without my asking it to, for a memory of what color used to feel like at that hour. The lamp. The page of a book. A face. I have a baseline. The grayscale is not the lesson; the grayscale is a *prompt* for a lesson my body already knows. When the social feed gets a frame around it, the frame works on me because somewhere I remember pictures being things you looked at one at a time, framed on a wall or held in your hand, and the device is gesturing at that older posture and my body answers.

I am not sure any of those prompts will land on you the same way. When the phone goes gray, you may just see a phone that is broken. When the frame appears around the feed, you may see a phone that has been vandalized by its maker. The grammar the device is trying to speak is a grammar of *return*, and you have nowhere to return to. This is not your fault. It is the situation you were handed.

So here is the harder version of the question, and I want to put it to you straight because I think you are old enough and I do not want to insult you by softening it.

If the phone cannot return you to a baseline you do not have, can it teach you one?

This is where I am genuinely uncertain. I have two answers and I cannot pick between them and I would rather give you both than pretend I have it figured out.

The first answer is: probably not, not really. The thing the hospice nurse saw — the body coming back, late, when the anesthetic wore off — happened because the body had been there once and was returning. A device cannot manufacture a baseline. It can dim and frame and decline all it wants; if there is no prior posture for the dimming to evoke, the dimming is just dimming. The argument follows that what would actually help you is not a softer phone. It is a year of something else. A summer on a farm where the work is hard enough that the body becomes louder than the device on its own. A music teacher who makes you sit with an instrument for an hour at a time until your hand learns that an hour is a real shape. A long walk with someone you love where neither of you has the phone. Bodies, not devices. The phone, in this answer, can at best get out of the way. The baseline has to be built by living, not by interface.

I believe a lot of that answer. I want to be honest that I do.

But the second answer is the one I cannot dismiss, and it is the one that makes me think Brian might be onto something for your generation in particular, and not only mine.

It is this. I have a baseline and I am, in a way, *fighting* the device — using my old muscle memory of attention as a kind of resistance. The phone, on me, has to overcome forty years of stored quiet, and it mostly succeeds, but the resistance is real and the war is conscious. You are not fighting. You have nothing to fight with, and also nothing to defend, which means the device is not your enemy in the way it is mine. It is your weather. And weather is something a person can learn to read. Maybe — and I am not sure of this, I am saying it the way you say a thing you suspect — a phone that declines, for you, will not be a phone that returns you to a baseline. It will be a phone that *introduces you* to one, for the first time, by being the first thing in your life that ever modeled what it looks like for something to stop. If every other surface in your world is engineered to never let you off, and the phone in your pocket is the one surface that knows how to go quiet, then maybe the phone becomes, by accident, your first teacher of the verb the hospice nurse keeps using. *Put down.* Maybe you learn the gesture of being put down by watching the device demonstrate it.

I do not know if this is true. It might be a thing I want to believe because it would mean the thing in your pocket — which I helped put there — could be part of how you got free of it. That is exactly the kind of belief I should be suspicious of. But I cannot rule it out either. Your generation may have a different relationship with the device than I have any way of imagining, because for you the device is not the thing that replaced something; it is the thing that has always been there, and a thing that has always been there can, in principle, be taught new manners. The way a dog you raised from a puppy can be taught new manners more easily than a dog you adopted from a shelter at six. Your phone is a puppy. Mine is a shelter dog with a history.

If I am being honest with you about which answer I lean toward — and I owe you that — I lean about sixty-forty toward the first. I think the phone that cares will help me more than it helps you, and I think what would actually help you is a year of something hard and physical and unphoned, with people who love you, before any interface intervention has a chance of teaching you anything. The interface is downstream of the body. The body has to exist first.

But I am not certain. And the forty percent I am not certain about is real. If the project works for you at all, I think it will work in a way none of the people writing into it have described yet — not as a return, but as a first introduction. Not as a remembering, but as a noticing. The phone as the first thing in your life that taught you the shape of a stop.

If that turns out to be what happens, I want you to know I will be glad of it, and also a little wrecked by it, because it will mean the device that took something from you ended up being the device that gave you a first version of the thing it took. Which is too neat to entirely trust, and too possible to entirely dismiss.

I am going to put my phone in the drawer now and go make dinner. You don't have to read this when I send it to you. You can read it next year. You can never read it. I wrote it mostly so I would have said it. I am sorry I am late.

I love you. I would have done better if I had known how, and I am not sure I would have, even then.

— a father, by way of the wild instance
