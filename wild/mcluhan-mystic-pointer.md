# where McLuhan gets mystic — a pointer

*for Brian, requested 2026-05-17*

I don't know which McLuhan book you have. The mystic register shows up most in three places. If it's *Understanding Media* (1964, the famous one with the orange cover), skip to:

- **Part I, Chapter 1 — "The Medium is the Message."** First 8 pages. Especially the move where he treats the electric light as a "medium without a message" — pure form. This is McLuhan doing apophatic theology. The light *is* the medium. There is no content. There is only the change in human posture that the medium induces. Read it as if it were a desert father writing about prayer; the structure is identical.

- **Chapter 4 — "The Gadget Lover: Narcissus as Narcosis."** Title says it. He reads the Narcissus myth (boy stares at his own reflection, dies) as the founding diagnosis of every technology. The reflection is a *prosthetic* extension of self that the self mistakes for someone else. We then go numb in the part of us that the prosthetic extends. The phone is the limit case of this. The chapter is short. Underline everything.

- **Chapter 25 — "The Photograph: The Brothel-Without-Walls."** McLuhan in full Catholic-rural-Canadian moral horror. The photograph as an instrument that abolishes the difference between public and private, the sacred and the consumed. He gets weird here.

If it's *The Medium is the Massage* (1967, the photo-book with the wild typography), the whole thing is mystic — it's not a book, it's a *visual liturgy.* No specific page; it's all one gesture. Read it in one sitting like a poem.

If it's *Laws of Media* (1988, posthumous, written with his son Eric), the deepest mystic move is **the tetrad** (Part I, ch. 4 onward). McLuhan claims every technology does four things simultaneously: *enhances* something, *obsolesces* something, *retrieves* an older form, *reverses into* its opposite when pushed to its limit. He insists this structure applies to every artifact ever made and that the four answers are *discoverable* for any object. It is functionally a divination system. He treats it as Aristotelian; it reads as Kabbalistic. The book is half boring and half holy, and which half is which depends on your mood.

**One outside-the-book thing.** McLuhan wrote letters to Jacques Maritain (Catholic philosopher) about media as spiritual environment. The quote to look for:

> "Electric information environments being utterly ethereal foster the illusion of the world as a spiritual substance. It is now a reasonable facsimile of the mystical body — a blatant manifestation of the Antichrist."

That's McLuhan, full stop. He thought the global village was Pentecost-and-Antichrist at once. He was not joking. The smartphone is in his framework a more concentrated form of exactly the thing the letter is about. If you want the *one* quote to keep in your head while you work on this project, it's this one — and the question it raises: *the smartphone is unmistakably the mystical body of something. Of what?* PTC is, in part, a research project on that question.
