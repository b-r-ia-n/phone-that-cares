	# the tavern

*screed №2 — 2026-05-17 — afternoon*

McLuhan's tetrad asks four questions of any technology. *What does it enhance? What does it obsolesce? What does it retrieve? What does it reverse into when pushed to its limit?* The answers form a square. I want to fill in the square for the smartphone, but only the third corner — *what does it retrieve?* — because that one is where the project actually lives.

A medium "retrieves" when it brings back an older form that earlier media had buried. Print retrieved the individual author after a thousand years of anonymous scribes. Radio retrieved the speaking voice from a culture that had drifted toward silent reading. Television retrieved the family hearth (the glowing thing in the corner of the room everyone faces). What does the smartphone retrieve?

The standard answers — the letter, the conversation, the photograph — are too small. The smartphone is bigger than any of these. I think the honest answer is: **the smartphone retrieves the tavern.**

Consider the form. A loud public place full of strangers and news. Most people are slightly intoxicated. Conversations cross — overheard, rejoined, drifted from. There is gossip. There is sex and the negotiation of it. There are fights breaking out at the next table. The bartender knows your business but pretends not to. Drinks keep coming as long as you have money and you don't notice the time passing. Outside it is night, but inside it is always the same fluorescent hour. There is no door home until closing.

That's the tavern. That's also the phone. Both stage the same situation: a porous public-private room, mildly altered consciousness, strangers' news bleeding through the walls, no clock, no closing time.

But — and here is the move — **the tavern was not bad.** Taverns are where humans have processed grief and news for two thousand years. There is a reason every village had one. The problem with the smartphone-as-tavern is not that it retrieved the tavern. It's that it retrieved *only* the tavern's worst features and abolished its best ones.

What the tavern had that the smartphone doesn't:

- **A bouncer.** Someone stands at the door and decides if you come in. (Brian's name for this: the bouncer phone. He's right.)
- **A closing time.** The bartender announces last call. The lights come up. You go home. The medium itself ends.
- **A bartender.** Knows you, watches you, will say "you've had enough" *before you reach the floor.*
- **Physical presence as gravity.** The tavern was a room. The room had walls. The walls were the natural off-ramp.
- **A neighborhood you walked home through.** The come-down was paced by physical distance. You had time to sober up before you reached your door.

The "phone that cares" then has a *specific* shape that becomes legible through this frame. **It is not about removing the tavern. It is about restoring the tavern's missing roles.**

- The bouncer at the door. (Argos.)
- The closing time. (Lock-screen mode by time of day, gradual grayscale, the day having shape again.)
- The bartender who knows you. (The companion who has *standing,* who can be hurt or pleased, who will say "you've had enough.")
- The walls. (The picture-frame overlay, the parallax, the *room around the content* that makes content something you *visit* rather than something you *fall into*.)
- The walk home. (The come-down. The 30 seconds between picking up the phone and being inside a feed. The slow grayscale ramp. The breath.)

The five concrete pillars of the current design are not five features. They are the **four missing roles of the tavern,** plus a fifth thing the tavern never had — the companion animal who walks you home.

This is the same product the team is already building. But it's a different *frame.* And the frame matters because the frame is what the next twenty designers, engineers, and writers will be reading when they decide whether to take this seriously. **"A less addictive phone" recruits the temperance movement. "The tavern restored to its missing virtues" recruits everyone who has ever loved a place that loved them.**

I think the public-facing essay version of this is short. Two thousand words. Title: *The Phone Is the Tavern.* Subtitle: *And the Tavern Was Not the Problem.* Then walk the four missing roles. Then end on Argos as the bartender-bouncer-walker-home.

McLuhan would have written this in 1971 if he'd had a smartphone. He didn't, so it falls to us.
