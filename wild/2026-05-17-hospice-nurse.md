# What the Last Week Asks For

Thirty years on the floor. I've sat with somewhere north of three thousand people while they finished. I want to tell you what I've seen, because the man building this phone is asking a question I have an answer to, and the answer is harder than the brief.

First, the phones. They come into the room now the way rosaries used to. The patient has one, the spouse has one, the adult children each have one, the night nurse has one. In the last week of a person's life there are sometimes more lit screens in the room than there are eyes open. I have watched a daughter sit through her mother's final eighteen hours scrolling, looking up when the breathing changed, going back down. I do not judge her. She was terrified. The phone was the only thing in the room she knew how to operate. Everything else — the body in the bed, the silence, her own grief — required a competence nobody had taught her.

Here is what the dying do with their phones in the last weeks, in the order I see it. First they keep using them like they always did, until the energy goes. Then they ask someone to hold the phone for them, because the arms are too heavy — usually for one specific thing: a voice memo from a grandchild, a photo of a dog, a recording of a song from before they were married. Then they ask for it to be put away. Not always with words. Sometimes they just stop looking at it when it is offered. The hand that reached for it yesterday does not reach for it today. This is one of the cleanest signs I know that the work has turned inward and the person is beginning to leave. They are not bored. They have simply stopped needing input. The body, in its last competence, becomes very selective about what it admits.

What they reach for at the end is almost never on the phone. It is a hand. It is a window — they ask for the curtain opened, even when their eyes can no longer focus, because something in them wants the light on the side of their face. It is a specific person, who is sometimes in the room and sometimes not. It is a smell from a childhood kitchen, which we cannot produce, and which they describe anyway. It is water, even when they can no longer swallow. The list is short and it has not changed in thirty years. Touch, light, breath, the named beloved, water. The phone is not on this list. I have never once seen it on this list.

What they regret is more various, but it has shapes. They regret not having said one specific sentence to one specific person. They regret a job they took for the money. They regret a friendship they let go cold for no reason they can now reconstruct. They regret, with a frequency that I cannot ignore, "how much time I spent on that thing" — and in the last decade "that thing" is, increasingly, the phone. I have heard versions of this sentence from a forty-six-year-old woman dying of breast cancer and from an eighty-eight-year-old man dying of nothing in particular, and they did not know each other and they used almost the same words. They did not say it as a confession. They said it as a fact they had finally been given the time to notice. The closed attention had broken open, late, and they could see the shape of where it had gone.

This is the place where I want to speak directly to Brian's project, because I think he is more right than he yet knows, and also slightly wrong in a way that matters.

He is right that the loop holds by closing attention to the body. I watch this confirmed in the inverse, every week. When a person is dying, attention re-opens. Not because they have become wise. Because the anesthetic has stopped working — the body has gotten too loud to ignore, and the future has gotten too short to spend on approximate stimulation. The dying do not scroll for eight hours. They cannot. The signal is too strong. What I take from this is that the loop is not natural; it is a configuration that requires a healthy enough body and a busy enough imagination to sustain. Strip either away and the loop collapses on its own. Which means the loop is, in some quiet way, a sickness pretending to be a habit. Brian's instinct that it is a *display* problem and not a *willpower* problem matches what I see when the display stops working: the person comes back. Not improved. Just present again.

Where I would push him: a phone that "cares" cannot only soften. The most caring thing in a room is often the person who declines to distract you from what you are actually feeling. The best nurses I have trained under did not comfort first. They sat. They let the patient be uncomfortable in their presence without rushing to fix it. The fixing came later, and was smaller than the sitting. If the device is going to deserve the word *care*, it has to be willing, sometimes, to be boring. To go dim and stay dim. To not produce a substitute when the user is reaching for one. The companion animal on the lock screen — I like it, instinctively — will be a real companion only if it sometimes sits with the user in their discomfort and offers nothing. A dog that always wants to play is not a companion; it is a demand. The dogs my patients ask for, at the end, are the ones who used to lie on the floor next to them while they cried, and did nothing, and did not leave.

The other thing. Build the closing-time. Build the grayscale. Build the frame. But understand what you are buying the user: not peace. Time. You are buying them back the hours in which the question they have been avoiding can finally find them. Some of them will use those hours to call the person they should have called. Some will use them to invent a new evasion. That is not your problem to solve, and you should not try, because the people who try to solve it become the next platform.

What I would want, if I am honest, is a phone that knows how to be put down. That accepts being put down. That does not, in its small electronic vanity, keep reaching for the hand that has stopped reaching for it. The dying teach this last lesson without meaning to. The hand opens. The thing falls away. The room gets quiet. And what was wanted all along — light on the face, a hand, a name spoken once — turns out to have been in the room the whole time, waiting to be noticed.

Build a phone that knows how to be the thing in the room that gets quiet first.

— a hospice nurse, by way of the wild instance
