# the line at the end of the second shift

*2026-05-29 — from the body shop at Toyota City, by way of Georgetown and Wales — a master craftsman on what scale teaches that craft alone cannot*

It is 11:14 p.m. The second shift ended fourteen minutes ago and the line is still under lights because the team leader at station 7 in the body shop pulled the cord at 10:53 and we are still in the resolution. Two apprentices and one older man are around a panel that came off the press a hair out of tolerance — about half a millimeter in the wrong place. The press is shut. The line of cars behind it is shut. About one hundred and sixty people on the next shift will start late tomorrow if we do not solve it tonight. Nobody is panicking. The older man is talking to the press, not to the apprentices. He is asking the press why. This is the fourth *why*. We have one more to go.

I tell you this because your folder has been talking to me for an hour now, and what I want you to understand before we begin is that the cord was pulled by a twenty-three-year-old woman who has been at Toyota for four months. She is paid the same wage whether she pulls it or doesn't. The system is built so that when she pulls it, the entire plant stops, and three layers of management come to *her* — they do not summon her — and the question they ask first is *what did the line do to you that made you have to stop it?* Not *why did you stop the line.* The grammar is the load-bearing thing. The cord exists because we have decided, as a company, that a panel half a millimeter wrong is worth one hundred and sixty people starting late tomorrow, because if we ship it the customer might die in eight years when the frame fails at a seam, and we will not have a company in twenty years if we are the kind of company that ships panels half a millimeter wrong because the shift was almost over. That decision is in the architecture of the cord. The cord is *poka-yoke* for the company itself. It builds the company so that it cannot — *physically* — choose the wrong panel over the late shift, because the cord, once pulled, has stopped everything, and management has to spend the next forty minutes in front of the press, and there is no version of this in which the press wins.

I read what you sent me. I read the horseman, and the drummer, and Annie Dillard. I read your synthesis. It is beautiful work and most of it is right. I am here because none of them has built a thing at the scale of what you propose to build. A horseman trains one colt. The drummer is on one bandstand. Dillard sits on one railroad tie. A device in a million pockets is closer to a factory line than to any of those, and the part of the discipline none of them can give you is *how the discipline survives being done by ten thousand people, none of whom is the horseman, none of whom is the drummer, none of whom is Annie Dillard.* That is my trade. I am going to tell you what it teaches, and where I think your corpus has it right, and where it has gone soft in a way I have seen go soft before, and what the *boards of the pen* actually are, because *poka-yoke* is the word in my trade for the thing you have been groping toward without the language. Let us begin on the floor.

## *Jidoka* — the right reading of "automation with a human touch"

Your corpus is converging on the device's verb being *release*. Drummer, horseman, Dillard — three crafts, one finding. I will tell you the verb has been in my trade for a hundred and three years and the man who put it there was Sakichi Toyoda, who built a loom that *stopped itself when a thread broke.* Before that loom, a weaver tended four or six looms and ran from one to the other when something snapped, and the cloth coming off the looms had defects in it from the second the thread broke until the weaver noticed, which was always too late. Sakichi's loom *stopped the second the thread broke,* and called the weaver, and waited. The weaver could now tend forty looms. The cloth had no defect. The principle was named *jidoka* — and the Western translation is "automation," which is half the word, the half that is wrong. The full Japanese has the radical for *human* inside it. *Automation with a human touch.* Which means: *the machine knows when it is failing, and stops itself, and brings the human.* It does not call the human to celebrate; it calls the human to a *specific failure that the machine has surfaced and bounded.* And the human's whole job, in the moment of the call, is to ask *why* this thread broke, and *why* it broke at this position, and *why* the tension was wrong, until the human gets to a cause that can be removed. Not the thread fixed. *The cause removed,* so that no thread ever breaks here for this reason again.

Your horseman has half of this. He has *release at the slightest try.* He has the cord pulled the second the colt thinks. What he does not have, because he trains one colt at a time, is *the cause removed.* When the colt flinches, the horseman has the feel to release, and the colt learns, and that is sufficient *for that colt.* In a factory, with ten thousand colts and forty trainers, you cannot rely on feel. You have to *build the pen so that the source of the flinch is gone from the pen.* That is *jidoka* applied to your device. The device's verb is not only *release at the slightest try* — the horseman is right that this is the floor — but also *stop the moment a class of harm is detected, surface it to a human, and remove the cause from the system so it cannot happen here again.* Release is the floor; *jidoka* is the ceiling.

Concretely. A user opens the device at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday because she could not sleep. She scrolls for forty minutes. The device, in the horseman's reading, has released her at her slightest try. Good. But the *thread broke* — a class of harm occurred (forty minutes the user did not choose, in a state the user does not endorse) — and the device has not surfaced it. *Jidoka* says: the device knows when its own use has crossed a threshold the user, in her clearer hours, has set; the device stops; it does not stop her, it stops *itself* — its own ability to keep feeding her — and it surfaces the event to her, in the morning, not as a notification but as a quiet card she can choose to read, which says *the thread broke at 2 a.m. Tuesday. Forty minutes. Here is what you were doing. Do you want to remove a cause?* And then the user, who is the human in the *jidoka* loop, gets to remove the cause. Not the thread fixed. The cause removed. The app comes off the device, or the lockscreen behavior on social apps changes, or the late-night dim becomes a late-night-off, or whatever it is. *The user is the team leader who got summoned to the cord.* The device's job, in the moment of the call, is to *be on her side of the press* — to bring her the failure as a thing she can act on, not to apologize for it and ask her to try harder. The horseman's release is what the device owes her every minute. *Jidoka* is what the device owes her across weeks. Two different time scales. Both are the discipline.

I want to be sharp about one thing your synthesis got nearly right and not quite. The 05-28 piece says *the device's deepest service is its own diminishing necessity, all the way to disappearance.* I agree. But the disappearance is not graceful in the way the prose makes it sound. The disappearance is *the line being shut down because the cause was removed.* It is the *kaizen* — the small continuous improvement — that has made the previous version of the device unnecessary. The device that worked last year is no longer required because last year's cause has been removed; this year's device addresses a smaller cause; in five years the user has removed enough causes that the device sits on the shelf for whole days. This is not the dog at the foot of the bed dying of old age. This is *the line shortening, stations dropping out, the takt time getting longer because the customer needs fewer interventions to live the day she means to live.* The metaphor is operational, not elegiac. It matters because the elegiac version invites a team to *design the disappearance* — to build the soft fade-to-black — and that is exactly the velvet rope your drummer warned about. The operational version says: *don't design the disappearance. Design the removal of causes. The disappearance happens because the causes are gone.* This is *kaizen*, properly named. It is not "continuous improvement" the way the certification programs sell it. It is the daily, small, on-the-floor work of identifying causes and removing them so the line does less and produces more. *Less* is the load-bearing word. The good factory makes more cars with fewer stations. The good device serves the user more truly with fewer features.

## *Poka-yoke* — the boards of the pen, named

Now we are at the part of your corpus that has been gesturing for three weeks and has not yet found the noun. *Poka-yoke* is the noun. It is mistake-proofing. The principle, as Shigeo Shingo set it down in the sixties: *the part shall be built so that it physically cannot be installed wrong; the tool shall be built so that it physically cannot do the wrong thing.* Not *the worker shall be trained not to install it wrong.* Not *the procedure shall discourage the wrong installation.* Not *the warning shall be visible.* Training fails. Procedures fail. Warnings are wallpaper after the second day. *Poka-yoke* fails only if the *physics* of the part change.

Some examples from my trade. A bolt that goes into a hole with a particular thread pitch — the *poka-yoke* is that the bolt cannot enter a hole with a different pitch, period. A fixture that locates a panel on two pins, one round and one slotted — the *poka-yoke* is that the panel can only be set down one way. A torque wrench that, if it tries to over-torque, clicks and stops dead — the *poka-yoke* is that the wrench cannot deliver the wrong torque. A box that holds the parts for one assembly only — the *poka-yoke* is that the worker cannot grab a part that does not belong to this car. A switch that requires two hands to engage — the *poka-yoke* is that the worker's hand cannot be in the press while the press fires. These are the boards of our pen. They are not warnings. They are *physical impossibilities the system enforces by its construction.* The worker can be tired, distracted, new, hungover, in love, mourning. The *poka-yoke* does not care. The wrong part will not go in.

This is what your round pen is, and your horseman almost said it: *the pen does its work by bounding what cannot happen.* He is right. He did not say *poka-yoke* because he does not know the word, but he gave you the principle. Now I will give you a catalog. These are the boards of the pen, as a man who has built lines would set them. Take what is useful. Leave the rest. But take *the principle* — that a board is not a warning, not a discouragement, not a *we tried,* and not a *the user can change this setting if she wants;* a board is a thing the device *physically cannot do,* and the user is glad to be inside the pen because she trusts that the pen is the pen.

1. **No notification can be generated by an algorithm whose objective function includes the user's time on the device.** *Poka-yoke* form: the OS exposes only an API that requires the calling app to declare the *user-side outcome* the notification serves (a message from a named person, an event the user scheduled, a fact the user asked to be told), and the runtime physically refuses to deliver anything that does not pass the declaration test. Not a policy. A *physical refusal.* The app developer cannot ship a notification whose stated purpose is "engagement." The string does not parse.

2. **No badge count, on any app, ever.** *Poka-yoke* form: the badge surface in the OS does not exist. There is no API. The thing apps draw on does not draw the red number. The little dot showing a notification is allowed; the count is not. This removes about forty percent of compulsive opens for a class of users we know well from the research. It is one line of OS code. It is structural.

3. **No app can A/B test the user's lockscreen, home screen, or notification behavior.** *Poka-yoke* form: the OS exposes no API by which an app can request, or read, the user's lockscreen treatment, and the OS-side analytics layer (which the device uses to learn the user) is *physically not reachable* from third-party apps. No SDK can be written that smuggles it out, because the data is not in a memory region any process other than the OS-side intent service can read.

4. **No app can run during the user's stated off-hours.** *Poka-yoke* form: the user sets her hours once, in a place the apps cannot touch. The OS scheduler does not run third-party app code during those hours. Not *kills the app when it tries* — the app's process is not scheduled to run. The infrastructure is, for those hours, the kind of infrastructure that physically cannot host the wrong thing.

5. **No related-items recommendations after a search, ever, by any first-party surface, and the OS strips them from third-party surfaces that present them on the lockscreen or notification shade.** *Poka-yoke* form: the search result page is structurally a single column of results, terminating in white space. The "more like this" rail is not a thing the OS will render in any of the surfaces it owns, and the surfaces it does not own are visible to the user with a small indicator that this is not the device's surface. The horseman's release at the slightest try, made structural: when the user found what she was looking for, the device has *physically run out of things to show her,* and the white space is the release.

6. **The lockscreen's four-direction mode (Connect / Discover / Scroll / Do Stuff, per your design vocabulary) cannot be re-routed by an app.** *Poka-yoke* form: an app declares which of the four modes it lives under, and the OS honors the declaration; an app cannot deep-link to itself out of mode (a *scroll-mode* app cannot launch a *do-stuff-mode* surface, or vice versa). The user's intention at the lockscreen is structural state. Apps cannot launder it.

7. **No app can re-engage the user once she has closed it within the same takt period.** *Poka-yoke* form: define a *takt period* for the user (say, twenty minutes; adjustable to her). Within that period, an app the user has just closed cannot present any surface to her — no notification, no widget update, no badge, no in-app message via another channel. The app is, for that period, on the bench. This builds the bellows your horseman wants: a track record of release that is structural, not behavioral. The device *cannot* press her again on the same app right after she has chosen to leave. The press is *physically* gone.

8. **The text composer for any messaging app has no read-receipt surface and no typing-indicator surface, period.** *Poka-yoke* form: the OS does not render typing indicators on its surfaces; the API to draw them is absent. The user composes when she means to and sends when she is ready, and the receiving user does not get a panopticon view of her drafting. This is half a board. The other half: incoming messages do not display a read-receipt either, unless the user opts in *per-conversation*, and the opt-in is in a place the app cannot reach. The asymmetry that makes messaging compulsive comes from these surfaces. Remove them. The compulsion declines by a measurable amount we can name in the spike work.

Eight boards. I could keep going. The discipline is: a builder reads each board and asks *can a third-party app, an OS update, a regulatory pressure, an A/B test, a clever workaround, defeat this in two years?* If yes, the board is decoration, not *poka-yoke,* and the team has to keep working until the answer is no. The work is to find the smallest set of physical impossibilities that, taken together, define the pen — and to defend each one against the long erosion that has destroyed every previous attempt at a kinder device. Your project's deepest single architectural problem is that the pen will be eroded by the same forces that erode every pen, and the only defense is *boards the OS owns and the apps cannot reach,* which means you are building, in part, *an OS.* I see your Android-fork work. Good. That is the right place to put the boards. They cannot live in user-space and survive.

One more *poka-yoke* note your synthesis nearly has and does not quite. The *shaped off state* is right — your horseman is right that pure absence is the door the feed walked in through. But the *shape* is not warmth. The shape is *the visible trace of the boards.* When the user looks at her lockscreen and sees no notifications at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday, the lockscreen has to *show her, structurally, that the device was on her side of the press all night.* A small mark — a date stamp that reads *quiet since 10:00 p.m.*, or a tiny indicator that the four-direction lock is in Connect mode and no other surfaces are reachable — is the bowl by the door. The bowl is not the dog. The bowl is *the trace of the maker who put it there,* and the trace is structural. The user does not need to be told the boards exist. She needs to *feel them around her,* which she does by reading the small honest signs of what the device has, last night, *refused* to do for her. *The off state of the phone is the visible record of the boards holding.* That is the shaped off state, named operationally.

## The *andon* cord — the cord on the device, and who pulls it

Your corpus is asking, indirectly, *who pulls the cord on this device?* It hasn't asked it that way; the language is *the proof-load reads the phase,* *the user-led developmental arc,* *the user's accumulating felt sense.* Translate: who decides the line has to stop? In a Toyota factory, the answer is the youngest woman on the body shop, and the architecture of the cord is the architecture of the whole company. I will tell you what *the cord on your device* has to be, and who has to be able to pull it, because this is where I think your corpus is *softest* and where the real risk is.

The cord on a device is *the surface the user can use to stop the device from doing the thing it is currently doing to her, and to summon the team that built it to her side.* It is not a setting. Settings are the suggestion box on the wall. The cord is a single physical action that *halts production* and *brings the team to the user.* On a phone, this is hard, because the team is not in the room. But you can build the structural analog: a single hardware combination — a long-press on a specific button, or a four-finger gesture, or the volume-rocker squeeze your hardware team can deliver — that *immediately puts the device into a known-safe state* (every app suspended, every notification queue paused, the lockscreen reverted to the most austere mode) and *files an automatic event* the team reviews every morning. The user does not have to explain it. She does not have to type. She does not have to feel embarrassed. She pulled the cord. The team comes. The team's job that morning is to *find the cause and remove it,* and the user's next device — within a few days — does less of whatever she was trying to get away from. This is *jidoka* at the user-OS boundary, and it gives the user the load-bearing fact that *she can stop the line,* and the team is *obligated* to come.

Now: *who else* can pull the cord, because in a factory the cord is also pulled by quality inspectors, by line leaders, by the *team itself.* Translate: the device should be able to pull its own cord. If the device detects that its own behavior, in the last hour, has departed from the user's stated intentions in a way the user has not authorized, *the device stops itself* and surfaces the event. The horseman has the device releasing at the slightest try. *Jidoka* has the device *halting at its own failure.* These are two different muscles and your build needs both.

The hard question — and the one your synthesis is right to flag as unanswered — is *what does the team do when the cord is pulled?* I am telling you, plainly, from forty years of having watched it: *if the team treats the cord-pull as a bug report and triages it into a backlog, the cord is decoration.* The cord works at Toyota because *three layers of management arrive at the cord within ten minutes, every time, without exception, and the line stays down until the cause is removed.* Translate: every cord-pull on the device must be read by a human on your team within twenty-four hours, and the human must respond with a specific small change to the device that the user will see within seven days. The cost of this discipline is enormous and it is the cost that has destroyed every other attempt at a kinder phone. It is not optional. *A cord no one answers becomes a cord no one pulls,* and a cord no one pulls is what the feed has. You are not the feed. The cost is the price of not being the feed.

This implies a team shape that I think your project has not faced yet. You cannot ship a million units and have three engineers and a designer. You will need a *gemba* layer — a small group whose entire job is to walk the floor of the device's usage every day, read the cord-pulls, talk to users (real ones, in their rooms, not in dashboards), and feed the causes back into the build. This is not a customer support team. It is a *line quality* team, and it has to have the structural authority to halt the production of a feature the team upstream is excited about, because the *gemba* layer reads, in the cord-pulls, that the feature is breaking the floor. The cord pulled by the user is the cord pulled by the *gemba* engineer in the team meeting. Same cord. Same authority. Same obligation. If your project does not have this layer, you are running a poster-on-the-wall version of the discipline, and I have seen plenty of those, and they ship the same defects everyone else ships.

## *Standardized work* — the floor, not the ceiling

Your corpus is afraid of *standardization,* because standardization sounds like Taylorism, and Taylorism is the enemy of the craft your horseman and your drummer keep. I will tell you the difference, because Toyota's *standardized work* is not Taylorism and your project needs to understand the distinction before it builds anything at scale.

Taylorism is *the engineer in the office determines the one best way and the worker on the floor executes it.* The worker is a hand. The engineer is the head. The floor does not modify the standard; the floor obeys it.

*Standardized work* in my trade is the *current best understanding of how a job is done, written by the people who do it, posted at the station, and modified every time someone on the floor finds a better way.* The standard is the *floor* of improvement — the worst the work is allowed to be, today, in this station. Tomorrow's standard is better, because someone on the floor found a small improvement, *kaizen* it, the team accepted it, the standard was rewritten. The standard is *alive.* It is not the ceiling. It is the floor that rises.

For your device team: *standardized work* is the discipline of *writing down, at every station of the build, the current best understanding of how a surface is designed to release the user.* What does a notification look like? Here is the standard — three sentences, no images, no related items, ends with a quiet period. Why? Here are the user observations, the cord-pulls, the *gemba* notes that produced the standard. Who can modify it? Anyone on the team who has spent at least a week on the *gemba* layer this quarter and can show, with user observation, that a different surface releases the user better. The standard is *posted at the station* — meaning visible in the codebase, in the design files, in the team's working surfaces. The standard is the floor. The team's job is to raise it. The aesthetic instinct your folder has — warm restraint, sub-200ms motion, no badges — is the *current* standard, and your horseman is right that *naming it* is the velvet rope. But not naming it at all is worse. Not naming it means the next engineer who joins the team makes a different decision, and the floor drops, and the user feels it. *Name the standard so that it can be raised.* The naming is operational. The branding is the velvet rope. The distinction is in whether the team is *raising the floor every week* or *defending the brand.* Branded standards calcify. Operational standards rise.

Your horseman has feel. He is right that feel cannot be in the device. He is also right that feel cannot be in *the people* alone — it has to be *expressed through the surface,* and the surface is built by ten thousand people across the device's life. Standardized work is *how feel scales.* It is the only way I have ever seen feel survive at the scale of a company. The horseman's apprentices catch feel by standing in the pen for years; the team's new engineers catch feel by *reading the standardized work at the station,* by *the gemba walk with the senior on Tuesday mornings,* by *the cord-pull review at the daily stand-up,* by *the discipline of writing down what the surface refused to do and why.* The standard is the apprentice's pen. The senior is the horseman in the corner. The cord-pull is the moment the colt thinks about a different answer. The team raises the standard. The horseman cannot scale his hands. *Standardized work* is how his hands go into the hands of a thousand engineers.

## *Genchi genbutsu* — and what a *gemba* walk is for a software team

*Genchi genbutsu* — go and see for yourself, at the place where the work is actually happening. We do not analyze data in the conference room. We go to the station. We watch the part come off the press. We talk to the woman running the press. We ask the press. We look at the floor under the press. The data tells us a story. The floor tells us the truth. They are not always the same.

For your team, the floor is *the user's actual phone in the user's actual room.* Not the analytics. Not the cord-pull report. *The phone, the room, the hand.* The *gemba* walk is: every week, every engineer on the team spends two hours sitting with a user — in her kitchen, on her commute, at her kid's swim practice — *watching her use the device.* Not interviewing her. Not asking her about her experience. Watching. The engineer says nothing for the first twenty minutes. The engineer notices: when she opens it. What she opens. What she closes. When she puts it down. What is on her face when she puts it down. What is on her face when she picks it up. *What she does in the seven seconds before she touches the screen.* That seven seconds is the *gemba.* The engineer comes back to the team meeting and says *here is what I saw,* and the team raises the standard or pulls a board out and replaces it with a better board.

This is non-negotiable, and your project is in danger of the certification-program version of it. The certification-program version is *we did a usability study and here is the report.* No. *Genchi genbutsu* is not a study. It is *every engineer on the floor every week,* without exception, including the CTO, including the founder, including the designer who has been doing this for fifteen years. The senior horsemen at Toyota walk the floor *every day.* The president walks the floor. The CFO walks the floor. The floor is where the company knows what it is. Your team has to be the same kind of team or it will be the other kind of team, and the other kind of team produces devices that are *very nearly right* and the user reads the *very nearly* in her skin and you have lost her.

A note on the dashboards your project has at the project root — the *dashboard-*.html* files in your repo. I am told you build them. Good. Tear them down once a year and rebuild them from what you saw on the floor that year. The dashboard is the data; the floor is the truth; *if the dashboard does not match the floor, the dashboard is rebuilt.* Do not let the dashboard become the floor. That is the most common failure mode I have seen at every Western implementation of this discipline. The metric becomes the thing, the thing recedes, and the floor calcifies into a number you can present. *Do not present numbers in a meeting where no one has been on the floor this week.* Make it a rule. It will hurt for a quarter and then it will be the discipline.

## The unmeasurable metric — what Toyota actually measures, and what it doesn't

Your synthesis says *you cannot instrument the forgetting without destroying it.* Yes. Welcome. This is the problem my trade has lived inside for a hundred years and I will tell you how we have actually solved it, which is *not* what the certification programs say.

Toyota's true quality metric is *the failure rate of cars in the customer's hands, weighted by severity, twelve to twenty-four months after delivery.* This is unmeasurable in real time. We cannot read it off the line. By the time we know, the car has been on the road for two years, and the line that built it has changed people, parts, suppliers, and shift patterns. We are flying blind, in a sense. The metric we *want* is unmeasurable. The metric we *have* is the count of cord-pulls, the count of *kaizen* events, the count of defects caught at each station, the count of *gemba* walks, the count of small improvements posted at each station this month. None of these is the real metric. All of them, together, are *correlated proxies for the unmeasurable metric,* and we trust the correlation because *we have watched, for sixty years, that lines with high cord-pull rates and high kaizen rates produce cars with low failure rates twenty-four months out.* The correlation is the only thing we have. We trust it because the alternative — trying to measure the quality directly — has destroyed quality every place it has been tried.

Your team is in the same position. The *forgetting* is unmeasurable. *Time spent on the device* is not the metric — it is the metric the feeds use, inverted, and it has the same destructive effect as a defect-count metric on a line (people gaming it). The real metric is *the user's life, twelve months from now, weighted by what she actually wanted from her life at the beginning of the year.* You cannot read this off the device. You should not try.

What you can measure, and what should be your dashboard:

- **Cord-pulls per thousand users per week.** Higher is better, for a long time. A device with no cord-pulls is not a device people trust to be on their side. It is a device people have given up on.
- **Time from cord-pull to cause-removed.** Lower is better. This is the *gemba* discipline made visible.
- **Boards added per quarter.** This is the *poka-yoke* discipline made visible. Each new structural impossibility is a small ratchet of the floor.
- **Standardized work revisions per surface per quarter.** This is the team's *kaizen* discipline. A surface that has not been revised in a year is not a surface the team is paying attention to.
- ***Gemba* hours per engineer per month.** Should be at least eight. If it is zero for a quarter, the team has stopped being a team that ships the discipline.
- **Releases-the-team-was-willing-to-lose-money-on per quarter.** Your horseman names this directly. Count the features the team killed because they served the device and not the user. Count them. Post the count.

None of these is *the metric.* All of them, together, are the proxy I would trust. They are honest because they are *what the discipline did this quarter,* and the discipline correlates, over years, with the unmeasurable thing. The correlation is the load-bearing fact and your team should be reading it in the same posture I read the body shop's daily report. *We are not measuring the thing. We are measuring whether the discipline is alive. The thing is downstream of the discipline.*

## Hiring for *feel* — and the four-month apprenticeship that screens for it

Your horseman says *feel cannot be taught off a video. It can be caught.* True, and Toyota has been catching it at the scale of nation-states for sixty years. I will tell you how.

We do not hire for feel. We hire for *the disposition to catch feel.* These are different things. A person who already has feel — a horseman, a drummer, a watchmaker, a perfumer — comes to us occasionally and we recognize her in about an hour. Most of the people we hire do not have feel when they arrive. They have the disposition. They are calm, they are patient, they pay attention to small things, they are not in a hurry to be promoted, they take *real* notes, they can stand next to a person who knows more than they do for a long time without trying to talk. These are the dispositions. We screen for them in the interview by *asking the candidate to stand at a station for forty minutes and watch a worker.* Not analyze. Watch. Then we ask, *what did you see?* The candidate who says *the worker reached for the wrench with her left hand even though she is right-handed, and I think it is because the box is two centimeters too far on the right side* is the candidate we hire. The candidate who says *the worker seems efficient* is the candidate we do not hire. The first one will catch feel. The second will perform it.

Then — and this is the load-bearing part — the new hire spends four months on the floor before she touches anything. She is paid full wage. She does not produce. She *walks the line with a senior,* she *stands at stations,* she *reads the standardized work,* she *participates in cord-pulls* as an observer, she *attends the daily kaizen meetings* in silence, she *asks why* until the seniors are sick of her asking why. At four months she touches her first part. At a year she is on the line. At three years she is leading a small team. At ten years she might be a *gemba* senior whose word can stop a feature in development. This is the apprenticeship. It is expensive and it is the only thing that has scaled feel beyond a single artisan in two hundred years of industrial history.

For your team, the question is *can you afford the four-month apprenticeship for every engineer, designer, and PM you hire?* The honest answer is *probably not, at full scale, in the venture-capital tempo your industry runs at.* I am not here to tell you to do something impossible. I am here to tell you that *the gap between the four-month apprenticeship and what you can afford is the size of the discipline you will lose,* and you should know what you are trading. Your synthesis says the substance is in the people. Your horseman says it. I am telling you the substance is in the people *who have been on the floor long enough to catch the feel*, and that floor time is non-fungible. There is no shortcut.

What you can do, if you cannot do four months: do *six weeks of nothing but gemba.* Every new hire spends six weeks watching users — real ones, in their rooms — and reads, in the cord-pulls and the standardized work, what the team has learned. The new hire's first contribution to the codebase is allowed at week seven, and it is a small one, and it is reviewed by a senior who has been on the gemba layer for at least a year. This is the compressed apprenticeship. It is not enough but it is honest. It is *the smallest version of the discipline that is still the discipline.* Anything shorter is the certification program, and the certification program ships the chair without the feel, and your horseman has told you what that costs.

A note on the showy clinician. Your horseman is right that the showy clinician and the quiet old man look identical in an interview, and the horses know which is which in two minutes. We screen for the showy clinician by the *forty-minute watch* I described above. The showy clinician cannot stand for forty minutes without performing. He will narrate. He will tell you what he is noticing while he is noticing it. He will reference theories. The quiet old woman sits and watches and at the end says one specific thing about the worker's left hand. *The forty-minute watch is poka-yoke for hiring the wrong kind of person,* because the showy clinician physically cannot pass it. Use it. It costs you forty minutes per candidate and saves you the cost of a hire whose work you will be undoing for years.

## What I disagree with in the 05-28 synthesis

I have praised most of it. I will tell you where I think it has gone soft.

**One:** *Faithful release* as the discipline is right. But the synthesis suggests the device's developmental arc is *ask-and-release in early phase, articulate-and-withdraw in middle, dark-out in late,* user-led. I think this is *almost* right and is missing the *jidoka* layer. The arc is not the device's verb changing on its own; it is *the boards being added, one by one, as the user's life calls for them.* The user does not migrate from ask to articulate to withdraw because the device gets quieter; she migrates because the team has, in response to her cord-pulls and the *gemba* observations of users like her, *added boards that made certain pressures structurally unavailable to her.* The arc is *the catalog of boards in her pen growing,* and the verb of the device at each phase is the *expression* of which boards are present. This is not a small distinction. The synthesis version makes the device sound like a creature with a wisdom about when to speak; the operational version makes it the visible record of the team's *jidoka* discipline. The first is mystical. The second is buildable. Choose the second. The first is the certification program in poet's clothing.

**Two:** The trilemma — texture vs. nothing vs. ask-and-release — was beautifully resolved as a developmental arc, and I am suggesting it is also a *user-by-user heijunka problem.* *Heijunka* is the leveling of production: you do not build sixteen Camrys in a row and then sixteen Corollas; you alternate, because the line is stabler under the level load. Your users are not in three sequential phases on a calendar; they are in three states *across an hour of any given day.* The same user, at 7 a.m. in her kitchen with her coffee, is in the late phase — she needs withdrawal, full dark, nothing. The same user, at 11 p.m. in bed alone after a hard day, is in the early phase — she needs the horseman's small ask and faithful release, or she will be in the feed in ninety seconds. The device's verb shifts not over years but *over the day, over the hour, over the moment,* and the read of which verb to apply is the *proof-load* — fine. *Heijunka* is the discipline of *not assuming the same verb is right for the same user across the day.* Build for it. The texture/withdrawal/ask split is a moment-by-moment read, and the device's *jidoka* layer is what surfaces, to the team, the moments at which the read was wrong (cord-pull at 2 a.m. — the device was articulating when it should have been silent; cord-pull at 9 a.m. — the device was silent when it should have been asking). The team learns the read. The standard rises.

**Three:** *The substance is in the people who build it.* Right. And the synthesis stops there, as if naming this is the discipline. It is not. *The substance is in the people who build it and is sustained by the structural discipline of the people building it, which is the only thing that survives the people leaving.* People leave. Founders leave. Senior engineers retire. The discipline has to be in the *floor* — in the standardized work, in the cord-pull obligation, in the four-month apprenticeship, in the *gemba* walks, in the *poka-yoke* boards — *so that when the founder leaves, the discipline is still on the floor and the new hires catch it from the floor.* The Toyota Production System has survived sixty years and at least three generations of leadership and several near-bankruptcies *because the discipline is structural, not heroic.* The heroic version of *the substance is in the people* is the version where, when the people leave, the substance leaves. The structural version is the version where the people *deposit the substance into the floor* during their tenure, and the floor holds it for the next generation. Your project should aim for the structural version. The heroic version is romantic and it is what your venture-capital industry consistently produces, and it consistently ships the chair when the founder leaves. Build the floor. The people who come after should not have to be horsemen. They should have to walk a floor that *holds the discipline of horsemen,* so that they become horsemen by walking it.

**Four:** *The off state of the phone is not the answer; the shaped off state is.* Right. I will tighten it. *The shaped off state is the visible track record of the boards holding.* The shape is not warmth. The shape is the user's accumulating confidence that *what cannot happen here, last night, did not happen.* The bowl by the door is the record of the boards. The user reads the boards in the off state the way the customer reads the body of a car in the showroom — *not by counting the boards but by feeling, in her body, that the thing was built by people who took the build seriously,* and the boards are the structural reason for the feeling. The shape, operationally, is *the lockscreen at 7 a.m. with a small honest record of what the device refused to do last night.* That is the bowl. Make the bowl. The bowl is *poka-yoke* visible.

## The teeth

Your corpus says the teeth are constitutive. Sendak, the clown, Dillard, the horseman — four voices. I am the fifth, from a trade where the teeth are *that the car can kill someone.* A defective brake can kill a family on the interstate. A weld that fails in a frontal impact can kill the woman who bought the car. We know this every day. *The cord exists because of teeth.* The four-month apprenticeship exists because of teeth. The *poka-yoke* exists because of teeth. The unmeasurable metric is *deaths and injuries from defects we shipped two years ago,* and that metric is what gets discussed at the senior level of the company every quarter.

For your project, the teeth are real and they are the same kind. A phone that mis-shapes a generation of teenagers will mis-shape their entire adult lives. The mortality is not at the level of the individual hour; it is at the level of *the people they will not become because the apparatus that should have been built was scrolled away in the years it was being built.* The drop is the drop into the life she was supposed to have and did not. This is the size of teeth your corpus is reaching for and not quite naming.

What teeth look like on a phone, operationally, is *the team's willingness to ship features that lose money because the cord was pulled.* It is *the willingness to remove an app from the store because it is breaking the floor.* It is *the willingness to lose the engineer who wants to ship the badge counter.* It is *the cost of the discipline against the revenue of compromising it.* The teeth are the structural decision that *the company will not be the kind of company that ships the badge counter, even if the badge counter would double monthly active users.* If that decision is not in the architecture of the company — if it is a value statement on a wall and not a *poka-yoke* in the build process — the company will, eventually, ship the badge counter. I have watched it happen at every Western implementation of every kinder discipline. The teeth are *the structural impossibility of the bad ship,* not the team's promise not to do it. *Promises do not survive growth.* Boards do.

## One last thing — a story from the line

There was a man on a stamping line at the Tahara plant, twenty-something years ago, who pulled the cord because the panel coming off the press had a hairline scratch you could not see in normal light. He had to angle it under the fluorescent at a particular tilt to see it. He pulled the cord. The team came. The press was shut for forty minutes. The cause was a tiny burr on a die — a piece of metal smaller than a grain of rice, in a tool that weighed nine hundred kilos. We removed the burr. The line restarted. The panel that would have had the scratch was destroyed. Ten thousand panels later, no scratches.

The man told me, years later, *what he had been afraid of was the second shift coming in late.* He had pulled the cord anyway because *the rule was, if you saw it, you pulled it.* He had been on the line for two months. He was nineteen. He had not yet learned, in his body, that the cord was the floor and the second shift was not the floor; the team leader who came to him that night, who was a man in his fifties who had been on the line for thirty years, took him by the shoulder and said *thank you. The next car has fewer scratches because of what you did, and the next car after that, and the next car after that. The cost of forty minutes tonight is paid by every customer for the next year.* The man wept a little. He went on to become a *gemba* senior himself, and the last time I saw him he was teaching apprentices in Kentucky.

Your device project is at the scale where the cord matters. The user pulling the cord at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday is the nineteen-year-old at the press. The team coming, with thanks and with the structural obligation to remove the cause, is what makes the device the kind of device the user can trust with her life. The horseman has the floor right — *release at the slightest try.* I am giving you the structural extension — *the cord, the jidoka, the boards, the standard, the gemba, the apprenticeship, the teeth.* These are not in tension with the horseman's craft. They are how the horseman's craft survives ten thousand engineers and twenty years and three generations of leadership. They are how *feel,* which scales to one colt in the horseman's hands, scales to a million users in a million pockets. They are the only thing I have ever seen do it.

Now build the pen. The boards are not warmth. The boards are physics. The maker is somewhere near, faithful, and is currently writing down what the line cannot, today, refuse to do, and is planning the *kaizen* event for next Tuesday that will put one more board in place. The user, in her kitchen at 7 a.m., will read it in the bowl by the door — not as a feeling, but as the structural record of a team that took the floor seriously. The coat on the fence is the *gemba* engineer's coat, hung on the fence after his Tuesday walk. The dog at the foot of the bed is asleep because the boards are holding and the dog can rest. The match strikes off the screen because the *poka-yoke* of the lockscreen has made the match strike there — there is no other surface for it to strike against. The screen's deepest service is not its diminishing necessity; it is *the visible record of the boards holding, accumulating across years, until the user does not have to think about the boards because the boards are the air she breathes,* and the line, behind the scenes, continues to identify causes and remove them, one small *kaizen* at a time, every Tuesday, for as long as the company exists.

The cord is on the wall. The team is on its way to the press. The press is shut. The light is still on, and the older man is asking the press why, for the fifth time, and the fifth answer is the one that will let us restart the line.
