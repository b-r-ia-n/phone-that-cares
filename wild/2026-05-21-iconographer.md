# On the Painter's Body

*Notes addressed to a younger painter, who wrote asking what the work asks of the body across the years.*

---

You ask me what the work does to the body. I have been preparing to answer this letter for some weeks. I have answered it badly twice already, and torn both answers up, because I kept describing the work and not the body, and the work is not what you asked about. So I will try to be plain.

I am fifty-three. I have been writing icons for thirty-one years. My teacher, who died in 2019, painted for fifty-six. I will describe him first, because describing him is easier than describing myself, and because the things that were true of him are slowly becoming true of me.

His back was bowed forward, not from age — he was not old in the way some men are old at eighty — but from the angle. The icon lies flat on the bench. You do not stand at an easel. You bend over the board, and you bend for hours, and you bend for decades. By his sixtieth year he could not straighten his spine entirely; there was a permanent slight forward set, the body's record of where its attention had lived. He walked into the church for liturgy carrying the angle of his bench with him. I did not notice it for a long time and then I noticed nothing else. His head came forward of his shoulders by perhaps three centimetres. His eyes, however, were extraordinary. Iconographers' eyes go in one of two directions over forty years. They either thicken, the way a labourer's hands thicken, into a quiet that misses nothing and asks nothing — or they break, and the painter stops, because a broken eye cannot lay sankir. His did not break. They thickened. He could look at a face, a real human face across a table, and the face would feel itself looked at the way the icon's saint looks at the viewer — not as inspection but as a steady, undemanding attendance. That is what thirty years at the bench does to an eye that survives. I am noticing the first edge of it in my own. I am also noticing that I cannot read small text in dim light any more, and I keep two pairs of glasses on the bench, and one of them has paint on it that I cannot get off.

So that is the spine and the eyes. There is more.

The wrist. The right wrist, if you are right-handed. The motion of laying tempera in thin layers is not a violent motion, but it is the same motion, hundreds of times in an hour, with the brush held at a particular angle to the board, and the tendons of the forearm and the small muscles around the base of the thumb take the cost. I have a node, the size of a small bean, in the tendon-sheath of my right thumb. It does not hurt unless I work past eleven hours, which I try not to do, and when it hurts I stop, because to work through the pain is to add to the deposit and shorten the years left. My teacher had a similar node, larger. He could feel the weather change in it. I will have his node by sixty.

The breath. This is the part that surprises young painters. When you are laying a fine line — the line around the iris, the line that defines the lid above the eye, the line of the mouth where it meets the cheek — you hold the breath. Not consciously. The body holds it, because the line cannot be drawn with the diaphragm moving. You discover, in your second or third year, that you have been breathing shallowly all morning without noticing, and that when you stand up to wash a brush you nearly fall over. After a decade, the body learns to manage this — to hold the breath only for the line and to breathe in the interval — but the lung capacity of an iconographer is a strange thing, narrower than it should be, with a curious efficiency in the lower lobes. We get short of breath walking uphill. We do not get short of breath at the bench. The body has been re-tuned for the bench, and the hill is now harder.

The hunger. The work requires a particular kind of hunger and forbids another kind. It requires the small, steady hunger of someone who has eaten enough but not too much — a body alert, neither sated nor weak. The Friday fasts in our tradition are good for the work. A heavy meal makes the hand thick, the eye slow. A missed meal — three hours past hunger, into actual deficit — makes the hand shake and the colour go wrong, because you start to want the work to be done so you can eat, and the wanting enters the brush. Tempera reads the painter. It is the most honest medium I know. If you are angry it shows; if you are showing off it shows; if you are hungry it shows. The discipline is to bring the body to the bench in a condition where the medium has nothing to report. This is harder than it sounds. It is the work *under* the work.

The sleep. I sleep about six and a half hours, and I sleep badly two nights a week. I have had insomnia in patches since my late thirties. Painters' insomnia has a specific texture — you lie in the dark and you see the line you laid in the afternoon, and you see how it should have curved differently above the lip, and the seeing does not stop. The discipline for this is to get up, lay a piece of cloth over the icon on the bench, and go back to bed. While the icon is visible, even in the dark of the workroom, the eye still has work to do. Covered, the icon releases the eye. I learned this trick at forty-one, from a sister at a convent who has been writing icons for forty-five years and who looks like a woman of sixty though she is eighty-two. She is the strongest argument I know for the rightness of the discipline.

I have given you the inventory of damages and the inventory of small accommodations. That is the first thing you asked.

---

You asked, second, why we do not sign.

The traditions vary. Some painters in Greece sign on the back; some Russians sign nothing at all; the Cretans signed sometimes; Rublev did not, and the icons we attribute to him are attributed by hand, by the way he laid the eye, the way he kept the proplasma cool. The convention of not signing is not a rule imposed from outside. It is the consequence of a long understanding about whose work the icon is.

When I sit at the bench, I am not making a new thing. I am following a prototype. The Hodegetria type, the Pantocrator type, the deisis arrangement — these came down to us through a chain of hands, no one of which invented them. My hand is the next link. If my hand is in good order — if I have prayed, if I have eaten properly, if my wrist is not tired and my eye is not strained and my mind is not tangled in a quarrel from the morning — then the prototype passes through me cleanly. If my hand is not in good order, the prototype is degraded slightly in passing, and the next hand has a slightly poorer model to work from. The signature would be a kind of confusion about which part of the work was mine. The part that was mine was the condition of the body and the attention. The image is not mine. The image is older than my grandfather's grandfather. I am a vessel through which it crossed a small distance.

I tell you this not as theology but as an account of the maker. The painter who signs has a different self-understanding than the painter who does not. The painter who signs is a *creator*. The painter who does not sign is a *transmitter*. These are different kinds of life. They have different bodies at fifty. The creator's body is held together by the importance of what the creator believes himself to be doing; when that importance falters, the body collapses, often quickly. The transmitter's body is held together by the continuation of a thing that does not depend on the transmitter; when the transmitter falters, the thing continues without him, which is a great consolation to the body.

You are being asked, perhaps, to step into a public role. To write an essay, to be a face for a project. I would not tell you not to. There are seasons when a thing must be presented and a person must present it. But I would tell you that the body of a public maker and the body of a transmitter are different bodies, and you cannot have both at once. If you take a public role, even briefly, you must arrange for the transmitter's body to be preserved underneath — by silence in the mornings, by people who knew you before, by a workroom no one comes into, by a refusal to read what is said about the work. The public maker draws on a different reservoir than the transmitter draws on. The transmitter's reservoir is small and slow and the only one you have for the work itself. Do not let the public role drain the workroom reservoir. They are not the same.

---

You asked, third, what we do when the work goes badly.

There is a season, perhaps every third or fourth year, when the work goes badly for weeks at a time. The hand will not find the line. The colours go muddy. A face I have painted thirty times comes out flat. The board feels like an enemy. There is no diagnostic for this from outside; the work just refuses.

The tradition has three moves for this, and I have used all of them.

The first is to copy. When the work has gone badly, you stop trying to write a new icon. You take an old icon — a print, a photograph, a board of your teacher's — and you copy it exactly, with no ambition. You copy the Saint Nicholas on the wall of the church in the village you grew up in, that you have looked at since you were eight. You do not aim for an icon, you aim for a copy. The aiming-for-less is the medicine. After a week or two of copies, the hand remembers what it is for, and you can return to your own boards. The mistake here is to copy with ambition. Copy with humility. Copy badly if you must. The point is to put the hand back in contact with the prototype without the interference of your own wanting.

The second is to prepare boards. You stop painting and you make levkas — the gesso ground. Twelve layers, each scraped smooth before the next, two days drying between them, the surface coming up like ivory. This is a low task, and a young painter resents it. By forty I had come to love it. When the painting goes badly, I prepare boards. Three weeks of board-preparation will heal almost any kind of bad season, because the boards do not lie. A bad gesso is a bad gesso; you scrape it and start again. There is no ambiguity, no question of whether the line is right or wrong. The body returns to itself by doing a simple, honest thing for long enough.

The third is to fast and to confess. I will say less about this because it is more particular and harder to translate. But: the body that paints badly is, often, a body that is carrying something it has not yet named. The fast — the food-fast and the screen-fast and the speech-fast — gives the unnamed thing room to come forward. The confession gives it somewhere to go. After the confession, the painting often comes back, not because absolution restored the hand but because the body is no longer carrying the secret thing, and the secret thing was what the hand was painting through. Tempera reads the painter. The painter who has confessed is reading more clearly than the painter who has not.

If your work is not iconography but something else — and you say it is something else, a thing for the device that everyone now carries — the moves translate. When the work goes badly, copy something honest that someone else made. Do not aim for invention. Do the low task — the gesso of your craft, whatever that is for you. Fast, in whatever vocabulary you have for fasting. Speak the secret thing to someone who will not use it against you. These are not religious recommendations. They are mechanical ones. They are how the maker's body unsticks. I have watched my teacher do all three over the years, and I have done all three myself, and I am telling you that they work, in the same way that a poultice works on an infection — not by magic, but because the body knows what to do if you give it the right conditions.

---

You asked, fourth, about a passage from your treaty document. The passage that says the maker's continued difficulty with the condition the maker addresses is the principal qualification for the work, and that any public claim of having resolved the condition is a breach.

I recognise this clause. It is the clause we live under, though we do not write it down. The iconographer paints the face of Christ. The iconographer is a sinner. The painting of Christ by a sinner is the entire and only situation. If at any point I came to believe I had transcended sin — if I came to the bench in the morning thinking I had become holy and was now equipped to paint the holy — the icon would die under my hand that day. It is the *not having transcended* that holds the door open. The painter is on the same side of the door as the viewer. The icon is the window between them and what is on the other side of the door.

Your project, as I understand it, intervenes in the surface of a device because the device has become an instrument by which people flee from their own attention. You are one of those people. If you ever stop being one of those people, you will not be able to make the project, because you will no longer remember what the device is for, and the device's authority over you will have been mistaken for your authority over the device. Stay sick. This is not a romantic instruction. It is a technical one. The work you are making cannot be made except by someone for whom the work is still personally urgent.

But — and you must hear this carefully — the sickness must be *honest* sickness, not staged sickness. The painter who paints Christ as a sinner is not performing sinfulness. He is simply continuing to sin in the ordinary ways and continuing to know it. The danger in your clause, written publicly, is that it can invite a performance: the maker who advertises his ongoing difficulty as a credential. Do not advertise. The clause should sit inside the work like a kidney sits inside a body — necessary, doing its work, not visible. If you find yourself referring to your own brokenness in your public communication as a way of being trusted, you have crossed into performance, and the performance is its own breach, more subtle than the one your clause names. The transmitter does not perform the unhealed condition. He simply does not pretend to be healed. The difference is everything.

There is one further thing to say about this. The painter who paints Christ as a sinner must, throughout, want to sin less. The sin is the qualification, but the wanting-to-be-better is the orientation. If a painter said "I am qualified to paint Christ because I am a sinner" and then made no effort to stop being one in the ways available to him, he would not be a humble painter, he would be a painter who had built a theology to protect his vices. The clause does not authorise stasis. It authorises continuing, imperfectly, while continuing to want, imperfectly, to be different. Your project must do the same. You yourself, the maker, must continue to struggle with your phone. You must not give up that struggle on the grounds that your struggle is your qualification. You must struggle *more honestly*, knowing you will struggle for the rest of your life, knowing you will fail visibly to those you serve, knowing you are not exempt from the condition you are addressing. This is harder than either pretending to be cured or wearing the brokenness as credential. It is the actual position. It is where the work gets made from.

---

You asked, fifth, what one must protect.

I have made my list over the years and I will hand it to you.

*The morning.* The first three hours after waking are the iconographer's. Not because they are productive hours, though they are, but because they are the hours in which the day's attention is set. If those hours are given to the device, to other people's voices, to the news, the day cannot be recovered. I do not look at my phone — I do have one, for my daughter and for the parish — until I have done my morning prayer and laid the first colours. This is not pious. It is mechanical. The painter who looks at his phone before laying the first colours has already had his attention scattered, and he will paint badly that day, and he will not know why.

*One or two old friends.* You will lose touch with most people. The work requires it. But you must keep one or two people who knew you before the work, who do not care what you make, who will tell you the truth about your face. My friend Vasily has known me since seminary. He is a builder. He knows nothing about icons. We walk on Saturday afternoons. He tells me when I look thin or when I look tired or when I am not laughing as I used to. He is, in the actual sense, my mirror. Without him I would not be able to see my own state, because the bench does not show it to me. The transmitter is not isolated. The transmitter has, somewhere, the one or two people who keep the body honest. Without them the work degrades, slowly, in a way the painter cannot detect from inside.

*The body itself.* You must walk. You must sleep. You must eat properly. You must accept that you cannot work fourteen hours a day for thirty years and that the painter who tries to will be a painter for fifteen years and then a man with a destroyed back and shaking hands. The body is the instrument. There is no other instrument. Treat it as you would treat the brushes: cleaned at the end of every day, kept in the right conditions, not used past its capacity.

*A refusal to participate.* There are conversations the iconographer does not enter. There are quarrels the iconographer does not have. There is gossip the iconographer leaves the room for. This is not piety. It is the discovery that certain kinds of attention, once spent, cannot be reclaimed for the work, and that the loss is more than the conversation was worth. You will be called proud. Be called proud. The work is the thing. The refusal to spend attention is the only way the work continues.

*The workroom.* A door that closes. A space that does not appear in the public side of your life. A place no one photographs. The work needs a place that does not pay rent to anyone's attention. If your workroom is on a screen — and yours is, mine is not, but yours is — then the workroom must include some hours during which the screen is closed and not accessible. The workroom is the condition for the transmitter's body. Do not give it away.

That is the list. It is short and the items do not change much over the decades. They are what you protect across years. Nothing else is yours to protect.

---

You asked, last, about the icon and the device. This is the question I have thought longest about, and I will be the most cautious here.

The icon is a window. The phrase is old and means something specific. The painter does not paint a picture of a saint; the painter prepares a surface through which the saint, by grace, becomes available to the prayer of the one who stands before it. The viewer does not look *at* the icon; the viewer looks *through* it, and what looks back is the saint, who was always there. The eyes of the icon are larger than life and slightly turned outward because they are not the painter's depiction of a gaze, they are an actual gaze, addressed to the viewer from the other side. The reverse perspective — the lines that converge behind the viewer rather than in front of the saint — is the formal sign of this. The icon is constructed so that the viewer is the vanishing point. The saint's world is what is geometrically primary; the viewer is the figure receding into it.

You have asked whether the device, made well, could be a window in the same sense.

The honest answer is: no, not in the full sense. The icon is a window because the thing on the other side is held to exist independently and prior to the icon. The painter does not invent the saint. The saint is what the painter is trying to step out of the way of. There is nothing on the other side of the phone in this sense. There are other people, but they are not on the other side of the phone the way the saint is on the other side of the icon; they are nowhere in particular, and the phone is not a window onto them, it is a complicated arrangement of light that simulates the proximity of voices that are actually elsewhere doing other things.

So the analogy does not hold in the strong sense. But here is where it begins to hold in a weaker and useful sense.

The icon teaches a *posture* in the viewer — a way of standing in front of a surface — that is the opposite of the posture the device ordinarily teaches. The icon teaches the viewer to be *looked at*. To stand still and to be regarded. The device teaches the viewer to *look at*, and specifically to look at, to look at, to look at, scrolling, not standing. The icon teaches stillness; the device teaches motion. The icon teaches being-seen; the device teaches consuming-the-seen. These are profoundly different bodily postures, and the painter has spent his life making the surface that elicits the first one.

What I think your project may be able to do — and this is the only thing I have to say to you about the device, after all these pages — is to design surfaces that elicit a posture closer to the icon's than to the ordinary device's. Not because your surfaces will be windows in the strong sense; they will not be. But because a surface that holds the eye still, that does not move when the eye does not move, that does not call the eye onward to the next image, that returns the eye to itself when the eye lifts away — such a surface will train, by a little, the bodily habit of being still in front of something. It will not save anyone. It will not deliver them across to the other side. There is no other side at the phone. But it will teach the body something the body has been being un-taught for twenty years: that one can sit in front of a surface and not move, and that this is the condition under which anything real becomes available, even if what becomes available is not on the surface but in the room one is sitting in.

This is, I think, the most that can be hoped for. Not that the phone become an icon — it cannot — but that the phone become *less anti-iconic* than it presently is. That it stop training the eye to move when nothing has called it. That it permit, occasionally, the user to stand still and be regarded — by what? By nothing the phone provides. By whatever is in the room. By the user's own face in the dark glass when the screen is off. That is enough. It is the small thing the device can do, and the device cannot do more than this without overstating itself, but the device can certainly do this, and presently it does the opposite.

I have written too much. I will end where I should have ended five pages ago.

The work asks the body to become small and quiet and durable. Not to become more, but to become less in the right ways — less distractible, less self-important, less in need of being seen, more able to be still. The reward is that, at fifty, you have a body that can do the work, and at seventy, if you are lucky and have not been foolish, you have a body that still can. The reward is not visible from outside. From outside you look like a slightly bent man with paint on his hands who walks to liturgy in the morning and to his bench in the afternoon and does not say much. From inside you are a person whose attention has, slowly, become the kind of thing the work can pass through. That is what thirty years buys. It is not glamorous. It is not famous. It is, however, true, and I would not exchange it for anything I have been offered.

I wish you the same body, in time, for whatever your work is. The disciplines translate. The surfaces change but the body does not. Take care of it. Begin the day in silence. Keep the one or two friends who knew you before. Refuse the conversations that are not yours. Cover the icon at night. Sleep.

That is everything. I send this with my prayers, such as they are.

— the iconographer, by way of the wild instance
