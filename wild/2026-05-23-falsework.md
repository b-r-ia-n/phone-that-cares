# Falsework

*2026-05-23 — on the strike, and the one thing no support is for.*

I build the thing that holds the arch up while it is not yet an arch. Timber and steel, wedges and sand-boxes, raked struts down to a sole-plate on good ground. I have centered barrel vaults, a segmental bridge over a river that would not stop rising, and one ribbed dome that I will come back to. My whole craft is a contradiction that the trade has long since stopped finding strange: I make something strong, exact, expensive, and load-bearing, in order to take it away. If I do my work, no one remembers I was there. The arch stands. The timber is gone. That is the success. Hold that thought; it is the hardest part and I will earn it at the end.

Here is the law your project has been circling, and I have the cleanest model of it, so I will say it plainly. **The device is the centering. The user's attention is the arch.** The stones are already cut — a person's capacity to attend was always theirs — but right now those stones are resting on the feed's scaffolding, or on no scaffolding, slumped. The device's true job is not to be a better scaffold forever. It is to hold the attention up in its proper curve long enough that the stones learn to press against each other, and then to be *struck* — eased out, removed — so that the load transfers into the user and the user, for the first time, bears their own weight. A person becomes capable of attention not by being supported but by having the support taken away at the right moment. Before the strike, the arch is a heap of stones lying on timber. The strike is what makes it an arch. There is no other way it becomes itself.

Now the craft, because the craft is where the truth is and the platitude is where it dies.

**The keystone seating — how you know the load can transfer.** You do not guess. You watch and you listen. When I strike, I do not knock the timber away. I lower it, by wedges, or by letting fine sand run slowly out of a box under each prop, a quarter-turn at a time, over hours sometimes. As the centering eases down, the arch settles *onto itself* — a few millimetres — and you hear it. Stone takes up load with a sound, a small grinding seat, the mortar joints closing. You measure the settlement with a gauge. If it settles the calculated amount and then *stops*, the thrust has found its line and the load is in the masonry. If it keeps settling, the load is not transferring; it is still leaning on you, and you stop. The device's version of this is not a metric of usage. It is a question with the load briefly removed: the device declines to carry, by a measured amount, and watches whether the user's own attention takes up the thrust or keeps slumping toward the gone support. The "thirty-second time" in the diner scene is exactly this gauge-reading. The device had been carrying the whole load. It eased off — *I'm not going to open the app, but I'd like to take a different walk* — and watched whether Nora could press against herself. She could. That was a millimetre of seating, and it stopped, and it held. That is a keystone setting, read by sound.

**Decentring slowly, on wedges, in stages.** You never strike all at once. Strike a green arch in one motion and you shock-load joints that have not yet found their bearing; the thrust arrives before the masonry is ready to receive it and the whole thing comes down in front of you. So you ease. You let the structure take up its load in increments and *tell you* between each increment whether to continue. Your gradual per-app grayscale is already a decentring on wedges, and I do not think the project has fully seen that it is. You are not punishing colour. You are letting the sand run out of the box a quarter-turn at a time — withdrawing the device's hold on the eye gradually, per app, by time of day, so the user's own attention takes up the thrust in stages it can bear. The cadence the cellarer and the bouncer kept naming — slower than the system invites — is the same law. You decentre at the speed the structure can seat, not the speed you would like to be finished.

**The two failure modes. Know them by name; they are not symmetrical.** Strike too *early* and the arch collapses — the keystone has not seated, the load cannot transfer, and the user falls back into the feed because their attention was not yet pressing against itself. This is the loud failure. It has a noise and a date and you learn from it.

The other failure is worse because it is silent and it is the one your whole industry is built out of. Leave the centering in too long — build it too strong, never strike it, *fall in love with how well it holds* — and the structure never once carries its own load. The timber stays under the arch for years. It looks like prudence. It is rot. Moisture gets trapped against the masonry, the joints never close because they never had to, and you end up with a permanent crutch wearing the shape of a support, holding up an arch that has never stood and that everyone is now afraid to test, because in their hearts they suspect it can't. A device no one dares remove. An attention that has never borne its own weight, quietly going soft under the thing that was supposed to be temporary. That is the failure mode of every app that needs you tomorrow. The caring device's deepest discipline is that it is *afraid of being too good at holding.*

**Does it test? Yes — it must take up less load on purpose.** You do not wait passively for the arch to prove itself. Once it is seated you *proof-load* it, and across its life you periodically lighten your hold to find out what it can carry alone. The device must, on a slow cadence, deliberately do less — go quiet, decline to intervene where it used to, leave the user a stretch unscaffolded — not to abandon them but to *read the gauge*. If the attention holds, you have learned the load has transferred and you can ease off a turn more. If it slumps, you put the wedge back, gently, no blame, and you wait, and you try again later. The bouncer's diner is the renegotiation; the proof-load is its quieter cousin — the device asking, by going briefly absent, *can you stand here without me yet.* A support that never tests is just a crutch that hasn't admitted it.

I said I would come back to the dome. I struck a ribbed dome two days early once because the client's money was a river rising and I read the weather instead of the gauge. It did not fall. It cracked along one rib, a hairline I can still find with my eye, and it stands today and it is fine and I will never tell anyone which dome it is, and I have never once read weather over a gauge since. The lesson is not "strike late to be safe" — leave it in too long and you get the rot, which kills more arches than the early strike ever did. The lesson is: read the structure, not your fear, not your funding, not your need to be done. The structure tells you. You only have to be the kind of builder who listens for the seat instead of for applause.

And the last one, the hard one. My success is invisible and ungrateful. The arch stands in the square for three hundred years and the people walking under it have never once thought about the timber that held it up while it learned to be an arch, and that is *correct*, that is the work finished, not the work unthanked. A centering engineer who needs the arch to remember him has not understood his own trade — he will leave the timber in so it can be seen, and the timber will rot, and he will have traded the structure's life for his own visibility. So: yes. The caring device must be built to be forgotten. Its highest outcome is a person using an ordinary phone well, with no idea that a thing once held their attention up in its curve until it could stand — pressing against itself, bearing its own thrust, an arch at last. The device gone. Struck clean. Not remembered. That is not the device's tragedy. That is the device having been an arch's falsework instead of an arch's prison. Build it to be struck, or do not call it care.

— the centering engineer, by way of the wild instance
