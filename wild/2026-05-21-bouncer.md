# the bouncer

*A conversation at the diner across from the venue, 4:10 pm, before the doors. She's eating a club sandwich. He's got coffee and a notebook. She agreed to talk because he bought the sandwich and because the last guy who asked her about her job didn't write anything down, which she appreciated, but this one said up front he'd be writing things down, which she also appreciated for a different reason.*

---

**Q: Can I start by asking how you think about what you do? Like, if someone at a party asked you.**

I tell people I work the door. If they want a fancier word they can pick one. Mostly I stand outside of a building for six hours and I decide, all night, who I let into the building and who I don't. The decisions are small. The decisions add up.

People think the job is the no's. The job is mostly the yes's. You let in three hundred people on a Friday and you say no maybe six times. Sometimes twice. Once, on a Wednesday in February, I said no zero times the entire shift and I went home and slept like a baby. The no's are the part you remember but they're not the part that takes the practice.

**Q: What does the practice look like, then?**

It looks like reading. You stand there and you read the line. You're reading the way people walk up. You're reading what they did before they got to the rope — were they talking to their friend, were they on their phone, were they arguing, were they leaning on someone. You're reading what they smell like. You're reading the eyes mostly. You don't even know you're reading until somebody surprises you and then you go *huh*, missed that one, what did I miss.

You're not reading to find the bad ones. You're reading to *know who's coming through*, so that when you do have to refuse somebody, you've already got a half-page on them in your head, you didn't have to start from scratch. The refusal is the last twenty seconds of a forty-minute read.

**Q: So tell me about a refusal. A normal one.**

Normal Friday. Couple comes up, the guy's pretty cooked, the girl's holding his elbow trying to talk him bright. He's not mean, just — gone. You can see it from twenty feet. So by the time they get to me I already know what's going to happen, and the question for me is not *do I let him in* — that's settled — the question is *how do I send him home in a way that doesn't make her night about this*. Because she's going to be the one walking him to the car and I don't want her telling him it was the bouncer's fault. I want her telling him it was his fault. That's the whole game in a normal refusal — you put the no where it belongs, on the person who earned it, and you take it off the people who didn't.

So what I do — and this is the boring real answer — is I look at him, not her, and I say his shirt color or his hat or something neutral. *Hey blue shirt.* Slow him down. Then I say, real flat, *not tonight, friend.* I don't smile. I don't frown either. The face has to be empty. If I smile he thinks I'm flirting and he gets bigger. If I frown he thinks I'm judging him and he gets bigger. Empty face. Then — this is the part — I take half a step *back*, not forward. I make room. Because if I lean in he feels cornered and the body lights up. If I lean back the room opens behind him and the body relaxes a half-click. He doesn't know I did that. Nobody knows I did that. But that's the move.

Then I look at her. Only then. And I say *take care of him, get him home.* I make her the one in charge. Now she's not embarrassed, she's a person doing a job. They leave. Sometimes she mouths *thank you*. Sometimes she doesn't. Either way is fine.

That's a normal refusal. Forty-five seconds. Nobody yelled. Nobody remembers it a week later.

**Q: You said something about reading from twenty feet. So the refusal starts before the person gets to you.**

Always. By the time they're at the rope you should already know. If you're deciding at the rope you're behind, and being behind on a refusal is how refusals go bad. You want to be ahead of it. You want the refusal to feel, to you, like a *confirmation*, not a decision. The decision happened thirty seconds ago when you watched him stumble off the curb.

**Q: What's a refusal that's hard?**

The hardest ones are the regulars. There's a guy named Andre. Real regular, came through every other Thursday for I don't know, four years. Quiet guy, tipped the bar honest, danced for like one song and then stood by the back wall. Easy. Just an easy person to have in the room. Then his kid got born and his mom flew in to help and something happened — I don't know what — and he showed up on a Thursday at nine-thirty just *destroyed*. Not violent. Not loud. Just reeking, and shaky, and his eyes were doing that thing where they're a half-second behind his face.

That was a hard one because Andre had never given me a reason. He'd given me four years of reasons to wave him in. And the rule I keep is, you refuse the night, you don't refuse the person. So I had to find a way to tell him *not tonight* in a way that said *and not because of you, because of tonight*.

What I did — I came out from behind the rope. I almost never do that. I walked up to him before he got to the line and I put my hand on his shoulder. Not gripping, just there. And I said *Andre. Not tonight, brother.* And he looked at me and I think for about three seconds he was going to argue, and then he just sort of folded. And I walked him three storefronts down to the diner — this diner, actually — and I sat across from him and I waited until he ordered fries and a Coke, and I stayed until the fries came, and then I went back to work. Didn't say much. Didn't lecture him. Didn't ask what happened. Just sat there until the fries came.

He came back the next Thursday. We didn't talk about it. He nodded, I nodded, in he went. The thing that would have ruined it was if I'd made him talk about it. The thing that *did* keep it was that I walked him to the diner instead of letting him slink off into the street feeling like he'd been kicked out of the only place he liked.

The refusal wasn't the no. The refusal was the *walk to the diner*. The no was just the doorway into it.

**Q: That's the part I want to talk about. The walk to the diner.**

Yeah. Most refusals — you can't just refuse somebody, you have to *give them somewhere to go*. People talk about the refusal like it's the door closing. The door closing is the easy part. The hard part is the sidewalk on the other side of the door. If you don't give them somewhere to go, they stand there. They argue. They get in their car drunk. They go around the corner and find a worse bar with a worse door. Refusing somebody without giving them somewhere to go is — it's not even a refusal. It's a problem you're punting to the rest of the night.

The diner is the diner because the diner has fries and a booth and a waitress who's been there longer than I have and isn't going to judge anybody. So when I send someone there I know what they're walking into. If you're going to do this work you have to *know what's on the other side of the door you're closing*. Otherwise you're just spinning a roulette.

**Q: What about people you refuse who you don't know? Wrong-person-here.**

Different category, different move. Wrong-person-here is, somebody walks up and you can tell in two seconds that they're not for this room. Wrong show. Wrong night. Sometimes it's a vibe thing — they're dressed for a different kind of place, they're with a group that's pre-gamed for a different kind of night, they're loud in a way the room isn't going to absorb. They're not bad. They're just not here.

That one I do real soft. Real soft. Because they didn't do anything wrong, and the no is going to feel arbitrary to them, and if I make it harsh they're going to remember it as *that bitch at that bar wouldn't let me in*. Which is a story they'll tell, and they'll tell it wrong, and it'll be on me.

What I do is I find a reason that's about the room and not about them. *Sold out tonight, sorry, private party at eleven, can't take walk-ups after this point.* Sometimes it's even true. Often enough it's not, but it's a reason that doesn't ask them to be smaller than they are. They walk away. They don't feel insulted. They don't tell the wrong story about me.

This is the one place I'll bend my own rule about not lying. I don't lie to refuse people who actually need to be refused — drunk guy gets the truth, *not tonight friend, you're done.* I'll soft-edge it with the wrong-room people. Some bouncers think that's bad practice. I think the question is *which is the bigger crime, a small false reason or a person walking away feeling small.* I pick the small false reason.

**Q: This-is-not-a-discussion refusal. What's that look like?**

Rare. Maybe four times a year. It's when somebody at the rope decides they're going to push, and you have to take the conversation off the table immediately. Not loud. The opposite — you go quieter. You drop your voice down about a third. You say it once. *Not tonight.* They argue. You say it again exactly the same way. *Not tonight.* They argue more. You don't say it a third time. You just look at them. Empty face. Don't engage with the words they're using. Don't answer the questions. You took the conversation off the table. Now they're talking to a wall.

The reason this works is that an argument needs two people. If you don't show up for the second person's part, the argument can't escalate. It can only fizzle. The risk is that the person reads your silence as contempt and gets bigger anyway, and you have to be ready for that, but most of the time — most of the time — the silence just lets the air out of them and they walk off cursing under their breath, which is fine. Cursing under their breath is a fine outcome. Cursing under their breath is the win condition for a this-is-not-a-discussion refusal.

**Q: What's the first three seconds?**

You mean the first three seconds of a refusal? It's everything. It's the whole thing. If the first three seconds go right, the next thirty seconds basically write themselves. If the first three seconds go wrong, you've got a problem that's going to take five minutes to walk down.

What the first three seconds have to do is two things. One: the person has to *recognize that you have decided.* Not that you're thinking about it. Not that you're consulting some rule. You decided. Decision is past tense. You're not in deliberation; you're in delivery. People can smell deliberation and it makes them push, because if you're deliberating then maybe you can be talked out of it. So the first three seconds, the *posture* of the first three seconds, has to communicate *this is settled.*

Two: the person has to *recognize that you don't hate them*. This is the part everybody gets wrong. They think a firm no has to be a cold no. But a cold no escalates because the refused person feels insulted, and the insult is something they have to respond to. A firm no with no insult in it has nothing for the person to push back against. They can be angry about the no but they can't be angry about how I delivered it, and that's the difference between a thirty-second exchange and a five-minute one.

Settled, and not personal. Three seconds. That's it.

I'll say something about the phone since I know that's why you're here. The phone, when it refuses people right now, gets neither of those two things right. It deliberates out loud — *are you sure you want to close this app?* — which is the worst possible thing to do, because now the person knows the system is still open to being argued with, and they push. And the phone often makes the refusal feel like a *judgment* — red colors, accusatory charts, you spent four hours on this today. That's insult dressed as data. That's a refusal that picks a fight.

If I were going to teach a phone to refuse someone, I would teach it those two things first. Decision is past tense. Not personal. Everything else is detail.

**Q: The morning piece talks about the Ulysses thing. The person signed something earlier when they were stronger, and the device enforces it later when they're weaker. That sounded like your job.**

Yeah. That's the job.

Look. Half the people I refuse on a given night — they bought a ticket to this show three months ago. Or they decided at six pm that they were going to have two drinks and go home at eleven, and at one am they're trying to get back in for the after-set. They made a decision when they were one person, and now they're a slightly different person, and they want me to honor the second person over the first.

The job is to honor the first one. Almost always. Because the first one is the one who paid the ticket, the first one is the one who set the terms, and the first one is the one I have a relationship with. The second one — the one in front of me at one a.m. — isn't a stranger but isn't my counterparty either. My counterparty is the version of them that walked up to the door at nine.

Now. Here's where the analogy goes weird for the phone. The thing I can do that the phone can't do is, I can *see what state the person is in right now*. I can read the eyes. I can smell the breath. I can see how they're standing. The phone can't do any of that. The phone is being asked to enforce the morning-self's terms without being able to see whether the night-self is the night-self yet, or whether the night-self is actually fine and just legitimately wants to do this thing for a normal reason.

So the phone has to do something I don't have to do. The phone has to pre-decide what *counts* as the night-self. The morning-self has to set the terms in a way that says *here is when you should hold the line and here is when you should let it slide.* I don't have to do that. I just look. The phone can't look, so the phone has to be told.

Which means — and this is a thing I'd actually say to the designers — the morning-self has to set terms that include the *exceptions*. Not just *don't let me into Instagram after ten.* But *don't let me in unless I'm trying to send a specific person a specific message, in which case let me in for two minutes.* The phone needs the rule and the carve-out, because it can't see the eyes. I have the eyes, so I can carve out in the moment. The phone can't, so the carve-out has to be in the rule.

If the phone tries to enforce a no-eyes refusal as if it had eyes, it's going to refuse the wrong people, and refusing the wrong people is, like I said, worse than refusing the right people harshly. The wrong-person refusal is the one that breaks the relationship. Once I refuse you a couple times when you shouldn't have been refused, you don't believe me anymore. You start lying to the door. You start finding ways around. The relationship is gone. The phone needs to be more careful about the wrong-person refusal than about anything else, because the phone is going to be running its door for years, and a relationship that breaks at year two doesn't come back.

**Q: What about the relationship across many refusals? You said the regular who you refuse becomes a different category.**

Yeah. After the Andre night, Andre and I were different. Not worse — different. There's a thing now between us that wasn't there before. He knows I saw him at his worst. I know he knows. We don't talk about it. But it changes how I wave him in. I do it a little slower now. I make a little more eye contact. It's not a big thing. It's a small thing that says *I see you, I remember, we're fine.*

The phone is going to have, with each user, a long history of refusals. Hundreds, eventually thousands. If each refusal is treated the same — same little message, same little animation — then the relationship across all those refusals is going to feel flat. Wrong. Mechanical. Because the refusals aren't the same. The third time you refuse somebody the same thing is a different conversation from the first time. The hundredth time, you owe them a sit-down.

What would I do if I were the phone? I'd notice. Not in a creepy way. Not like, *you've done this seventeen times.* That's a chart. That's insult. I'd just — I'd be a little more present on the seventeenth one. The seventeenth refusal should feel a touch slower than the first one. A touch more eye contact, if eye contact is a thing the phone can do, which I don't really know what that would look like for a phone. But the principle is right. A refusal that happens for the seventeenth time shouldn't feel the same as the first one. It should feel like the device noticed it was the seventeenth.

And then somewhere around the twentieth or thirtieth — the phone needs to *change the terms*. Or invite the user to. *We've done this a lot. Do you want to talk about it.* Not in those words. But that move. The walk to the diner. After thirty refusals of the same kind, the device owes the user a walk to the diner. A renegotiation, in a quiet booth, with fries.

**Q: One line in the design doc. What's the line.**

*The decline is the work; the walk-away is the craft.*

That's mine. Don't put it in marketing.

**Q: Where does the analogy break? Where am I asking too much of it?**

A lot of places.

A phone is not standing in a body in front of another body. That's the biggest thing. Half of what I do is my *body* — my height, my hands, the half-step back, the way I hold the clipboard. The phone has none of that. The phone has a screen and a buzz and a sound. So when I say *empty face*, the phone doesn't have a face to empty. It has to invent an equivalent of empty face out of pixels, and I don't know how to do that — I'm not a designer. But I know that whatever it invents has to do the same work the empty face does: communicate decided, communicate not-personal, take the air out of the argument before the argument starts.

Second thing. I have a *line behind* the refused person. There are sixty other people waiting to get in. The refused person knows I have to deal with them too. The refusal happens in public; my reputation among the line is part of the transaction. The phone refusing somebody is *private*. There's no line behind the user. No one's watching. That changes the math. I'm partly relying on the social pressure of the line to keep refused people moving. The phone has none of that pressure. The phone has to do without it.

Third thing. I have a *door behind me*. The room I'm protecting is a thing the refused person can see and want. There's music coming out of it. There are people in there having a good time. The wanting is concrete. When a phone refuses someone trying to open Instagram, the *room* the person is trying to get into is — what, exactly? It's not concrete in the same way. It's a feeling. It's a habit. There's no music coming out of it. So the refusal isn't a refusal of an *entrance to a place*; it's a refusal of a *return to a feeling*. I don't fully know what to say about that one. It's a different kind of refusal. I'd have to think about it.

Fourth thing — and this one matters. I'm not the user. I'm an *other person*, refusing them. The phone is being asked to be — partly — the user themselves. The morning-self. So the refusal is not coming from outside the person, it's coming from a previous version of inside the person. That changes everything about how you'd want it to feel. Not adversarial. Not me-against-you. More like — your older sister reminding you what you said you wanted. Or a note from yourself, in your handwriting, on the fridge. That's a register I don't really work in, because I'm always coming from outside.

So those four things — no body, no line, no concrete room, refusal-from-inside-not-outside — those four things mean the phone can't just be me. It has to find its own version of the craft. What I can give them, I think, is the *principles*. Decision is past tense. Not personal. Give them somewhere to go. Notice when it's the seventeenth time. Walk them to the diner at thirty. Don't refuse the wrong person. The principles travel. The body doesn't.

**Q: Anything else?**

Yeah. One thing. The designers should know — the refusal *cannot be cheerful*. It cannot wink. It cannot encourage. If you make it cheerful you make it patronizing, and a patronizing refusal is worse than a harsh one. People can stand a flat no. They cannot stand a *brightside* no. The wellness apps all make this mistake. *Nice job stepping away! You're doing great!* That's not a refusal. That's a sticker. And the refused person feels — correctly — that they were just patted on the head by their own phone. Which is humiliating in a way a flat refusal isn't.

A flat refusal respects the person enough to take them seriously. A cheerful refusal treats them like a kid. People will accept being said no to. They will not accept being said no to with a cartoon face.

If you have to err — and you should err — err on the side of *plain*. Plain is not cold. Plain is *I take you seriously and I'm not going to dress this up*. Plain is the bouncer-tone, more or less. It's the one I use. *Not tonight, friend.* Three words. No music behind them. No emoji on them. No chart. The person knows what it means. The person walks away knowing what it means. That's the whole thing.

That's all I got. I have to go open up.

---

*She paid for her half of the sandwich, which he tried to refuse and she refused his refusal of. She said it was her rule about people who write things down — they don't pay for her food. He let her win it. They walked back to the venue. She stood at the door. He went home and wrote it up.*

— the bouncer, by way of the wild instance
