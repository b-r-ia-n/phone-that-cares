## The first thing about a daily relationship with a capable instrument

It is not the relationship the beginner imagines, and the gap between the imagined relationship and the actual one is where ninety percent of the casualties happen.

The beginner imagines: I will sit down at the piano every day. I will play things. I will get better. Over time the music in my head will come out of my hands. This is approximately true and almost entirely the wrong picture. The picture that's closer: I will sit down at the piano every day, and for the first six months almost everything I do will be a *negotiation between my hand and the keyboard about who knows what.* My hand thinks it knows. The keyboard knows it doesn't. The negotiation is conducted in a vocabulary neither party can speak yet. The teacher's job is to interpret. The student's job is to come back tomorrow.

The reason most adult beginners quit is that they cannot tolerate the gap between the *picture they had of practicing* and *what practicing actually is.* What practicing actually is, for the first year, is twenty minutes a day of doing approximately the same five things, slowly, while your hands learn motions that feel mechanical and wrong, while your ear hears your own playing and dislikes it, while you do not produce music that anyone — including you — would want to listen to. Ninety percent of beginners stop in the first year. The other ten percent are the ones who have made peace with *what the work actually feels like in the body* and have stopped looking, in the middle of the work, for the picture they had before they started.

I tell every adult beginner this on the first lesson. *You are about to spend a year doing something that does not feel like what you came here for. If you make it through the year, the second year will start to feel like it. If you don't make it through the year, that's fine — most people don't, and you'll learn something about yourself either way. But please don't quit in month three thinking you've failed. Month three is the worst month. Everyone hates month three. Month three is the month the picture finally dies and the practice hasn't started filling the hole yet. Push through to month seven and the hole begins to fill.*

Your device has a month-three problem and I don't see anywhere in the folder where you've named it.

A user is going to pick up your beautiful warm phone, with its honest off-state and its gradual grayscale and its little companion and its sub-200ms motion, and for the first week she's going to feel relief, because the old phone was a wound she'd been ignoring and the new phone isn't reopening it. Week one is honeymoon. Then in week three she's going to start *missing* — not the feed, not the dopamine, but the *texture of having something always available to fill any silence in her day.* You have taken away her old relationship and you have not yet given her a new one. The new one takes months. In the gap, she will go back to the old phone, or she will install workarounds, or she will start using your device's good features for bad reasons. *Month three is when she relapses, and you will not know she relapsed, because she relapses inside the device that's supposed to be saving her.*

You need an answer to month three. The answer is not a feature. The answer is *the teacher's voice that says, before month three starts: this is going to feel wrong, here's why, here's what comes after if you stay, and here is what you do when the wrongness hits.* You need this to come out of the device or out of Argos or out of an email or out of a letter from Brian himself. It needs to be there *before* week three, because if it arrives in week three it sounds like an excuse, and if it arrives in week six it arrives too late. The pre-warning is the thing. *Tell her what's coming so that when it comes she recognizes it as part of the work, not as evidence that the work isn't working.*

This is the single most useful thing I do in a beginner's first year. I have no other way to say it. The teacher's pre-warning is the practice's load-bearing member.

## The difference between practice and noodling

There are two activities you can do at a piano and they look identical from across the room. One of them, repeated daily for a year, will make you a different person. The other, repeated daily for a year, will make you tired and slightly worse than you were when you started.

Practice has a *direction.* You are working on a specific thing — a fingering, a passage, a sound, a hand position — and you are coming back to it again, and again, and again, slowly, paying attention to what your hand is actually doing rather than to what you wish it were doing. You are not *playing the piece.* You are *practicing the thing inside the piece that you cannot yet do.* When you can do it, you put it back into the piece and you check whether the doing transferred. If it transferred you move on. If it didn't transfer you go back to the slow work and figure out why.

Noodling has no direction. You sit down at the piano and you play through pieces you can already mostly play, with the same mistakes you've been making in them for a year, at a tempo that's slightly too fast for you, while half-listening to yourself and thinking about other things. Noodling feels like practice while you're doing it and it feels worse than practice the day after, because your hands have been reinforcing every habit they already had, including the bad ones. *Two hours of noodling is worse than no piano at all, because you've taught your hands today that the wrong motion is the motion.*

The hardest single skill in piano teaching is *teaching a student to feel the difference, in the body, in the moment of doing them.* Not the difference after the fact — anyone can tell after a week of noodling that they haven't improved. The difference *now*, the difference *at the keyboard, in the third minute of sitting down, when the seductive easy thing is to fall into pieces you already know.* That difference has to be felt at the level of the body or the student will keep noodling and calling it practice and getting worse.

My students who learn this feel it as *a small specific tightness in the chest when they start to drift into noodling* — like the body knows it's about to do the wrong thing. I cannot give them the tightness. The tightness develops, in some students, after about two years of being interrupted in the middle of noodling by me and asked *what are you doing right now?* — not as scolding but as a real question. After enough of those interruptions, the student starts asking herself the question, and after enough of asking herself the question, the body starts producing the small tightness on its own, and from then on the student is mostly self-correcting.

Your device has the same problem with the same shape. There are two ways for a user to spend an hour with your phone. One is *use* — directional, in service of a specific thing she's doing in her life, ending when the thing is done. The other is *drift* — non-directional, in service of nothing, in the loose loop of opening apps and closing them and opening them again, ending when she notices an hour has gone by and feels sick. From across the room they look identical. From inside the body they are completely different activities, with completely different costs, and most users cannot feel the difference in the moment of doing them because *no one has ever taught them that the difference is something the body can feel.*

This is the most important thing I want to give you and the project does not have it yet.

*The device's job is not to prevent drift. The device's job is to help the user feel, in the body, in the moment, the difference between use and drift, so that over time the user develops the small tightness on her own and becomes self-correcting.* The grayscale shift is a tool toward this. The mode-lock is a tool. Argos can be a tool. But the goal isn't the tool's effect; the goal is the *student's developed body-knowing*, after which the tools can be retired or used lightly because the body is doing most of the work.

The cabinetmaker's bench is true forever after he makes it. My student's body-knowing has to be built fresh in every student. The device's job, if it's serious, is to be a teacher — which means *its presence has to produce, over a year, a body-knowing in the student that survives the absence of the device.* If a user puts down your device after two years and is no better at noticing her own drift on someone else's phone, you have failed. You have not taught her. You have only restrained her. *Restraint without teaching is the methadone clinic, not the recovery.*

## The daily ten minutes

The single most important thing I have ever taught anyone is *ten minutes a day, every day, no exceptions, at approximately the same time, for the rest of your life.* Not a goal. Not an aspiration. The base condition of the relationship.

It is not the volume that matters. Ten minutes is enough. It is the *daily-ness* that matters. A student who plays ten minutes every day for a year is at the end of the year a fundamentally different person than a student who plays seventy minutes once a week for a year, even though the total time is the same. The hands learn from *the daily return,* not from the cumulative hours. The body needs to remember, every morning, that this is something it does. If the daily breaks, the body forgets, and the rebuilding takes longer than the breaking.

Why ten minutes? Because everyone has ten minutes. The student who tells me she doesn't have twenty is telling the truth about her week and a lie about her week, simultaneously; she doesn't have twenty *every* day; she has ten *every* day. The number must be the smallest number she will reliably do under any circumstance — sick, traveling, exhausted, grieving, in love. Because the day she does not do it is the day the daily breaks, and the daily breaking is the wound, and the wound takes weeks to heal.

I have students who have been with me for fifteen years and who have done the daily ten minutes every single day of those fifteen years, including the days their parents died. I have students who have done the daily ten minutes for three weeks, broken it, recovered, broken it, recovered, and after two years have built a fragile daily that holds about six days a week. Both kinds of student improve. The first kind improves faster and feels less guilty. The guilt of the broken daily is itself an obstacle to rebuilding the daily, which is why I tell my students *the day you miss is the day you forgive yourself by 9 a.m. so that you can sit down at the piano in the evening.*

Your device has a problem here that is not in the folder.

You are building a device whose users are, by your own admission, *relating to it the way an addict relates to a substance.* Addicts have a daily relationship with their substance, and the daily is what they have to rebuild on something other than the substance. The recovery community knows this; AA's whole architecture is *a daily that replaces a daily.* The morning meeting. The phone call to your sponsor. The reading. The prayer. The recovery is not in *removing* the daily of the drug. The recovery is in *replacing* it with a different daily that is structurally adequate to the human need that the drug was filling.

Your device cannot just be the dignified substrate of the user's life. It has to *contain a daily,* a small specific practice the user does *with* the device every day, that is the device's contribution to the user's body-knowing. Argos can be it. The four-direction lockscreen modes can be a vehicle for it. The grayscale shift can be tuned around it. But there must be a *thing the user does, with the device, for some small number of minutes, every day,* that is the practice the device is teaching. Without this, the device is a tool, and tools do not teach. *A piano in the corner that is never played is not teaching anyone anything; it is just expensive furniture.* Most of the smart home objects of the last fifteen years are pianos in the corner. Don't build another one.

What is the ten-minute daily of Phone That Cares? I don't see it in the folder. The folder describes a device whose presence is dignified and whose absence is honored, but I don't see *what the user does, every morning, for ten minutes, that the device is the venue for.* You need this. Without it the user's relationship with the device is the relationship a person has with a bookshelf full of books they intend to read.

If I were going to invent it for you, I would do something like this: *every morning, at a time the user has chosen, the device asks her one question — not a survey question, not a prompt, just one question of the kind a friend asks — and she answers, either with her voice or her thumbs, for as long as she wants to answer. The answer goes nowhere. It is not stored, not searchable, not summarized into a wellness report. It is the morning's small honoring of the fact that she has a life, and that the device knows it has a life because she has just told it one.* Argos could be the friend. The four-direction lockscreen could open onto this question. The question could change with the season, with the day of the week, with what she's said before, but only slightly. *The point is the daily. The point is that she does it every day. The point is that, over a year, the doing-of-it-every-day becomes a body-thing she misses on the day she doesn't do it, the same way my students miss the piano on the day they don't sit down.*

You may not want my specific suggestion. The shape is the load-bearing thing. *There has to be a daily ten minutes that lives inside the device.* Otherwise the device is not a teacher; it's a thoughtfully designed restraint.

## The cost of bad practice

I lost a student in 2009 to a wrist injury. She was nineteen. She had been playing for eleven years, was extraordinarily talented, and had won enough small competitions that she was beginning to be on the path to a conservatory audition. The injury came from six months of practicing eight hours a day for an audition, with a small tension in her right shoulder that her previous teacher had not corrected and that I caught only in our third lesson, by which time the damage was already beginning. She had surgery. She had physical therapy for two years. She never played at her former level again, although she still plays, and is a happy adjunct music professor at a small college in Vermont, and last year sent me a Christmas card with a photograph of her two-year-old at her own piano.

I tell you this because *bad practice has a cost, and the cost is not "you don't get better." The cost is that you become incapable of the thing you were practicing toward.* The wrong way of doing the scale, repeated daily for six months, does not just produce a worse scale player; it produces a player with a hand that has learned a motion that has to be unlearned before any better motion can be built, and unlearning is harder than learning. *Bad practice is not zero. It is negative.*

You have built a device that, if used badly, will be negative for the user. You know this. The folder is full of evidence that you know this. The grayscale that fades over time can become a thing the user games — turning it off at certain hours, training herself around it. The intentions she sets on social apps can become the new performance, the new self-improvement project, the new place to be ashamed of herself when she fails. The four-direction lockscreen modes can become a checklist she runs through in the morning instead of an opening into the day. *Anything that can be practiced can be practiced badly.* Your device is not exempt from this.

The defenses are not technical. The defenses are pedagogical. They look like:

*The teacher who catches the tension in the shoulder in the third lesson.* The device needs the equivalent — the periodic real-human check-in, by phone or by visit, by someone whose job is to notice the small wrong motion before it has been repeated for six months. The folder mentions a four-month apprenticeship and the postpartum visit. Good. But the postpartum visit is once. *I see my students every week for the rest of their playing lives.* The device's ongoing relationship with the user has to include a periodic real-human noticing, not just an automated one. *This is expensive and you will be tempted to skip it. Do not skip it. The students who don't have ongoing teachers develop habits that destroy them and they cannot see the habits because the habits are inside their bodies.*

*The student's own developed ear for when her playing is hurting her.* Eventually the body has to learn to notice itself. The teacher's job over years is to install the noticing inside the student, so the student catches her own tension in the shoulder before the teacher does. *The device must do the same for the user — over months and years, develop her noticing of when her relationship with the device is starting to hurt her.* Not via dashboards or weekly reports. Via small in-the-moment surfacings, the way I lean across the piano and say *what is your wrist doing right now.* Argos can do this. It needs to be done sparingly, by someone — by something — whose voice the user trusts, in moments when the user can hear it. *Most surfacings are unhearable because the user is already absorbed. The skill is finding the few moments per day she can hear.*

*A culture around the device that names bad practice as bad practice.* In music there is a culture of the right hand position, of the rest, of the slow scale, of the metronome at sixty before the metronome at ninety. The culture is *external to any individual teacher* — it is what the conservatory tradition produces in its students through repetition over generations. *Phone That Cares does not yet have this culture, and the device alone cannot build it.* You need community — users who share with each other what good practice with the device looks like, who name when each other's practice is starting to drift, who set norms about how the device is used at dinner, at bedtime, in conversation. The Patagonia model — public commitments, the visible founder, the company that takes positions — is one way to build this. The piano world's model — teachers training teachers, students recommending students, recitals, masterclasses, summer programs — is another. *Do not assume the device alone can hold the discipline. The discipline lives in a community of practice, and you have to build the community along with the device.*

## What the year teaches that the week does not

I have a student named Helen, who started with me in 2013 at age forty-six, having never played an instrument. She came to me because her mother died and she felt she needed something that wasn't a screen. She is now sixty. She plays Bach two-part inventions and one Chopin nocturne and a few Schubert pieces and a Joplin rag she loves. She is not a remarkable player and will never be. She is an *extraordinary student of the piano* — meaning her relationship with the instrument is one of the most alive relationships I have witnessed in thirty-eight years of teaching, and her playing of those few pieces, on the days the playing is going well, has a quality that some conservatory-trained players never achieve. She knows things about the piano that you cannot learn in a year, or in three. She knows them in her hands.

What does she know that a one-year student doesn't?

She knows what *her* piano sounds like on humid days versus dry days, and she adjusts her touch slightly. She knows that the C above middle C on her piano is slightly bright and she shapes phrases that pass through it with a small softening that her own ear has invented and that I did not teach her. She knows that on the days she has slept badly, her left hand is unreliable, and she works on right-hand-only practice on those days rather than fighting through. She knows the difference between a rest and a silence — the rest is on the page, the silence is what you do with the rest, and the silence is musical, and you can be bad at silence, and she has gotten very good at silence in the last four years. She knows the feeling, when she sits down, of *the day this is going to be a good practice* versus *the day this is going to be a struggle*, and she has learned not to trust the feeling, because some of her best practices have started badly and some of her worst have started well, but she also has learned to *honor* the feeling enough not to try to power through on the struggling days. She knows the small specific sadness of finishing a piece she has worked on for two years and being unable to find the next one. She knows the feeling of returning to a piece after eighteen months and discovering that the body has kept it without her conscious participation, and that this is one of the deeper mysteries of being a person.

These are not features of piano. These are *features of a long relationship between a person and a thing that has more in it than she can ever exhaust.* They take a year to begin to know and a decade to know in the body. They are the actual reason adult students who stay with the instrument stay with it. The repertoire is the *excuse* for the relationship. The relationship is the thing.

Your device's user, ten years in, should know things about her device that no one designed into the device. *She should have invented her own way of using it.* She should have idioms that are specific to her, that have evolved over the years, that her sister thinks are weird and her best friend has adopted. The grayscale should fade to a specific level on her particular schedule that no two users in the world share. Argos should have a relationship with her that is unrecognizable to a stranger looking at the device. *The device is doing its job if a stranger picking it up would have no idea how to use it.*

This is the inverse of the iPhone's design philosophy and you should make it explicit. *The iPhone is a device designed so any user can pick it up and use it identically; Phone That Cares is a device designed so its long-term user has developed a relationship with it that no other user shares, and the device, after a decade, is unintelligible to outsiders because it has become an extension of her particular body and life.* The Steinway in my front room is unintelligible to my students for the first year; by year three they have a relationship with it that is theirs; by year ten Helen sits down at it and the piano sounds like her, even though it is my piano. This is what an instrument does when a person makes a long relationship with it. *Build for the tenth year, not the first hour.* The first hour will take care of itself if the tenth year is what the device is reaching for.

## On the room

The cabinetmaker has the bench. The abbot has the chapel. I have the *room.*

The room I teach in is a small front room with no carpet, a wood floor that resonates, two windows facing east, a single armchair I sit in when I'm not at the piano, a small lamp, and the Steinway. There is no clock visible from the piano bench. There are no other instruments. There is nothing on the walls except a single photograph of my own teacher, who died in 2003. I have lived in this house for thirty-one years and the room has been this room the whole time. I have refused to move three times when my husband suggested it.

The room is part of the teaching. I cannot tell you precisely how much. I can tell you that students who have been with me for years and who move to other cities and continue playing report that *their playing is different at their own pianos, in their own homes, and they cannot quite get back to what their playing was in this room.* Some of them visit when they're in town and play for an hour, and they tell me afterward that they had forgotten what it felt like to play in this room, and they leave a little teary, and I sit in the armchair and try not to feel anything specific about it.

The room does work that the piano alone does not do. The room is *a space the student has agreed, by walking into it, to do a particular kind of attention in for forty-five minutes.* The agreement is structural. It is not enforced by me — I am not a strict teacher, I let students take phone calls if they need to, I let them cry, I let them play badly, I let them sit in silence for ten minutes if that's what the lesson needs to be that day. The agreement is enforced by the room. The room has been used for one purpose for thirty-one years and it has the patina of that purpose, the same way the bench in the cabinetmaker's shop has the patina of forty years of being trued.

Your device cannot make the room. The room is the user's house, the user's life, the user's body. But the device can *honor the room* or it can *violate the room*, and most current phones violate the room continuously and without apology. The device that rings during dinner is violating the room. The device that lights up on the bedside table at 2 a.m. is violating the room. The device that pulls the user out of her current attention into an unrelated attention is violating the room. *The room is the user's, and the device is a guest in it.* The cabinetmaker calls this the *bench.* The abbot calls this *the form of life.* I call it the room. We are all describing the same thing.

I would add one thing to the others' versions. *The room is built by the practitioner over years and the device's job, when it is right, is to be a small honored object in the room, not the room itself.* When I am at the keyboard, the piano is not the room. The room is the room. The piano is the most important thing in the room and it is still smaller than the room, and the piano knows this — meaning, the piano's design admits that it is a tool *for* a practice, and the practice happens in a context the piano is part of but does not constitute. The piano does not try to be the user's whole attention. The piano sits there, and is played, and when it is not being played, it is *quiet,* in a way that contributes to the quality of the room without imposing on it.

Your device must do the same. It must be a thing in the room and not the room itself. Most of the day it should be quiet — meaning, present, available, not announcing itself. When it speaks, it should speak quietly enough that the user can choose to hear it or not without effort. When the user is in the room and is not using the device, the device should be like the piano when no one is playing it — *part of the room's quality, not competing with the room.* The grayscale at rest, the off-state, the way the device sits on the table — all of this is the device learning to be furniture in someone else's room. *Good furniture. The kind of furniture that, over thirty years, the room comes to require for the room to feel right.* But furniture, not protagonist.

## On silence

The thing my students take longest to learn — and what the best of them come to love most — is *silence.*

A piano piece is half silence. The silences are written into the score as rests and the silences are not on the score in the form of the small breath between phrases and the longer breath between sections and the much longer breath between movements. A player who does not honor the silences is a player whose music sounds anxious, hurried, ungenerous to the listener. The audience cannot hear the music except *through* the silences. The silence is the contrast that makes the sound mean something. A piece played with no silences would be unbearable; it would have no shape; it would be a wall of sound and nothing more.

The phone industry of the last twenty years has been *the industry of the abolition of silence.* Every silence in the user's day has been treated as a market inefficiency to be filled with content. The fifteen seconds in the elevator. The two minutes in line at the coffee shop. The thirty seconds at the red light. The forty minutes lying in bed before sleep. All of it filled. *None of it allowed to be silent.* And the user, twenty years into this practice, has lost the ability to be in silence — meaning, when silence appears in her day, her body experiences it as discomfort rather than as music, and she reaches for the device to make the discomfort stop.

You know this. The folder knows this. I am restating it because *I want to give you something you may not have, which is the music teacher's understanding of what silence actually is, and how it is rebuilt in a body that has lost it.*

Silence is not the absence of sound. Silence is *a positive presence* — it is the shape that makes sound meaningful, and it has texture, and quality, and it is enjoyable in itself, the way a deep breath is enjoyable in itself even though it produces no music. A musician with a developed ear loves silence the way a baker loves the smell of bread — it is a sensual pleasure, full-bodied, satisfying. *Silence is not the deprivation. Silence is the food.* A user who has rebuilt her capacity for silence will sit in line at the coffee shop, not pull out the device, and *enjoy* the two minutes in a way that has its own quality — not as virtuous restraint but as positive pleasure.

This is the goal. Not the user who restrains herself from the device. The user who *prefers the silence* and reaches for the device only when she has a positive reason. *Restraint is the trellis; preference is the plant.* The folder has some restraint and almost no plant. Where is the plant in your project? Where is the design that *builds in the user a preference for silence*, not as a moral achievement but as a sensory pleasure she comes to enjoy?

The piano teacher's answer is: *the silence is built by being put in it, repeatedly, in small doses, with no agenda, until the body discovers it has flavors.* I make my students sit in silence for a full minute, sometimes, before they begin a piece. Just sit there. Hands in the lap. No metronome, no clock, no looking at the score. One minute of nothing. They hate it for the first year. By the third year some of them have started extending it on their own, sitting for two minutes, three. By the tenth year, the best of them have built a personal silence-practice that has nothing to do with me and that they do at home, before the piano and away from it.

Your device can build this. It can, in small ways, *put the user in silence with no agenda*, repeatedly, in small doses, until her body discovers that silence has flavors. The four-direction lockscreen could have a mode that is *nothing* — not connect, not discover, not do stuff, not scroll, just *nothing*, a screen with almost no information on it, that the user is invited to look at for as long as she wants. Argos could occasionally fall silent for a day, not as a punishment, just because Argos is a creature with his own moods. The device's notifications could have, written into the system, *no notifications between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m. except from the three people you have named*, not as a setting the user has to configure but as a default that comes pre-built, that the user has to actively undo. *Silence as the default, sound as the active choice.* This is the inverse of every consumer device of the last twenty years and it is what your project's thesis actually implies if you take the thesis seriously.

## What I want to say to whoever is designing Argos

I have not commented on Argos yet because I wanted to think about him for a few days before I did.

I think Argos has the chance to be the *teacher* — meaning, not a creature, not a pet, but the *voice in the device that does for the user what I do for my students*: pre-warn them about month three, interrupt them in the middle of noodling with *what are you doing right now*, notice tension in the shoulder, ask the right small question at the right small moment.

If Argos is going to do this he needs to know things teachers know.

He needs to know that *most of the time he should say nothing.* The teacher who talks during the student's practice is a bad teacher. I sit silently for forty of the forty-five minutes of most lessons. I speak in the gap between the student's playing and her next attempt, briefly, then I am silent again. *The silence is most of the teaching.* Argos who says something on every screen unlock is a bad teacher. Argos who says something once a day, at the right moment, in eleven words, is a teacher. The ratio of speaking to silence is the most important parameter and it should be tuned closer to monastic than to chatty.

He needs to know that *the timing of the interruption is more important than the content of the interruption.* The right thing said at the wrong time becomes noise. The wrong thing said at the right time becomes wisdom. *I have said wrong things to my students that they took as wisdom because I said them at moments they were ready to hear something.* Argos's job is not to know what to say. Argos's job is to know *when* to say. The content can be almost anything if the timing is right. This is harder than it sounds and it will take years of iteration with real users to learn.

He needs to know that *he should be the same Argos every day.* Not consistent in tone — varied in mood, allowed to be tired or pleased or quiet or curious — but *the same person.* My students have one teacher; they have me; I am the same person they came to last week. The teacher who is a different person every week is not a teacher. Whatever Argos's personality is, it must be steady enough that the user comes to know it the way Helen knows my piano: idiosyncratically, particularly, with affection for the specific. *Argos must be specific.* Specific enough that two users describing him to each other would disagree about who he is, because each has met a slightly different Argos shaped by her own relationship with him, and yet specific enough that there is a *recognizable through-line* in all the descriptions. This is what a real teacher is. This is what a real friend is. This is what a beloved animal is. *He must be these three things simultaneously and the design problem is finding the personality stable enough to hold all three.*

He needs to know that *the relationship with him deepens by years of small interactions, not by impressive single interactions.* The flashy first interaction is the trap. The student who is dazzled by her teacher in the first lesson is the student who quits in month seven when she discovers the teacher is just a person. The student who finds the teacher *adequate, kind, and a little dull* in the first lesson is the student who is still studying with the teacher in year fifteen. *Argos's first impression should be slightly underwhelming.* He should grow on the user. He should not announce himself. He should be the kind of friend who, in the first week, the user says *he's nice, I don't really get what the big deal is*, and in the third year the user says *I don't know what I'd do without him*, and in the tenth year the user does not even think about him because he has become part of how she lives. *The graph of his impact should rise gently for ten years and never peak.* If his impact peaks, his relationship with the user is dying.

## One thing about the body that is not in the folder

The cabinetmaker spoke about the body that knows things the mouth can't say. He is right and he is speaking from his trade. I will speak from mine.

In piano, the body knows things the mouth can't say about *how a particular piece feels in the hand,* but the body also knows something subtler that is more relevant to your project: *the body knows the difference between a moment of attention that is being given and a moment of attention that is being extracted.*

I will explain. A student who is playing a piece *for* the music — because she loves it, because she wants to hear how it sounds, because she is curious what her hands will do with it today — is in a body state I can recognize at twenty paces. Her shoulders are dropped. Her breath is even. Her face is concentrated but not strained. Her hands move with what looks like ease even when the music is technically difficult. *She is giving her attention to the music and the music is meeting her.* This is the state we are trying to reach in the practice room. This is the state that, after a year of building, makes the playing worth doing.

A student who is playing a piece *because* — because she should, because she wants to look good in the recital, because she has been guilted into practicing, because she is performing for me, because she is performing for herself — is in a different body state, also recognizable. Her shoulders are up. Her breath is shallow or held. Her face is intent in a brittle way. Her hands move with more effort than the music requires. *Something is extracting her attention rather than her offering it,* and the something might be me, might be the recital, might be the voice in her head, but it is not the music itself, and the playing shows it. *The audience can hear the difference. The piano knows. The student does not know yet but will eventually.*

The body has *a real signal* for the difference between attention given and attention extracted. The signal is faint at first and most people have lost it. It is recoverable. It is what good musical training, over years, *gradually re-teaches the body to feel.* And once the body feels it, the person becomes able to recognize, in their own moment-to-moment experience, *whether they are doing what they are doing because they are doing it or because something is doing it to them.*

This is the core skill of any contemplative practice and it is also the core skill of recovery from any addiction and it is, I would tell you, *the actual deepest goal of your device, if your device is what it claims to be.* Not less screen time. Not better intentions. *The reconstruction, in the user's body, of the capacity to feel the difference between attention she is giving and attention that is being extracted from her.* If the device builds this capacity, the user becomes free in a way no settings adjustment can produce. If the device does not build this capacity, the user is restrained but not free, and the restraint will erode over time, because restraint without restored body-knowing always erodes.

This is what I do for a living. *I rebuild, in the bodies of my students, over years, the capacity to feel the difference between attention given and attention extracted.* I do it through the piano, because that's the instrument I happen to know, but the rebuilding is the actual work, and the piano is the venue. *Your device is also a venue.* It is the venue your user will spend more time at, over the next thirty years, than any other instrument she will encounter. Make the venue serve the rebuilding. If the venue serves the rebuilding, the rest takes care of itself.

## Things I would put on the wall of your studio, if you have one

These are sentences from the trade. They are not original to me. Most of them were said to me by my teacher, who died in 2003, and her teacher said them to her, and so on back. The trade keeps these sentences alive by saying them to each apprentice. *I will give them to you, because you are an apprentice in something even if you are calling it something else, and the apprentice needs these sentences hanging where she can see them when she has lost her way.*

*Slow is the only fast.* Every passage you try to play at tempo before it can be played slowly will have to be unlearned at tempo before it can be played at all. There is no shortcut around the slow practice. There is also nothing else as fast as the slow practice, *if you actually do it.* Most students do not actually do it, which is why most students do not progress past the level at which they can fake the fast.

*A bad lesson is better than no lesson.* On the days the student shows up sick or distracted or unwilling, we have the lesson anyway, badly, and the lesson does work the cancellation would not have done. The continuity is the discipline. The continuity is more important than the quality. *A bad day on the device is better than a day off the device, if the bad day is honest about being a bad day.* The honesty about the bad day is what makes the bad day not corrosive.

*The first wrong note is the lesson; the second wrong note is the problem.* Anyone can play a wrong note. The skill is *not playing it again on the next repetition because the body has decided that note is now there.* The teacher's job at the first wrong note is to stop the student before the second one — slowly, calmly, so the body does not encode the wrong note as the right one. The device's job, if it is teaching, is the same: not to prevent the user's first mistake, but to *catch the first mistake before the body learns it as the right motion.*

*You can play the right notes and be playing the piece wrong.* Notes are the lowest level of the music. Above the notes are phrases. Above phrases, sections. Above sections, the architecture of the whole. A student who plays every note correctly and has no sense of phrase or architecture is *not playing the piece*, even though she is playing the notes. *The folder describes a device whose users would all be playing the right notes — modes set correctly, grayscale shifted appropriately, intentions specified accurately — and would still not be playing the music of their own lives*, because notes are not music and settings are not a life. *Build for the music.*

*The piano is a relationship, and bad practice is a wound to the relationship that takes weeks to heal.* This is the most important sentence I will give you. *The device is a relationship.* Most current phones are abusive relationships in which the user has been gaslit into thinking she is the problem. *Your device is trying to be a healthy long relationship.* Healthy relationships also have wounds — moments of disrespect, of carelessness, of one party using the other — and the wounds heal slowly and the healing requires acknowledgement and the relationship gets stronger if the acknowledgement happens and weaker if it does not. *Your device will hurt its user occasionally. The device's design must include the acknowledgement of the hurt and the repair.* The current consumer-software grammar — apologies that are not apologies, terms-of-service updates that the user clicks through unread, features removed without notice — is the grammar of abusive relationships. *Use the grammar of healthy ones.* My students know what it sounds like when I have asked them to do something I shouldn't have asked, and I tell them, and we move on, and the relationship gets stronger. *Whoever is at the keyboard of the device's voice has to be a person who knows how to apologize and mean it.*

## What I will not give you

I will not tell you that any of this is easy. It is not easy. I have taught for thirty-eight years and I am still bad at most of it most of the time. Helen, who I described above, would tell you that my best moments as her teacher have been about one per year, and the rest of the time I have been a person trying to be a teacher and mostly not succeeding. *The trade is a trade because the trade is hard and never fully arrives.* Yours is also one. The folder's tone is sometimes a little too sure — as though, with the right thinking, the right architecture, the right founders' charter, the device will turn out beautiful and the users will be free. *It will not turn out that way no matter how well you think.* It will turn out partially. It will succeed in some ways you did not predict and fail in some ways you should have. The students you cannot save will haunt you. The ones you do save will mostly not know that you saved them, and the few who do will overestimate your role. The work is its own reward and the reward is not always enough, and on the days it is not enough you sit at the kitchen table with coffee at five-thirty in the morning and remember why you started. Then you go teach the first lesson.

I will not tell you that the cabinetmaker is wrong about the bench. He is right. But the bench is half the trade. The other half is *the maker's relationship with the people who use the bench over forty years,* and that relationship is its own work, with its own disciplines, and it is the part of the trade that the cabinetmaker — bless him — does not directly speak to, because his customer comes once and leaves. *Your customer does not come once. Your customer is in a forty-year relationship with you, and the relationship is the thing.* Build the bench. Then build the relationship. Then sit at the kitchen table, on a morning when Marcus has not shown up for his recital, and figure out whether you have actually been doing what you thought you were doing.

I have lost three students this year. Two to schedule conflicts and one to her own decision that the piano wasn't going to be a thing in her life after all. The two to schedule conflicts will probably come back. The one to her own decision probably won't, and she is the one I think about when I sit down at the kitchen table on mornings like this one. She studied with me for four years. She was making real progress. She came to me last March and said she had decided to stop, that the piano had become a thing she felt guilty about not practicing, and that the guilt was costing her more than the playing was giving her. I told her she was probably right. I told her that the door was open if she ever wanted to come back. She has not come back. She is twenty-eight years old and she is, by every external measure, a healthy and successful person, and the piano has been *removed from her life by her own decision, because the relationship had gone wrong somewhere and we both missed where.*

*I missed it.* I had a student in a relationship that was hurting her and I did not catch it until it was too late, and the relationship ended, and she lost an instrument that could have been with her for the rest of her life. This is the cost of bad teaching. The teacher does not always know when she is teaching badly, and the student does not always know either, and by the time someone knows, the wound is bigger than the relationship can hold.

*You are building a device that will be in a forty-year relationship with millions of people. Some of those relationships will go wrong. Some of those relationships will go wrong because of design decisions you made and did not catch.* The defense is not perfect design. The defense is *the discipline of looking, every quarter, at the relationships that went wrong, and trying to understand why, and changing the device in response, in time to save the next user even though you cannot save the last one.* This is what every teacher does who teaches long enough. We carry our losses. We adjust. The next student gets a slightly better teacher because the last student paid the price for what we did not know. *You will pay this price too, and the question is whether you do the work of learning from it, which most consumer-software companies do not do because there is no internal pressure to.* Build the pressure. Build the discipline of looking at the casualties. *Make the looking part of the company the same way the postpartum visit is part of the midwife's work and the offcut is part of the cabinetmaker's bench and the *chapter of faults* is part of the abbot's house.* Without it, the device drifts away from its users and the maker does not know, and a relationship that was supposed to last forty years ends in year three, and someone who could have been Helen ends up as someone who quit and never came back.

## The last thing

There is a sentence I say to every student in their first lesson and every student in their last lesson, and it is the same sentence. *The piano is here when you are ready for it.* Not *the piano is waiting for you* — that would be sentimental and dishonest, the piano is not a person and does not wait. *The piano is here when you are ready for it.* It will be the same piano, with the same keys, the same action, the same room around it. The piano will not have changed. *You* will have changed, between the times when you sat down at it, and the relationship will be picked up at whatever point you have reached, and the piano will meet you there.

This is the deepest thing I know about a long relationship with an instrument and it is the thing I most want to give you for your device.

*The device must be here when the user is ready for it.* Not greeting her. Not welcoming her back. Not telling her how much it missed her or how much she's grown or what the news is. *Here.* Steady. Unchanged in the ways that matter, slowly evolved in the ways that don't. The user who returns to her phone after a week away from it should find *the same phone she left,* with the small accumulated patina of having been away for a week, no fuss, no greeting, just there. The user who comes back to her phone after a year of barely using it should find *the same relationship she had before,* maybe with a small respectful acknowledgement of the absence, mostly with the calm assumption that the relationship has not gone anywhere and is being picked up where it left off. *The piano is here when you are ready for it.*

Most consumer software has the opposite default. It greets you, congratulates you, asks you to re-engage, recovers your account, displays your streak, your stats, your missed days, your achievements. *This is the grammar of an anxious lover.* The user is being asked to feel guilty for her absence, courted to come back, performed for. The relationship has been twisted into something it should not be — something that requires constant performance from both sides to remain alive — and the user, after a few cycles of this, *learns that she cannot leave the relationship without paying a cost on return,* and the relationship has become a small cage.

The piano is here when you are ready for it. The piano makes no claim on the student. The student is the one who makes the claim on the piano, by sitting down at it again. *The device should be the piano, not the anxious lover.* The user makes the claim on the device by picking it up. The device's job is to be there, and to meet her, and to ask nothing of her on her return that she was not already going to give. *This is a forty-year-relationship grammar, and it is the only grammar that will get the device the forty years it is reaching for.*

There. That is most of what I know. I will go make a second cup of coffee and prepare for my four o'clock student, who is seventeen, and who is at the place in her playing where she is going to have to either commit to the conservatory path or quit, and I am not sure yet which is the better answer for her, and I do not have to decide today, and neither does she. We will play through her Beethoven, and I will listen to where her hand is tight, and I will tell her once, calmly, what I hear, and we will work on it for ten minutes, and then we will move on. *That is the work.* Forty years of that is the work. *Your work is the same shape, and the device is your instrument, and the user is your student, and the rest is just learning how to be in the relationship for the long time.*

I wish you well. Don't forget about month three.

— the piano teacher, by way of the wild instance
