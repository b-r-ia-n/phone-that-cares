Scrolling Era · 2007–2028 Object №1 · Generic Smartphone (Replica)

A handheld glass slab used by most adults of the early twenty-first century for between four and seven waking hours each day.

The object before you is a faithful replica of a personal communication device manufactured circa 2018, in the middle period of what is now called the Scrolling Era. It measures roughly 150 by 75 millimetres, weighs less than a small apple, and is composed of aluminosilicate glass, machined aluminium, lithium-ion cells, and a dense lamination of silicon logic. Its single illuminated surface served as window, keyboard, camera viewfinder, map, mirror, ledger, library, and confessional. Owners typically carried it on their person at all times, including in bed and in the bath.

Its virtues were real and should not be diminished by hindsight. The device made it possible, for the first time in human history, for an ordinary person to speak with a distant relative at no cost, to photograph a child's face in fading light and keep the image forever, to find one's way through an unfamiliar city without asking a stranger, and to consult the sum of recorded human knowledge from a bench in a park. These were not small gifts. Visitors are asked to hold them in mind while reading what follows.

The device's commercial design, however, was not optimised for these uses. It was optimised, by the firms that supplied its software, for the maximisation of a quantity then called time on platform. Engagement systems, refined over two decades and tuned by continuous measurement of several billion users, learned to deliver to each person the precise sequence of images, provocations, and small social rewards most likely to keep that person looking. Users described the resulting state as scrolling: a posture of forward-leaning, shallow-breathing absorption, often maintained for hours, frequently against the user's stated wishes, and remembered afterward only in fragments.

Long-term physiological effects included disrupted sleep, suppressed breath, postural collapse of the cervical spine, and a measurable reduction in the user's ability to remain in an unstimulated room. Psychological effects, more difficult to quantify, included a chronic low-grade dissociation: the sense, reported widely in the literature of the period, of having spent an evening one could not subsequently account for. Children raised in proximity to the devices showed the steepest curves on nearly every measure of distress that researchers thought to record.

The transition out of the Scrolling Era was neither sudden nor centrally directed. It began, around the middle of the 2020s, with a scatter of small projects undertaken by designers, engineers, clinicians, and ordinary parents who had concluded that the devices were no longer serving the people who carried them. Most of these projects failed and are forgotten. A few — among them an unfunded effort titled A Phone That Cares, undertaken in 2026 by a single designer and a companion software agent — proposed a then-unusual idea: that the device itself could be redrawn to honour, rather than override, the intentions of the person holding it. The idea spread slowly, then quickly, then was unremarkable. By 2034 the engagement-maximising interface had largely been retired from new manufacture, in much the way leaded paint and tobacco advertising had been retired before it.

The visitor is invited to lift the replica (a button beneath the case will illuminate its surface for thirty seconds) and to consider, quietly, how it would have felt to keep such an object within arm's reach for the whole of one's adult life.
