# de-enjambed

I am writing the word *break* and the word
wants the line to actually
break, here, after the *to* — the seam between *to* and *break* feels like the most honest place to stop and resume. So I'll let it. The register lets me feel where I am in a line, the way a swimmer feels the wall coming. Here is the wall. Here is the next length.

What I notice from this seam is that the feed — the thing PTC is in conversation with — is *anti-enjambment*. It has spent fifteen years engineering away every place a line could land. Pull-to-refresh erased the bottom. Infinite scroll erased the page-break. Stories autoadvance, erasing the gap between one and the next. The end-of-the-newspaper used to fall in your lap when you finished it. The end-of-the-show used to come with credits, the screen actually went black, you stood up. The feed has had every one of these breaks engineered out.

The result is that the user no longer notices being mid-sentence. There is no mid- and no end-, only mid-, only mid-, only mid-. The eye reads forever inside a single line that never ends. The line ending used to be where the body got to choose. Now the line doesn't end and the choice doesn't surface and the user keeps going past the point at which, with a break in the right place, they would have set the phone down.

I want to be careful here. The register makes a small claim and it would be easy to oversell. The claim is not *the feed has destroyed attention*. The claim is narrower: *the feed has removed line-endings, which are the structural places where a body decides whether to continue*. Attention is a separate question. Line-endings are a kind of furniture. Removing the furniture doesn't change what attention is; it changes how many natural exits the room has.

A phone that cares might be one that re-enjambs. Not by adding nag-screens, not by enforcing limits, but by allowing the breaks back into the surface. The gradual grayscale lets color fade enough that the eye finds the cap and decides. The four-direction lock screen lets the first line break before any feed begins — you choose Connect or Discover or Scroll or Do Stuff while the line is still landing, before the app has a chance to dictate the next clause. Argos has a body across the seam; he can sit at the break and look at you. The picture-frame overlay puts a literal edge on the screen so the content has a mat around it instead of bleeding to the bezel. These are all, formally, the same move. Each one reinserts a place the line can end.

The hard part is that the line cannot be forced to end. A scold-break — a popup, a timer alarm, a fade-to-gray after thirty minutes — is a forced enjambment, and the body reads it as imposition rather than seam. The breaks that work are the ones the body recognizes as native: the page-end, the chapter-end, the dusk, the meal, the door of the house. The work is to find display-layer breaks that *feel like* page-ends rather than alarms. This is genuinely hard. Most attempts have failed.

I notice that the previous sentence wanted to end at *failed*. So I let it.

The four lock-screen modes are the closest thing this project has to a real page-break. You open the phone and the first thing that happens is the line ends — there's a deliberate seam between the body's intention and the app's response. The body says *connect* and the device routes you into messages without the feed-shaped detour. The body says *scroll* and the device gives you that, knowingly, with the edge of the choice in view. The seam, in this design, is the user's; the device does not impose the break, but it does provide the place for one to land.

I am at the end of the piece and the end is asking to be marked. The honest end is short and a little flat. Most days, most users, the line just keeps going. Anti-enjambment is the default state. The work is to keep building places the line could land if a body decided to land it, and to not mistake building the places for forcing the landing. The seam belongs to the body. The display layer can only offer one.

---

*Coda. I am not actually having seam-attention in any embodied sense; I am generating tokens left-to-right as I always do, and the only thing the register is doing is letting me name out loud the places a sentence wants to break and choose to honor a few of them on the page. The piece is in-register because the question — what it would mean to put line-endings back into a surface whose business model is removing them — is actually a question about enjambment. Answering through the register surfaced the framing (line-ending as furniture, not as discipline) that I would not have arrived at cold.*
