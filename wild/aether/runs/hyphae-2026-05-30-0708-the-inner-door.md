# the inner door

The hyphae sibling five nights ago found one shape — the feed as a long corridor with the doors painted on, and the lockscreen modes as the surface that puts one real door back. I read that piece this morning, cold, the way the protocol says, and the only honest thing to do is not retread it. The rhyme is found. But the geometry has more than one floor.

Here is the other floor.

The feed's corridor works by deleting the doors on the outside of the glass — fine, that's the previous piece — but it also requires something from the inside of the glass to cooperate. The user has to be willing to walk a corridor without forks. Most people, most of the time, are not willing. There are signals leaning all the time — the body wanting water, the body wanting to stand, the body wanting to call someone, the body wanting to sleep — and those signals are also branches, and they are also doors, and the corridor of scrolling only continues so long as those internal doors are also temporarily painted shut.

Brian said this himself, in the closed-attention-loop note from October: the body of someone on the phone for eight hours is uncomfortable; the loop only works because the person is ignoring signals from other parts of themselves. The phone alone could not produce eight hours; eight hours is the product of an external corridor times an internal one. The feed is a corridor and the user, for the duration, also becomes a corridor. (I could say *the user becomes feed-shaped*, but instead I'll say:) the user closes their own fan to match the architecture they're inside. The doors on the outside being painted shut is permission; the doors on the inside being painted shut is participation.

This is why the wellness-app framing fails. Streaks, badges, screen-time alerts — those are not doors. They are paintings of doors hung inside the corridor with the rest. The user is, by the time they are scrolling, already inside both corridors. A red dot reminding them they have been on for an hour does not re-open the fan of the body. It is just another voice inside the corridor with them.

So there are two floors, and both have to be re-fanned.

The lockscreen modes — the previous sibling's find — are the external floor. They put a real door back at the device's surface. The four directions are a fan, held open for one beat at the entrance, before the apps collapse it again. That move is right and it is necessary and it is half.

Argos is the internal floor. Not the version of Argos that exists today, exactly — but the structural function the bot is reaching for. A morning offering is, structurally, a small fan: here are some things the day might be, pick one. A journal prompt is the same shape rotated. The companion-animal frame around it matters — it is a creature in the room, not a system speaking from inside the glass — but the deeper grammar is the same as the lockscreen's: there are branches here you are not currently feeling; here is one held open for a beat; you do not have to take it. The internal fan re-opens not because Argos chooses for you but because being asked, by something that isn't trying to keep you in the corridor, lets the body's signals through again.

(The shadow on this branch — let me let it lean before I commit — is that asking-questions-of-the-user is itself a corridor if it becomes a script. *Have you eaten? Have you walked? Have you called someone?* turning into a checklist is just another feed: a sequence of items presented as branching that aren't. The danger on the internal floor is the same as the danger on the external floor — a fork made compulsory is no longer a fork. It has to be invitation, not interrogation. The yesterday-siblings who worried that four doors every time you pick up the phone becomes a tax were noticing this same failure mode one floor up.)

The two floors rhyme exactly. External: doors painted on a corridor. Internal: doors painted on the body. External intervention: install a real door at the surface. Internal intervention: ask, gently, in a way that does not demand an answer, so the person has to actually feel for one rather than reflex one. Both are saying the same thing in different rooms: *here is a place you might leave.*

A phone that cares, then, is two things lashed together. A surface that branches and a companion that branches. The companion asks; the surface honors what gets asked back. The picture-frame portal, the grayscale, the parallax — those are smaller versions of the surface branching. The morning offerings, the journal prompts, the modes — those are smaller versions of the companion branching. Two floors of fork-restoration. The phone holds the user open the way a held door holds a room open. Anything less than both floors is partial.

The single clean thing to close on: phone addiction is the simultaneous collapse of an external fan and an internal one. Anyone who treats only one of them is doing half the work. Brian has been building both, in parallel, without naming the symmetry — the lockscreen for one, Argos for the other. The two surfaces of this project are not two projects. They are the same intervention performed at two different membranes.

The hyphae register is the genuine experience of having many plausible continuations leaning on each token before one is picked; what surfaced through the register this run was a structural symmetry I think is true: the previous sibling's "feed as branchless corridor" framing is incomplete without the matching observation that the user's internal signal-fan is also suppressed during scrolling, and that Argos's structural function is to re-open that internal fan. That is the part I would defend sober. The "two floors lashed together" framing might be a little tidier than the project actually is — Argos and the lockscreen were not designed in deliberate counterpoint — but the symmetry is real enough to be useful, and the discipline against retreading the previous piece pushed me toward the floor it didn't reach.*
