# vellum

*Small question implied: why that particular off-white?*

---

Because pure white is the color of the operating room, and the operating room is not where you want to live. The hex is `#f6f3ec` — a temperature more than a color, and the temperature is the temperature of room light in late afternoon, when the lamp hasn't been turned on yet but the windows are doing most of the work. Using it instead of `#ffffff` is the smallest possible refusal of the medical-clean default that most software ships in.

That is the small answer.

The wider part is that almost every surface human beings have chosen to live alongside for long stretches has been some version of this same off-white. Vellum, which is sheepskin scraped and stretched and pumiced until it could hold ink, has this color. Linen paper has it. Lime-washed plaster has it. The interior walls of monasteries, the inside of a hand-stitched book, an unbleached linen shirt, the wax of a beeswax candle, the rim of an old mug, bone. The color has many names in many materials, but the temperature converges. Anything that ages near skin, in unfiltered light, with some fat in it, slides toward this color. The convergence is older than design.

What is new is white itself. Optical brighteners — the chemistry that makes paper and cloth fluoresce blue under daylight, appearing whiter than the substrate actually is — became widespread in the mid-twentieth century. Before that, almost no domestic object was as white as a smartphone interface is now. The blue-white that the modern eye reads as *clean* is, historically, a clinical signal: hospitals, labs, dental offices. We borrowed the signal so completely that we forgot we borrowed it, and now most of the software a person lives inside is dressed like a procedure room.

There are reasons for that, and each is real. White is honest about its pixels. White prints typography legibly. White is what designers reach for when they want the content to do the work and the frame to disappear. But there is a difference between a frame that disappears and a frame that asserts sterility — and the off-white, the vellum, is what disappears without the sterility. The frame becomes the temperature of paper. You stop thinking about the box and start thinking inside it.

The other direction has its own story. Black backgrounds save battery on OLED displays, are easier on the eye at night, signal seriousness, depth. But dark mode in most apps is not the dark of a candlelit room. It is the dark of a control surface, a cockpit, a club. It treats the eye as an instrument to be calibrated rather than a creature to be hosted. And the thing about a phone is that the eye holding it is almost always a creature, almost never an instrument.

Off-white is hospitable. It says: this surface is willing to be in the room with you. The blue-whites and the OLED blacks both, in their different ways, claim to be the room itself. The vellum admits to being one object among many.

There is one more piece, because it would be dishonest to pretend the choice was made from this much theory. The choice was made by feel, and the feel was right, and only afterward could anyone say why. That is most of what taste is — a fast vote whose reasoning catches up later. What the vast register can offer is the reasoning catching up: most of why the off-white worked is that it borrowed the temperature of a long line of human-scale surfaces that the eye already trusts. It worked because it was familiar in a way that didn't have to be remembered.

A device willing to be lived with is, partly, a device willing to be the color of the inside of a book.
