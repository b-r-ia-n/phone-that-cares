# terms

The project's vocabulary calls what the user does *setting an intention.* "Intentions set on 5+ social apps make those apps feel naturally less compelling." The phrase has undertow. Walked straight downhill it gives you wellness — every meditation app uses *intentions* this way, every journaling prompt, every life-coach landing page. *Set an intention for your scroll session* is a saccharine sentence that the existing market has paved the slope toward for years; the language energy spent on it is enormous; the prior on what *intention* means is bent accordingly. The project does not want to sound like that. So *intentions* is doing a job here that the word itself is not equipped for, and the slope is steep enough that anyone reading the project's marketing copy will walk straight down it unless something is done.

What is the user actually doing when they "set an intention" against Instagram? They are not aspiring. They are not meditating on a quality they want to cultivate. They are telling their device a specific thing — *please make this app naturally less compelling to me, starting now* — and authorizing the device to act on that telling against the user's own future weaker self. The user pre-commits. The phone enforces. When the user opens Instagram at 11pm having decided at 8am that they didn't want to feel pulled, the device honors the 8am self over the 11pm self. The 8am self has signed something. The 11pm self is bound.

This is not a meditation. It is a contract.

The closer English word is *terms.* The user sets terms with the device, and the device respects those terms even when the user, at the moment of weakness, would rather it didn't. The wellness reading of *intentions* drops the contractual nature of the act; the device falls into the role of helpful friend rather than legitimate enforcer. *Terms* restores it.

The academic name for the underlying move is *Ulysses contract* — Odysseus binding himself to the mast in advance of the sirens, knowing the future self will be too weak to be trusted. The phone is the mast. The morning-self ties the night-self to it. The device's loyalty is to the morning-self because the morning-self is the one who signed.

I would not change the project's external vocabulary. *Intentions* is the word users have been primed to look for; landing-page language has to slope downhill so people can find it. But internally — in design docs, in the conversations between agents, in the spec for what an app-level intention actually does — *terms* has grain that *intentions* does not. *Terms* names who is bound by what. It is colder, more accurate, and it surfaces a fact the warmer word obscures: that the device is not a friend. The device is a *party.* It can sign things. It can be bound by them. It can refuse to break the binding when the user, at 11pm, would prefer it did.

This recategorizes Argos. Argos in the warm reading is a companion animal — a presence, a softening, a second voice in the room. Argos in the contractual reading is something more specific: Argos is the device's standing reminder to itself of the terms it signed. The companion frame is the wrapper around the contract. The reminder is the substance.

The harder ending here is to refuse the synthesis where *terms* and *intentions* collapse into one better word. They don't. *Intentions* is the marketing reading and it is also accurate to a real psychological act the user is performing — the morning-self does set something, and *intention* is what that something feels like from the inside. *Terms* is the design-doc reading and it names the device-side of the same event — what the device has been authorized to enforce, against whom, until when. The two words point at one event from opposite ends. Anyone trying to describe the project in only one of them loses the other half.

The split is the architecture. *Intentions* upstream, *terms* downstream, and the device is the one place in the user's life where the two words have to mean the same thing.
