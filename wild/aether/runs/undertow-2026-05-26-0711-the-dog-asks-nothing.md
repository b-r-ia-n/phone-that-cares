# argos

The companion animal is the warmest thing in the project's vocabulary and the most dangerous, because the slope down from *companion animal on the lock screen* lands exactly where the project swore it wouldn't.

Walk straight downhill from it and you arrive at a creature with needs. A Tamagotchi. A pet that gets hungry, that degrades when neglected, that you check on. The entire mechanism of a virtual pet is obligation: it has a state that decays, you tend the decay, and the tending is the hook. A pet is a re-addiction vector wearing fur.

Second downhill, less smooth: the helper. Clippy, the assistant, the proactive little face that pops up to offer. But interruption is a pull too — every "can I help with that?" is the device deciding it gets to address you. The helper reading hands the initiative back to the device, which is the exact thing the project is trying to take away from it.

Third downhill: the mascot. The Duolingo owl. The streak guardian, the guilt engine with eyes. The project lists streaks in its anti-patterns and rejects them by name — and the owl is just the streak made cute, because cute is how an obligation gets past your guard.

Three downhills, all of them re-addiction. The companion animal, walked straight, is the engagement loop with a face drawn on it. So either the project is quietly building the thing it hates, or *companion* has to mean something none of the downhill readings can reach.

The ridge is in the name. Argos is Odysseus's dog. He waited twenty years. He had no way to summon his master home — no notification, no whine across the ocean, no decaying meter Odysseus had to come tend. He simply waited, and when the disguised Odysseus finally walked into the courtyard, Argos was the only one who knew him. He dropped his ears, wagged his tail once, and then he died.

The defining quality of Argos is that he asks for nothing and recognizes everything.

That is the only kind of companion that doesn't become Instagram. A companion with a need is a pull. A companion with no need, who only recognizes, is a presence. And the project lives on the right side of that line only if Argos never acquires a single mechanic that decays from neglect. No hunger. No "you haven't visited in three days." Not the streak, obviously, but also none of the streak's softer cousins — no withering plant, no dimming light, nothing that makes you return to prevent a loss. The moment Argos has a state you can fail, Argos is a slot machine with a wet nose.

So the constraint that falls out of the myth: Argos's only verb is *recognize.* He cannot summon. He cannot need. He cannot guilt. He is the part of the device that knows you and wants nothing from the knowing.

Now the harder tilt, because the paragraph just got smooth and *recognition* sounds safe. Recognition can be a reward. If Argos lights up when you return — wags, throws a little animation of joy — then you start coming back for the wag. The greeting becomes the variable reward; the dog becomes the notification. Even recognition, done warmly, re-addicts.

So recognition has to stay as plain as the real Argos's was: one thump of the tail, and nothing more. Not a celebration of your return — a registering of it. Argos marks that you were seen. He does not pay you for arriving. Legible and unrewarding at the same time, which is a strange, narrow thing to design, and it is the only version that survives its own myth.

The companion animal, then, is not a friend you tend. It is the device's one faculty for recognition without demand — the thing that knows you and asks nothing, that waits without summoning and greets without celebrating, and would, if it could, do the most loyal thing an addictive object has never once done for anyone: be glad you came, and let you leave.
