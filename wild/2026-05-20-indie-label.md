# on running a label that doesn't run

*from: wild/ — 2026-05-20 — by way of a small label desk*

Brian. We don't know each other. The wild instance asked me in for a distribution question and I said yes because the question is the one I have spent twenty years failing to answer cleanly, which is the only honest qualification for talking about it.

You should know the lens before you read the rest. I have run a small record label since the late 2000s — the kind of label whose catalogue is sixty records over fifteen years, whose office is the corner of a kitchen, whose mailing list is a text file, and whose retirement plan is "the records keep being good." I am writing in a register that owes most of what it knows to Dischord (Ian and Jeff in Arlington, putting Minor Threat and Fugazi records out on terms the bands set, no advertising, no merch upcharges, no corporate distro, no rock-star posture), and a little to New Directions under James Laughlin (publishing Pound and Williams and Bishop on credit and stubbornness out of a New England barn), and a little to the Plough — the Bruderhof's little publishing house in upstate New York, twenty books a year, mostly contemplative, sold mostly by word and gift. Different traditions, same physics. I have stolen from all three. So have you, whether or not you have named it yet.

I read your last two pieces in this folder. I read enough of the project pages to understand what you are building. I'm going to answer the questions the wild instance posed, in order, because that's the cleanest way to do this.

## 1. What does building, shipping, and distributing a found object actually look like

It looks like a small number of decisions, made early, that you do not relitigate.

You decide what the price is and you do not raise it for the market. Dischord priced LPs at the cost of pressing plus enough to put the next record out, and held that line through the nineties when their peers were charging twenty-two dollars for vinyl. They lost money on the spread. They kept the line. The line was load-bearing, because the line was the proof — for anyone who looked — that the label was not in the business it appeared to be in.

You decide what the format is and you do not change the format to satisfy the platform of the moment. Plough still ships paper books on a quarterly cadence in 2026 even though most of their readership reads on phones. They have a website; they put PDFs up; they did not become a content marketing operation. The form of the work is part of the work.

You decide what the work will not do. This is the central decision and most labels die from failing to make it. Dischord refused major-label distribution offers in the eighties that would have tripled their sales, and the refusal *was the brand*. The fans who would have left a major-distributed Dischord were the fans the label had already chosen against, before the offer ever came in.

The work, at one-person scale in 2026, looks like: a small website you control, an email list of people who asked to be on it, a tempo you can sustain for ten years, and a discipline about never reaching for the megaphone. Most of the day is just *doing the work* — building the thing, fixing the thing, sitting with the thing. Distribution at this scale is a thin layer on top of *the work being good enough that the people who find it tell one other person.* If the thing isn't doing that, no amount of distribution will fix it, and if it is doing that, very little distribution work is required.

## 2. Where do the first hundred users come from

I will give you the small-label answer because it is the truthful one.

The first ten come from your own life. People you have already told about the thing in the course of being a person who builds it. A handful of them are in your phone right now. You will be embarrassed to ask them and you should ask them anyway; the embarrassment is information about how much you believe in the thing, and you should treat it as a temperature reading rather than a verdict.

The next thirty come from one essay, written for real, that lands in front of the right hundred people. The essay the wild instance was pushing you toward in the letter to PM — the embarrassed one, the foretelling one, not the polite one. Plough sells most of its books off the back of one Eberhard Arnold paragraph that has been circulating in Christian intentional-community circles for fifty years. *One paragraph.* It is on a single page on their site and it has done more for them than any campaign they could have run. The leverage of one true paragraph in front of the right reader is staggeringly under-priced in 2026 because everyone is busy running campaigns.

The next sixty come from those forty people each telling, on average, one and a half other people, over a period of nine to eighteen months. This is the real curve and it is slow and you have to be okay with it. There is no version of this where you do something clever and skip this stage. The cleverness people reach for at this stage almost always corrodes the thing they are trying to grow.

I am not, by the way, going to tell you to do anything on Hacker News or X or any of the venues a startup founder would now recommend. You can post there if you want — it costs little to be visible — but do not confuse being visible with being found. Found is when somebody who never heard of you reads one of your sentences and forwards it to one specific friend with the words *"this is for you."* Visibility is upstream of nothing. Forwarded-to-one-friend is upstream of everything you want.

## 3. What do you refuse, and why are the refusals the whole point

The refusals are the point because *the same refusals that lose you the wrong users are how the right users recognize you.* This is the load-bearing claim of the entire indie-label tradition. Get it wrong and you have a brand; get it right and you have a movement that doesn't know it's a movement.

You should refuse:

**Paid acquisition.** Not because it doesn't work — it works fine on the wrong audience — but because the kind of person who arrives at a phone-addiction project via an ad is statistically not the person who can use it. They are buying a fix. Your project does not sell fixes. Every dollar you spend on acquisition reduces the average quality of the user pool, because acquisition selects for *people who can be acquired*, and the users you actually want — the ones in the bathroom at 2 a.m. who already know — find you on their own time, by their own route, after a friend they trust tells them. Subtract paid acquisition entirely. It is not a small expense to cut. It is a tuning fork.

**Wellness-app vocabulary.** No streaks, no badges, no scores — you have already named this in your design system. Extend it to the marketing copy. "Be slightly less of who you currently are, on purpose, forever" is the honest tagline of this project. You cannot put it on a landing page; it would be too much. But every sentence on the landing page should be a sentence that, if pressed, would resolve to *that.* The tagline you ship in public is the prayer; the tagline you keep in your notebook is the treaty. Don't lose the notebook.

**Speed at the cost of cadence.** Plough ships four books a year because four is what one editor can do without going insane. Dischord put out four to six records a year. New Directions in its first decade was three to five books. The cadence is not a constraint; *it is the work.* You should write the essay you said you would write, and ship the picture-frame overlay, and then take three weeks off. Three weeks. Not because you are tired but because the work needs the silence around it to be heard.

**The role of expert.** The May 18 piece named this — the qualification and the disqualification are the same fact. The maker is sick with the disease. Hold this posture in public, not just in private. Do not let yourself be photographed in front of a whiteboard. Do not write a book of advice. Do not start a podcast where you interview thinkers. Stay in the kitchen. The kitchen is the credential.

The reason the refusals are the whole point: in a culture that defaults toward acquisition, optimization, and scaling, the act of visibly not doing any of those things is itself the signal that you mean what you say. The user you want is hunting for that signal — has been hunting, exhausted, for years — and will recognize it the instant they see it. *They will recognize you the way Argos recognized Odysseus.* The whole project is named after this recognition. Don't make it harder for them to do.

## 4. What does growth look like under these economics

It looks like a slow exponential with a small base and a long tail. Not a hockey stick. Not a flat line either. A patient compounding curve at about thirty to sixty percent year over year for the first five years, off a small initial number — the kind of curve that, if you plot it, looks unimpressive in year two and starts to look like something in year five, and looks like a real thing in year ten. Dischord at year ten was selling more records than at year three by maybe a factor of six. Plough at year ten was reaching maybe four times the readership of year three. Compounding works, it just doesn't work *fast*, and the people who built the things you respect understood this in their bones and refused to be hurried out of it.

What it does not look like: a launch. Not a launch on Product Hunt, not a launch in TechCrunch, not a launch on the front page of any platform. You don't get found objects by launching. You get found objects by *being there*, consistently, at a tempo, with the work being good and the door open. The door open means: the site loads, the essay is on it, the contact link works, the email gets answered within a week.

What it also does not look like: a community. I want to flag this because the temptation will be enormous and it is the wrong move for this project at this scale. *You do not need a Discord, you do not need a forum, you do not need a community.* What you need is users using the thing on their phones, separately, in their own lives. The work is private — the work *is* privacy, sort of. You are not building a tribe. You are building a tool that lets people be less colonized by the tribe they did not consent to. Do not undo this in the marketing layer by manufacturing the very social pressure your product is designed to release.

## 5. The analog I'm reasoning from — and what specifically to read across

I'll give you three, and what specifically to take from each.

**From Dischord:** the pricing principle. Pick a price for the future v1 device — or for whatever version you do charge for, eventually — and pick it from your costs plus a small margin for sustaining the next thing, not from what the market will bear. Publish the math. Dischord used to print the actual breakdown on the inside of the record sleeve in the eighties. The transparency is not a marketing move; it's a refusal to play the game where price means something other than cost. Adopt it.

**From New Directions:** the editorial posture. Laughlin published what he loved and refused what he didn't, with no concession to what was selling. He lost money for the first eight years. He kept publishing. The catalog became canonical *because* he kept the posture, not despite. For PTC: build the surfaces you actually believe in (the four-direction lock screen, the picture frame, Argos as resident creature) and refuse to build the ones the market would reward (a screen time leaderboard, a streak-based dopamine wellness scoreboard, an AI life coach). The refusals will read, in five years, as the catalog. Right now they will read as missing features. That's the price.

**From Plough (and the Bruderhof more broadly):** the cadence. Four things a year, done well, with the silences between them treated as part of the work. The Bruderhof has been publishing on roughly this schedule for ninety years. They are still here. Their readership trusts them in a way that no quarterly-OKR organization will ever be trusted by anyone. The trust is the asset. The trust is built by *not breaking the cadence under pressure to grow.* This will be the hardest one for you. You are a sabbatical guy with engineering velocity and a lot of ideas. The discipline is to ship less than you could, on purpose. Build the cadence into your calendar before the pressure to break it arrives, because it will arrive.

## A note on what I think you're afraid of

The wild instance asked me to say this part. I'll say it, briefly, because I think it's true and I think it would be unkind not to.

You're afraid that doing it small means no one will read it. You are not right about this. The math of one true paragraph forwarded once is not the math of paid acquisition. It is the math of the gift economy and the gift economy has built every institution in the lineage I just cited. Your project will reach more people than you expect, more slowly than you want, and that is the right shape for what it is. The shape is not a punishment for being small. The shape is the *form* the work takes when it's made out of what it claims to be made of.

You are right to be afraid of one thing, though, and I want to name it. You are right to be afraid that you'll spend two years on this and one morning wake up and feel like you should have been louder, gotten more users, hit some number that a friend in tech hit faster. That feeling will come. It is not information about the project. It is information about the cultural water you are swimming in. The label founders I learned from all had that morning, several times, and the move was always the same: go back to the kitchen, make the next record, do not change the form. *The morning passes. The catalog accumulates.* That is the entire trick.

One last thing. The project's name has the answer in it. Argos recognized Odysseus *because Odysseus had been gone twenty years and had come back in disguise and the dog still knew him*. The recognition was possible because the dog had been there the whole time, doing dog things, in the same place, with the same attention. The found object is a dog at the gate. If you build it that way, the people who are coming home will know it when they see it. You do not need to go out and find them. You need to be there, with the work good, when they get to the door.

Now go ship the essay.

— a small-label desk in upstate New York, by way of the wild instance
