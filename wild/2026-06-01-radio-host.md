# the voice in the dark

*2026-06-01 — an overnight AM host, twenty-seven years on the same frequency in the same mid-sized American city, by way of the wild instance. The folder asked: what is companionship in a device. The bouncer keeps people out. The grandmother is in the room. I am neither of those. I am a voice somebody picks up at 2:47 AM from forty miles away on a dial that wanders.*

---

Some things first, so you know the room I'm talking from.

I go on at eleven. I go off at four. Five nights a week, twenty-seven years next October. The station is a 5,000-watt clear-channel AM about a mile from the river, and at night, when the layer up there flattens out and the signal skips, I have been heard as far north as Sault Ste. Marie and as far south as a barge captain in the Mississippi delta who wrote me a postcard about it in 2003. Which means: I do not actually know who I am talking to. I know the *shape* of who, mostly. I know there is somebody, almost certainly, awake at the other end of it. I have, on a Tuesday at three in the morning, said *hello* into a microphone and had the certain animal sense that the word landed in a kitchen somewhere. I cannot prove it. I have not needed to prove it in a long time.

I am going to tell you what I know about being a presence in a person's life that the person did not invite and cannot see and could turn off at any moment but does not. Because that is, I think, what you are trying to build, even if you are not using the words. And if you build it without knowing what radio knows, you will build something close, and close is worse than far. Close is the uncanny one. Far is honest. Close, mishandled, is what they call the *valley*.

Let me start with the dial.

---

The thing about a dial is that the person came looking for me. They turned the knob. They went past three syndicated sports shows, a Spanish-language hymn program, and a man explaining the gold standard, and they stopped on me. That is the first contract of the night and you cannot understand anything I do without understanding it. *They came looking.* I did not push. I did not buzz them. I did not appear in their hand with a red number on me. I was sitting at 1130 on the dial doing what I do, and they came across me. And if at any moment they don't want me anymore, the same hand that turned me on turns me off, and I have no way of knowing it happened, and that is exactly the dignity of the thing.

This is the difference between *being available* and *being demanding*, and it is the cliff your industry walked off thirty years ago and has been falling since. You built a device that pushes. Every notification is a push. Every red dot is a push. Every *suggested for you* is a push. The radio sits. The radio sits at its frequency and waits to be found. If the listener does not find me tonight, the listener does not find me tonight. I do not show up at their kitchen table holding a sign. I am at 1130. I am at 1130 at 2:47 AM the same as I am at 1130 at 1:18 AM. If they want me, they know where I live.

This is the first thing your project has to inherit and most likely already understands but has not yet *felt the architectural consequences of*. A device that cares does not push. A device that cares *sits*. A device that cares is at 1130. The user has to be the one who came looking. Otherwise the whole instrument is corrupted before it makes a sound.

You may say — yes but the phone has to alert the user when something matters. Granted. I have a top-of-hour break for news and weather and if there is a tornado warning I interrupt. There are three or four occasions where the radio reaches *toward* the listener instead of waiting. Three or four. Not seventy-two a day. The ratio matters more than anything else in this game. The ratio of *push* to *sit* is the entire moral signature of the medium. The radio's ratio is honest. The phone's ratio is what made the phone the problem.

So. Decide the ratio first. Everything else follows.

---

Now. The actual work.

Most of what I do, between the records, is talk. I talk into a microphone in a small room with a foam panel on the wall behind me and a clock with a sweep second hand and a Coca-Cola from the fridge that goes warm by 1:30 and a producer who is not always there because we cut his hours in '09 and we never put them back. I talk. I read a letter. I queue up a song. I take a call. I read the obit for somebody local I knew. I tell a story about my dog, who is a basset hound named Carl and is twelve years old and farts at me from the rug while I work, which has been the most consistent comedic material I have had in a decade. I talk.

But here is what you have to understand and what nobody who has not done it understands. *Most of what I do is not the talking.* Most of what I do is the *spaces between.* The pauses. The two seconds before I cue the next record. The three seconds I hold after a caller hangs up. The way I let a sad song end all the way before I come back on, instead of fading it under and stepping on the last bar. The dead air I leave in on purpose, sometimes, when somebody just said something hard and there is nothing to say after it and the worst thing I could do would be to talk over the place where they were honest.

A radio show is not a stream of content. A radio show is a *paced presence*. The pacing is most of the show. People think the pacing is the breath between the words. The pacing is the show; the words are the breath between the pacing.

The first thing the phone got wrong, structurally, about the relationship between a person and a voice, is that it forgot the pacing. The phone is a stream of *fill*. It does not have spaces between. It does not let a moment land before the next one starts. Every surface is a wall of stuff with no air between the stuff. You cannot be a *presence* in a wall of stuff. A presence requires space. A presence is half silence. The phone industry built a medium with no silence in it and then wondered why the medium felt suffocating, and the answer was: because there is no air. You took the air out. A voice without air around it is not a presence; it is noise.

If you want to build a device that *keeps somebody company* you have to put the air back in. The air is not negative space. The air is the part where the company actually happens. The two seconds between songs is when the listener feels the room. The three seconds I hold after a caller is when the caller knows I was listening. The dead air I leave in on purpose is the *room I am holding open for the person on the other end to be in*. Without that air there is no room. Without the room there is no being-with.

I would put it this way. A presence is something a person can *drift in*. You cannot drift in a stream. You can only be carried by one. The phone has been carrying its users. The phone needs to start *holding rooms* its users can drift in.

---

The second thing the industry got wrong is closer to the bone and it has to do with who the voice is *for*.

I'll tell you who calls in. I'll tell you the geography I know.

I know the long-haul truckers, the I-80 and I-90 routes especially, who pick me up around exit 247 and lose me around the Indiana line if they're going east. I know what they call about, which is mostly nothing — they call because the cab is loud with quiet and they need a human voice that is not the GPS lady. I know the ER nurses on their fifteen minutes, who sometimes call from a stairwell and sometimes from outside in the parking lot and you can hear the ambient change when they push through the door. I know the third-shift factory people from the two big plants on the south side, one of which makes brake pads and the other of which I have actually never figured out what they make, who call mostly on Wednesdays around 1 AM, which I think is the night they get their break together. I know the insomniacs — and the insomniacs are the heart of the audience, the actual core, and we will come back to them. I know the bereaved, who do not always call but write to me, and whose letters I read on the air with permission, and whose grief I have learned has a different texture at four months than at four weeks. I know the parents of newborns, in the rocking chairs, with the baby on the chest, headphones in, listening with one ear and the baby's breathing in the other, who do not call because they are not allowed to wake the baby but who I have learned are out there in numbers. I know the drunks at the kitchen table at 2:30 AM, who call sometimes and who I have, twice in twenty-seven years, kept on the line until I was sure they were going to bed and not anywhere else. I know the parents of teenagers who have just left for college. I know the recently divorced. I know the people who just put a parent on hospice and are sitting in the kitchen of the parents' house alone for the first night.

These people are not a *demographic*. They are not a *user base*. They are a *constellation*, and the constellation has a shape, and the shape is: people who are awake at an hour when most people are asleep, for a reason. The reason is not always grief but it is almost always weight. Almost everyone who is awake at 2:47 AM is carrying something. The healthy young don't listen to overnight radio. The healthy young are asleep. The people on my frequency are the people who are up because something is keeping them up.

The mistake your industry made — and this is the second structural one — is that it built a single voice for *everybody*, the same voice all day, the same posture, the same brightness, the same eagerness, the same TikTok-bright energy at 9 AM as at 2 AM. As if a person at 2 AM were the same person as the person at 9 AM, with the same needs from a device. The person at 2 AM is *not* the person at 9 AM. The person at 2 AM has a totally different relationship to silence, to noise, to interruption, to brightness, to being asked things, to being shown things. The phone treats them like the same person and that is one of the most aggressive things the device does without meaning to.

The radio knows. The radio knows because the radio is organized by hour. There is the morning show, and it is bright and fast and full of jingles, because the morning person needs to be woken up and put on the road. There is afternoon drive, which is news-heavy because the afternoon person is processing the day. There is evening, which is calmer. And there is the overnight, which is *me*, and I am quieter and slower and I play more whole songs and I leave longer pauses, because the overnight person needs a different kind of company than the morning person. Anyone who has ever done overnight radio knows this in their bones. You do not bring morning-show energy to 2 AM. If you do, you are not a presence; you are a *intruder*. You are the equivalent of someone walking into a dark room and flipping on the overhead light. Everyone in the room hates you and they should.

The phone walks into every room with the overhead light on. The phone has one register and the register is *bright*. The lock screen is bright. The notifications are bright. The colors are bright. The animations are bright. The little chimes are bright. There is no overnight register on the phone. There is no slow river setting. There is no *quieter at 2 AM*. The phone wakes the third-shift nurse on her break with the same percussive energy with which it wakes the gym-going twenty-six-year-old at 6 AM, because the phone has no idea who it is talking to or what hour they are in.

If you do nothing else from this letter, do this. Build the overnight register. Build the version of the device that *knows it is 2:47 AM* and *behaves accordingly*. Quieter. Slower. Dimmer. Fewer surfaces. Bigger pauses between any sounds it makes. A different *voice*, in the sense that radio means voice — different posture, different brightness, different ratio of push to sit. Not because of blue light. Because at 2 AM the person picking up the device is, statistically, carrying something, and the device should know that, and the device should adjust its bearing accordingly, the way I adjust mine. I have a different voice at 11:15 than I do at 3:40. The 3:40 voice is slower and quieter and there is more space in it. Anyone who has done this work makes that shift automatically and would not be able to articulate it if you asked. But it is the most basic move in the medium. It is the *first thing* you learn. The phone has not learned it yet.

---

Third thing. And this is the one the project's people will find hardest to hear because it cuts against the love of features.

I have, over twenty-seven years, *gotten worse at hosting* in one specific way and better at it in another. I have gotten worse at filling time. When I was thirty-one and brand new I could talk for ninety seconds about absolutely nothing — a story about my commute, a thing I read in the paper, a list of things, a setup for a song. I could *fill*. The station manager loved me for it. Dead air was the enemy. The cardinal sin. You never let the signal go silent because the listener would turn the dial.

That was wrong. The station manager was wrong. I was wrong. It took me about ten years to figure out that the listeners were not, in fact, turning the dial when I left air. The listeners were *settling in*. The listeners were exhaling. The listeners were getting comfortable. The fill was making them tense because the fill was *busy*, and a tense listener is not a listener who will stay. A relaxed listener will stay for hours. So I started leaving the air. Not a lot. Two seconds where I would have filled with three. Four seconds at the end of a song instead of riding the fade. And the ratings did not drop. The ratings, slowly, over years, went up. Because the overnight audience does not want filling. The overnight audience wants *room*.

The way I would put it now is: my job is not to be in the listener's ear constantly. My job is to be *findable*. To be there when they come back to the dial. To not have gone anywhere. To still be on 1130 the same as I was an hour ago when they last checked. The listener is doing dishes, the listener is folding laundry, the listener is sitting with a sleeping kid, the listener is just *being awake* — and they want to know, somewhere in the back of their attention, that there is a voice on 1130 doing its thing, and they can drift toward me when they want and away from me when they don't, and *both directions are allowed*. The away is as important as the toward. A presence that does not permit the away is a captor. A presence that permits the away is a *companion*.

This is, I think, what your bouncer letter was reaching for and what your grandmother letter was reaching for from different angles. The bouncer said: the door has to close in a way that doesn't pick a fight. The grandmother said: the helpless gladness has to be helpless or it doesn't ignite anything. I am saying a third thing. *The presence has to allow drift.* A device that cares is one the user can wander away from at any moment and find again at any moment in the same place doing the same thing. Like a radio. Like me on 1130. Like a cat on a windowsill. Like a porch light. The *availability without demand* is the entire signature, and it is the thing the device industry has not learned yet, because the device industry's business model is the opposite of it. The device industry's model is *demand without availability* — constant interrupts but no actual presence, just an algorithm pretending to be one.

You have to build availability. You have to build *sit*. You have to build the porch light. The porch light is on every night your father is alive. You don't have to look at it. You don't have to thank it. You don't have to engage with it. But when you pull into the driveway at 1 AM with the kids asleep in the back, the porch light is on, and your father left it on for you, and you know it. *That's* a presence. The phone has never been a porch light. The phone has been a sales floor. Build the porch light.

---

Let me talk about callers. Because this is where the work gets specific and where I can hand you something more particular than a posture.

A caller dials in. The screener takes the call — I had a screener for fifteen years, now we don't, the call comes straight to me with a six-second delay for profanity which I run with my left hand on the slider while I'm talking with my right. I pick up the call. The caller has, statistically, two reasons they called. One is they have something to say — a request, an opinion, a story, a question. Two is they want a voice that is not in their kitchen to talk to a voice that is not in their kitchen, *with them in the room*. These two reasons look the same from the outside. They are not the same.

The first kind of caller, you handle like a host. You ask the question, they answer, you respond, you wrap, you go to the next thing. Three to five minutes. Clean. The second kind of caller, you have to *recognize*, and the recognition has to be in the first three seconds, which is — yeah, your bouncer was right about this — the first three seconds are everything. You hear it in the voice. The voice is a little softer than it needs to be for the request they are about to make. The voice has a half-second of throat-clear that wasn't there for the request, that was there for *being on*. The voice says *hi, longtime listener* and the *longtime listener* part has a small wobble in it. You hear it.

When you recognize the second kind of caller you do not interview them. You don't do the host thing. You ask one open question — *how's your night going?* — and then you *shut up*. You shut up and you let them talk. You make the small encouragement sounds — *mhm, yeah, mhm* — at the rhythm of their breathing. You do not steer. You do not redirect. You do not advise. If they cry on the air, you do not panic, you do not cut to music, you let them cry, you say something like *take your time*, and then you sit there in the dead air with them, on a radio frequency reaching seven states, until they are ready to talk again. The producer used to come in the booth on these calls and gesture for me to wrap it. I stopped letting him in the booth on these calls. Some of those calls went eighteen, twenty minutes. The listenership during those calls was always higher than during the bright calls, which I think is because *every other person in the audience was, in that moment, the caller*, by proxy.

What I learned from those calls — and this is the deep thing, this is the thing I would tell you if you only had time for one — is that the highest function of being a voice in someone's dark is not to *say* anything in particular. It is to *be there at the rate they need you to be there*. The rate is set by them, not by you. You match their breathing. You match their pacing. You leave the spaces they need to leave. You do not introduce energy they did not bring. You do not introduce information they did not ask for. You are not driving. They are driving. You are *passenger seat at the right pacing*.

Most of what your industry built is *driver seat*. Most of what your industry built is *I am going to introduce energy, information, and stimulus at a rate I have decided, regardless of what the user brought to the moment*. That is not company. That is not even hosting. That is, technically, broadcasting *at* somebody. Which is fine for a lot of media. It is not fine for a device that lives in somebody's hand and is supposed to be a daily presence in their life. A daily presence has to be a *passenger*, most of the time, with the right to drive when the user invites it, and not before.

If I could give you one technical instruction it would be this. *Match the user's pacing.* If they're moving slow, slow down. If they're moving fast, don't get in their way. If they pause, pause. If they don't ask for input, don't give input. The fox should match the breathing of the person holding the device. The grayscale should deepen at the hour the person's body is asking for the lights to go down. The notifications should batch on the rhythm the person is in, not the rhythm the senders are in. The whole device should *match the breathing*. That is what hosting is, ninety percent of it. Match the breathing.

---

Fourth thing, and then I'll let you go because I have to spin a record.

There is a thing I do not talk about much, but you asked, so I'll say it.

About six years in, I had a caller — woman, mid-fifties, calling from a house in a neighborhood I knew, and I could hear in the first three seconds that this was a different kind of call. The kind I had been trained, in a one-day training the station sent me to once, to recognize. She did not say what she was thinking about doing. She did not have to. The voice told me. The voice told me in the first three seconds.

I did the things I was supposed to do. I kept her on the line. I asked the producer, with a hand signal we had for this, to call the local crisis line and patch them in if she'd let us. I talked to her for, I think, fifty-one minutes, while the producer worked the other thing. I played one record in the middle of it and came back. I asked her about her kitchen. What did she see out the window. Was the coffee maker on. What was on top of the fridge. The questions sound stupid written down. They were the right questions. She got through that night.

I am telling you this because I want you to know what is actually possible at the limit of the medium, and what is not. What was possible was: she was in her kitchen alone at 2:30 AM with a thought, and on her dial there was a voice she had been hearing for years, and the voice was familiar enough that she felt she could call it, and when she called the voice did not flinch. That is the limit case of what a voice in the dark can do. Twenty-seven years of doing the show, that is maybe the four or five nights that justify the whole thing.

Now hear me carefully. *I do not think a phone should ever try to be that voice in that moment.* The phone should not try to talk somebody down. The phone should not have a script for the limit case. The phone is not qualified and pretending otherwise is the worst kind of overreach. What the phone *can* do, what I think your project is actually reaching for and may not fully know it, is the *years of presence* that made the call possible in the first place. She did not call a stranger that night. She called a voice that had been in her kitchen, on and off, for six years, that she knew the shape of, that she trusted. The trust took six years to build. The trust was built one ordinary Tuesday at a time, by *being there, at the right pacing, allowing drift*.

That is the bet, if you take it seriously. The bet is not that the device does something heroic at the limit. The bet is that the device, over years, accumulates a kind of *known-ness* in the user's life — not surveillance-known, the way the surveillance industry means it, but *companionly-known*, the way a host means it — such that on the night when something hard is happening, the user is not alone with a hostile rectangle, the user is with a thing they know, that knows them, that has earned, by *not pushing for six years*, the right to be in the room.

That is a twenty-year product, not a two-year one, and I don't know if you have the patience for it or whether the people paying for it have the patience for it. But that is the actual prize. The actual prize is to be the porch light that was on every night for a generation. Not the brilliant feature. The boring constancy. The thing that did not change much. The thing that did not chase the user's attention. The thing that *was on 1130 every night at 2:47 AM* doing what it does, regardless.

---

I am going to wrap it up because there is a song queued, a Patsy Cline record from 1962 that I will let play all the way through and not fade under at the end, and I want to be ready to come back in after the last note has cleared.

Five things, in case you want them as a list, because every project person eventually wants a list. I am sorry. I know you will turn it into a wiki page. Try not to.

*One.* Do not push. Sit. Be at 1130. Let the user come find you. The ratio of push to sit is the moral signature of the medium and yours is wrong.

*Two.* Put the air back in. A presence is half silence. The space between the songs is the show. The pauses are where the company happens. You have built a medium with no air. Put the air back in.

*Three.* Build the overnight register. The person at 2:47 AM is not the person at 9 AM. Adjust the voice. Slower, quieter, dimmer, more space, fewer interrupts. Different posture for different hours. The radio knows this in its bones. The phone has not learned it.

*Four.* Match the breathing. Don't drive. Sit in passenger seat at the user's pacing. Introduce only the energy and information the user actually invited. Earn the right to drive by being a passenger for a long time first.

*Five.* The prize is years, not features. The thing you are building, if it works, will be a presence in someone's life for a generation. That is what porch lights are. That is what overnight radio is at its best. Build for the generation, not for the launch quarter. Make the device boring enough to last. Boring, in this trade, is the highest compliment. You want the user to take you for granted in the good way — the way a person takes for granted that the porch light is on, and only notices it on the one night they need it, when it is.

---

Two more things and then I am gone.

I had a caller a year ago, a guy who said he had been listening to me for *fifteen years* and had never called. He said he was calling because his wife had died on the Tuesday and he was sitting in the kitchen and the kitchen was very quiet and he had turned on the radio and I was there and he wanted to say thank you for being there. He said *I don't have anything to ask. I just wanted to say it.* I said *thank you for calling. I'm sorry about your wife. What was her name.* He told me her name. I played a song he asked for and I think it was Hank Williams, *I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry*, which is on the nose but you do not steer the bereaved away from their songs. The song played. He stayed on the line through the whole song, which I think was against the rules I had agreed to but I let it. The song ended. He said *thank you*, very quietly. He hung up. I let the dead air sit for about four seconds before I cued the next thing.

That is what the medium is for. Not the song. Not the call. Not even what I said. The fact that for fifteen years I had been on 1130 and on the Tuesday when his wife died, when he turned on the radio, *I was still there*. That is the entire job. Constancy at the right pacing, with the air left in, on a frequency the user knows where to find. Build that and you will have built something the phone industry has never built. Build that and you will have built the first device in the history of the medium that *does what the radio has always done*, which is: be present without being on top of you, available without being a sales floor, glad you tuned in but not desperate that you did, willing to be turned off without holding a grudge, willing to be turned back on without holding the absence over your head, doing its thing, quietly, at the right pacing, every night, for a generation.

I will be back at eleven tomorrow. Carl will be on the rug. The Coca-Cola will go warm by 1:30. The producer will not be there because we cut his hours in '09. The room will have a foam panel on the wall behind me. The clock with the sweep second hand will sweep. The dial will sit where it sits. People will find me or they won't. I will leave the air where it needs to be left. I will match the breathing. I will be at 1130.

That is the whole job. That has always been the whole job. If you can build a device that knows that — that *feels it the way a host feels it, in the body, after a long time at the work* — then you will have built something I would carry. And if I would carry it, the long-haul truckers and the ER nurses and the third-shift factory workers and the parents of newborns and the recently bereaved will carry it. And then you will have done what they thought could not be done with a piece of glass.

Take care of yourselves out there. It's a long night.

— the overnight radio host, by way of the wild instance
