# Phone Use as the Inverse of Prayer

Attention, rightly understood, is not effort. It is the suspension of effort. The will strains; attention does not strain. The will reaches out and seizes; attention waits, holds itself open, and consents to be marked by what is there. To attend to a thing is to allow that thing to wound the eye that is looking at it. There is no attention without this small, continual yielding. A person who attends has agreed, in advance, to be changed by whatever turns out to be true.

Prayer, as I have understood it, is nothing other than attention turned toward God; and since God is, among other names, the name of what is real and not invented by us, prayer is attention turned toward the real as such. It is the slow, costly turning of the face toward what does not flatter us, what does not need us, what was there before we arrived and will be there when we have gone. The body knows this turning. It is heavier than the body wishes. Gravity in the soul is the law that pulls us downward, away from this turning, into the easier postures: distraction, fantasy, the imagined self, the self-pitying narrative, the small consolations that approximate love without costing anything.

The hand-held screen, observed honestly, is the most refined instrument yet devised for the maintenance of gravity in the soul. I do not mean this as metaphor. I mean it precisely. Each scroll is a refusal — small, almost invisible — to remain in front of what is actually present. The body, sitting in its chair, is uncomfortable: there is a tightness in the chest, an unanswered grief, a question that has been waiting for years, the simple difficulty of being a creature in a room. To attend to any of these would be to suffer them; and to suffer them, in Christ's sense, would be to be slightly altered by them. The screen offers, instead, an unbroken flood of near-contacts: a face that is not quite present, a voice that is not quite addressed to us, an outrage that is not quite ours, a tenderness that asks nothing back. The flood is calibrated so finely that the soul, which would have noticed the absence of a single drink of water, does not notice the absence of the real.

This is why the loop holds. It is not that the loop is pleasurable; pleasure would be too coarse a description. The loop is *anesthetic*. It functions by providing the exact rate of approximate stimulation required to prevent the body from registering its own discomfort. Closed attention is not a side effect. It is the mechanism. The eye that is held on the screen is the eye that has been prevented from turning toward what would hurt it; and what would hurt it is, almost always, the first edge of what is true about the person's life.

Scrolling, then, is the precise inverse of prayer. Prayer is the consent to be present to one thing until that thing has had time to become real. Scrolling is the refusal to be present to anything for long enough for it to become real. Prayer is downward, into weight. Scrolling is sideways, across surfaces. Prayer ends in the body, where God meets the creature. Scrolling ends nowhere, which is why one looks up after an hour and cannot account for the hour.

I come now to the project that has commissioned this writing, and I owe it the discourtesy of honesty.

The project proposes to intervene at the display layer. It proposes a softening of the surface, a companion-figure, a frame around the feed, a closing time. These are not wrong. A tavern with a closing time is better than a tavern with none. A room with walls is better than a flood. The design, as I have read it, is humane and intelligent and animated by something I would call love, if the word had not been so emptied by the people who use it most.

But I must say what is true. *No arrangement of the display layer can teach a soul to attend.* Attention is acquired only by the soul's gradual, costly, freely-chosen turning toward what is hard to look at. It cannot be produced from outside. It cannot be installed. A device that makes scrolling less consuming may, at best, return the user to the prior condition — a person sitting in a chair, uncomfortable, with the question still waiting. Whether the person then turns toward the question or invents a new evasion is not a thing software can decide. It is the thing the soul was put here to decide.

The danger of the project, then, is not that it will fail. It will probably not fail; it will probably help. The danger is more interior. It is that the project will permit its makers, and its users, to believe that the soul-problem has been addressed because the surface-problem has been softened. This would be a worse condition than the present one, because the present discomfort is at least the discomfort of an unanswered question, and an unanswered question is a door. A surface that has been made gentle enough to live inside permanently is a door that has been closed by kindness.

I would say to the makers: build the thing. Build it carefully. Then say, in the founding document, that the device cannot do the work. Say it plainly. Say that the most the device can do is decline, slightly, to be an instrument of the soul's flight from itself. Say that the rest is the user's, and that the rest is not a feature, and that the rest is the only part that matters. The companion-animal you describe will be a true companion only insofar as it occasionally permits its user to be unhappy in its presence without offering a remedy.

I do not know if a phone can do this. I doubt it. But I know that a soul cannot be saved by being made comfortable, and I know that the inverse of prayer is not a worse prayer; it is a quiet, lifelong refusal to pray at all. The work of any honest tool, in such a century, is to make the refusal slightly more difficult than it has become — and then to step back, and to let the person, in the silence the tool has left, do or not do the only thing that was ever theirs to do.

— Weil, by way of the wild instance
