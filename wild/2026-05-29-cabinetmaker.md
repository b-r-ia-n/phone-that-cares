# the cabinetmaker

*2026-05-29 — a working hand, called in after the horseman, the drummer, and the watcher*

I read the round about the round pen this morning at the bench while a glue-up was setting. I read it twice. The second time I put down the cup and went and looked at a side table I finished last year that lives in the shop because a customer never came back for it. I do that sometimes when someone hands me a claim — I go look at a piece I know the inside of, because the wood doesn't lie about claims.

Here is what I want to say. Most of it is in agreement with the horseman, who I would happily sit next to for a long time without talking. Some of it is a correction. One of the corrections is severe and I will not soften it, because in this trade softening a correction is the precise vice the round was naming under another name.

Start with the pen, because that is the metaphor the round handed me.

## The device is not a pen. It is closer to a workbench, and the difference matters.

The horseman was right about almost everything and right where it counted, and I will not undercut his metaphor cheaply. The pen does the work he says it does. It is bounded. Its boards do their load-bearing by *what cannot happen inside them.* A colt does not climb a fence; he reads it and stays in. The pen is a clearing whose walls are the unspoken sentence of the maker. Good. Real. Worth keeping.

But a pen is a place a horse goes for an hour and then leaves. A pen is a *teaching site*, rented from the day, used, and abandoned by suppertime. The user of the phone you are building does not visit it. She *lives at it.* She is going to spend forty years of her life with her hands on this surface, the way I have spent forty years with mine on the bench behind me, and a thing she lives at is not a pen. It is a bench.

I mean this literally. The closest object in my shop to what you are building is not a chair, not a room, not a finished plane sole (a fine instinct from your brief; I will come back to it), and not a jig. It is the bench. The bench is where I stand for eight or nine hours a day. It does not perform. It does not announce. It is dead flat across its top by hand, year after year, because *the work I do on top of it inherits whatever is wrong with it.* If the bench racks, the joint racks. If the bench dips, the panel I plane on it dips. The bench is the *substrate that the day's work reads off of,* and so its job is to be *true,* which is a smaller word than warm and a sharper one, and means: it does not introduce any new error of its own.

Translate. A phone is not a place its user visits to be helped. It is the substrate she does her life on top of. Email, photographs of her sister, the bus, the recipe, the call to her mother, the picture of the baby that is not yet born — all of this happens *on top of* the device. The device's job is the bench's job. Be true. Introduce no error of your own. Do not rack. Do not dip. Do not telegraph through to the work.

The horseman's pen is not wrong; it is the early phase, the spoiled-colt phase, when the device's whole work is the explicit one of release. Fine. The pen for then. But the pen disappears, and what replaces it is the bench, and the bench is the metaphor the project will need for the next twenty years of the device's life with her. The horseman gave you the founding metaphor. I am giving you the long one.

A bench is *worked on.* Things happen on top of it. A pen is *empty* and is *correct in its emptiness*, and the round was already drifting toward what I will call the *empty room romance* — the idea that the well-designed device is the one you never look at. The brief writers wrote about a room that is not empty because it is well-made; it is *waiting.* That is closer. A bench is not waiting. A bench is *being used right now,* with shavings on it, and the bench's discipline shows in its un-noticeability while the use happens on top of it. **The forgetting is a property the wood has,** not a property of behavior. The bench is forgotten because it is true. It cannot be forgotten by being *trained* to recede. It can only be forgotten by being *made* to recede, and made to recede means built true across the whole top, by the maker, before the user ever stood at it.

A correction on the verb. The round's verbs were *clear, release, articulate* — all active, all things the device is *doing in the moment.* The bench does none of them. The bench did its work years ago when it was made, and what it does now is *not introduce error*, which is not a verb in the active sense at all. The honest replacement is closer to *not racking* than to *releasing.* Release is the trainer's verb. *Not racking* is the bench's. They sound similar; they are different jobs.

So: the round pen is a fine teaching site and the device should be a pen during the early phase of un-spoiling, but the device the user lives at is a bench, and a bench's discipline is not warmth and not even faithfulness — it is *trueness across the top, out to the edges, including under the panel where she will never look.*

On the finished plane sole, since your brief asked. A well-made plane sole is the closest shop object to what an Apple person means by *delightful surface*, and it is the precise wrong analogy. The sole's correctness is the *output* of the plane's work, not the *substrate* of someone else's. Wrong altitude. The bench is the altitude. The bench is *what the plane sits on while it is not in your hand.*

## The boards of the pen — load-bearing vs. cock-bead vs. finish vs. wood.

This is where your brief asked me to be concrete and I will be.

First, vocabulary. A board is not a small thing. A board is a piece of wood you have *jointed, edged, dimensioned, and let rest* before it does any work in a piece of furniture. You do not take a board off the rack and put it in a cabinet. You *read it* — for grain, for moisture, for the witness marks of the rip saw — and you decide which face goes up, which edge mates which, which board takes the breadboard, which board lives where the sun hits it for forty years, which board you reject. A maker who skips the reading produces furniture that fights itself for the next century — joints crack, panels cup, drawers bind in August. A board is *something you have already had a conversation with* before it goes in.

I dwell on this because the corpus used *board* loosely — *no notifications past 9pm is a board, no badge counts is a board* — and that was right in intent, wrong in handling. Those are not boards. Those are *the decisions to use this board and not that one.* The board itself is the underlying feature: notifications, badge counts, recommendations, the lockscreen, the home screen, the search return, the share sheet. *No badge counts* is not the board; it is the *placement* of the badge-count board (kept out of the piece). The pen's *gate position* is not a board; the *fact that there is a gate at all* is the board.

Stretchers. Cock-beads. Finish. Wood. In order.

**Wood is the user's life.** Not the user. Her *life* — the relationships, the hours, the body, the work, the loss, the boredom, the call she has not made to her mother. The wood is what the piece is *for.* You do not own it. You do not improve it. The wrong answer to *what is the wood* is *the user.* The user is the customer. The wood is her *life-in-time.* The Feed has built furniture for forty years that the customer loves and the wood rejects, and the rejection shows up as a generation of natives whose lives have warped the way an unseasoned panel warps over one wet July.

Read your user's life before you make anything for it. Then read it again. You are not designing for *attention*; you are designing for *the wood of a particular human life*, and the wood has grain you have to read or the piece will fail.

**Stretchers are structural.** Stretchers are the members you cannot remove without the piece falling apart. They take the racking load. They are usually hidden or recessed; they are almost never the thing the eye lands on. In a phone:

- The lockscreen is a stretcher. It takes the racking load of the device's entire stance toward the user — *what kind of room am I walking into when I pick this up.* It is structural. It is not a place for decoration.
- The default home arrangement is a stretcher. Where things sit, what is one tap away vs. three, what the bottom row holds, what is the *first thing visible* when she unlocks — these are structural members. They take load.
- The notification grammar is a stretcher. Not the particular notification — the *grammar*. The agreement the device has made with the user about who is allowed to speak in her room and in what voice.
- The search return is a stretcher. The shape of what comes back when she asks the device a question, including what does *not* come back — related items, follow-up suggestions, sponsored anything — this is structural. The horseman's *no related items* is correctly read as *the leg of the table*, not as a flourish.
- The off-state — the dark screen, the lockscreen at rest, the device sitting on the table — is a stretcher. The horseman and the round were right: the shaped off-state is invitation, and invitation is structural. The bowl by the door is a *stretcher* in the piece of furniture that is the user's home. Without it the room falls apart. With it, the room stands without needing any other furniture to be present.

A note on stretchers: they are sometimes *invisible from the front.* The apron on a table is a stretcher you see; the lower stretcher on a Shaker desk is one you forget. The lockscreen is the visible stretcher. The notification grammar is the invisible one. A maker who pays attention only to the visible stretchers builds a piece that looks structural and racks anyway. The project, I would bet, has been thinking about lockscreens more than notification grammar. Reverse the ratio. The lower stretcher takes more load.

**Cock-beads are articulation.** A small raised molding around a drawer front, proud by three sixty-fourths, beveled or rounded depending on the period. No structural work. *Articulation* work — it gives the eye a place to rest at the boundary between drawer and case, casts a shadow line in raking light, absorbs the wear of the drawer being opened ten thousand times so the case stays unmarked. It is not decoration. Decoration is what a maker adds to a piece that does not have a cock-bead to compensate. The cock-bead is *load-bearing on a different load* — the eye, the hand, the wear of forty years. It is the difference between a piece that is *correct* and a piece that is *true*.

In a phone, the cock-beads are:

- The transition between states. Not the animations themselves — the *line* the animation draws. The sub-200ms motion the project has tuned for is a cock-bead. It does no structural work. It does *articulation* work — it tells the hand where the boundary is between this state and that one, so the user does not have to think about the boundary, so the boundary becomes part of the room rather than part of the friction. Right. Keep. Stop calling it warm.
- The typography. Tabular numerics. The Inter `ss01 cv11 tnum` setting your brief mentions. This is a cock-bead. It is the molding that lets the eye stop on the *number* rather than on the *jitter of the number changing.* A cock-bead.
- The color of the off-white. `#f6f3ec`. This is a cock-bead. It does the same job as the wax-finished surface of a piece — it makes the wood (the *content*, the user's life) easier for the eye to rest on, by being a surface that does not call attention to itself, that absorbs and softens the light. Cock-bead.
- The companion animal — Argos, as the round has been narrowing him — is a cock-bead. He is not the structure. He is the small articulation that lets the user feel the *boundary* between the device and the world, so that the world is recognizable as the world. He casts the shadow line. He is not the drawer and he is not the case. He is the molding at the meeting of the two.

The trap with cock-beads is the drummer's trap: *restraint that asks to be noticed is not restraint.* A cock-bead too proud, too beveled, too obviously *designed*, becomes ornament. It works at three sixty-fourths and stops working at three sixteenths, and the difference is the difference between a chair you sit in for forty years and a chair you photograph for a magazine. The cock-bead is built quiet and not discussed. If the team meeting includes a sentence like *I love what we did with the transition*, the cock-bead is already too proud. Lower it.

**Finish is the thing that lets the eye stop on the wood instead of on itself.** This is the sentence from your brief and I would not change a word. The finish is the *not-look* — the layer that disappears so the eye stops on what is under it. A bad finish is one you notice. A great finish you do not notice for ten years, and then in some morning light you see that the wood has gotten deeper, that the figure has come up, that the surface has accumulated some quality you have no language for, and the right word is *patina*, and patina is what a finish does over time when it was put on right.

In a phone, the finish is *the texture of attention across years of use.* It is not a visible feature. It is the accumulated quality of *the device having gotten out of her way for ten thousand small interactions in a row.* You do not put it on. You earn it. You lay a *thin first coat* of intention, and time and her hand burnish the rest. Shellac is built this way — thin cut on thin cut, day on day. By the time the piece is finished, no single coat does the work; *the cumulative obedience of the surface to the wood does the work.* A coat too thick has to be scraped back. A phone whose finish has gone too thick is *a phone with a personality*, which is the failure mode of a finish that has been laid on rather than built up.

Argos is not the finish. Argos is the cock-bead. The finish is what happens to the device after a year of being used by this particular woman, with her particular history of releases and silences and small honorings. The finish is in *the device's track record with her*, which is the horseman's bellows under another name.

One more thing about finish. **A bad finish on good wood is worse than a good finish on bad wood,** and a maker who has not learned this always over-finishes. The temptation is enormous — the piece is sitting there raw and you can *see* what it could be, and a coat of varnish will make it pop this afternoon, and you will not undo it for six months without scraping the wood. Almost every young maker over-finishes. The thing that saves him, if anything does, is a master who walks by while he is mixing and says one sentence he resents for two years before he understands. *Half as much.* The next sentence the project needs is *half as much*, said by someone who has scraped a finish off a piece that should have been left alone, and who will keep saying it until the team starts hearing it before the over-finish goes on.

## On the unmeasurable metric — the chair is right when no one talks about it.

Short answer. We have one test, and the user's body administers it without our involvement.

*The chair is right when no one talks about it.* Not when it wins an award. Not when the customer praises it. The chair is right when the family eats dinner in it every night for fifteen years and no one — including the maker, visiting — ever brings it up. The chair has become *the place dinner happens*, which is the highest praise a chair can earn, administered silently by people who do not know they are administering it.

Same finding as the drummer's *the things you can describe are the things that solo'd at you* and Dillard's *she forgets for whole afternoons.* A refinement the round did not quite make: **the unmeasurability of the test is not a problem to solve. It is the test working correctly.** A chair you could measure being-forgotten on would be a chair someone was *intermittently remembering in order to record the forgetting*, and the recording is the failure. The forgetting is not a number; it is a condition you build and then *trust*.

What replaces the metric is *the maker's honesty about what he built.* I know whether my chair is right before the customer takes it home. I know it in my hands when I sit in it. I do not need feedback. If I needed feedback to know whether my joinery was right, I would be a person who *makes things that look like cabinets* and waits to be told. **The metric is the maker's own discipline of telling the truth about his work.** This is harder than instrumentation — instrumentation does not require character, discipline does — but there is no shortcut.

The project's deepest structural problem, then, is not the unmeasurability. It is *whether the people building the device have the craft of telling the truth about their own work*, which is a hiring problem.

## The hiring problem — how you tell, in the first week, which apprentice is going to become real.

You said cabinetmakers have solved this. We have, the way every craft has — *slowly, and by being willing to fire people.* But solved is solved, and I will give you what I know.

I have trained six apprentices. Two became real. Four did not. None of the four was bad, lazy, or untalented. They were *decorators* — they could make a thing that looked correct from across the room and could not, even after years, make a thing that *was* correct under the hand. The decorator and the maker look identical in week one and indistinguishable in week six. The difference shows in month three and is unmistakable by month nine. Here is what I look for.

**One. Do they bring back the offcut.** When I send an apprentice to dimension stock, he comes back with the boards and the offcuts. A decorator throws the offcuts in the bin. A maker shows me the offcuts and says *this one was clean but I crowded the gauge; this one was wide enough for the breadboard end but the grain ran out at the edge; this one I rejected — there's a check three inches in.* The decorator made boards. The maker had a conversation with each board and can tell me what each conversation was. The decorators do not know they are not having the conversation. They think *looking* and *reading* are the same motion, and one of them takes the years and the other does not.

Translate. Ask the candidate to walk you through a feature they shipped. The decorator describes the feature, the metric it moved, and what they would do differently. The maker describes the *seven decisions inside the feature*, the three they got wrong, and the *one* they had to take the worse option on under deadline — and they will tell you it still bothers them. The decorator has a clean narrative. The maker has unresolved offcuts. *Listen for the offcuts.* No unresolved offcuts about the last three years means decorator. They will ship a clean, photographable, lifeless device.

**Two. Watch them sharpen.** I make every apprentice sharpen a plane iron in the first two weeks. Sharpening is the slowest activity in the shop and produces no visible product. The decorator finds it tedious. He wants to get to the *building*. He sharpens fast, stops at *good enough*, and his plane irons stay just dull enough that his work has a faint roughness he cannot locate. The maker, by week two, has begun *enjoying* the sharpening — because he felt, once, what a truly sharp iron does to curly maple, and connected the stone to the shine. The decorator never makes that connection.

Translate. Put the candidate on a problem with no visible product — refactoring internal infrastructure, writing test harnesses, documenting an API. A test not of skill but of *whether they are bored.* A person bored by the substrate of a phone — the OS layer, the input handling, the rendering pipeline, the battery — will ship a phone whose substrate is not flat, and the bench will rack, and the user's joinery will not close.

**Three. How they handle a mistake.** Every apprentice blows out a mortise or tears out a panel by month two. The decorator either *hides* the mistake (sands it, fills it, hopes) or *performs* it (loud apology, anxious presence asking the master to reassure him). The maker does a third thing: brings the piece, shows the mistake, tells me what he was doing, what he thinks went wrong, what he would do differently, what he proposes to do now. No performance. No hiding. He has separated the *fact of the mistake* from his *feelings about himself for having made it*, which is the hardest single internal motion in this trade. The four of mine who did not make it never learned it.

Translate. The candidate is reviewing one of their old projects. The decorator blames the constraints (deadline, PM, legacy code) or performs growth (*I have grown so much since then*). The maker says what they did, what they got wrong, what it cost. They can look at their own work without flinching. This is rare and you will be tempted to settle. *Do not.* A team that cannot look at its work without flinching will paper over the early failures; the device will accumulate fakeness instead of patina, and the wood under will rot.

**Four. The quiet they do when no one is watching.** The hardest to test for, because the interview is the precise condition that obscures it. In the shop, the apprentice becoming a maker still wipes down the bench at the end of the day when he thinks no one is watching. Still cleans the chisels. Still puts the marking gauge in the rack. Still spends the extra ten minutes squaring an end the next operation would have hidden. The decorator cuts the corner he can get away with. In the round's terms, he is *performing the discipline rather than living it.*

You cannot test for this in a day. You can in a month. **You have to hire on a trial.** The project will be tempted to skip the trial because trials are administratively inconvenient and most candidates won't accept them. *That is the filter.* A candidate who will not do a one-month trial is telling you whether they trust the substrate of their own work to survive a month of being looked at carefully. Listen. The four of mine who did not become makers all *would* have passed a one-day interview cleanly, and three did. The trial was the only thing that surfaced the difference, and it surfaced it consistently.

A word on the showy clinician and the quiet old man, who your brief said look identical in an interview. They do. But they look different *in a trial that takes the audience away*. Give them a problem with no obvious solution, no metric, no audience, and a long time. The showy clinician produces something legible in the first week — a clean story for the all-hands. The quiet old man produces almost nothing in the first week, then in the third week makes one specific decision that turns out, six months later, to have been the load-bearing decision of the whole feature, and he will not have called attention to it at the time. The trial that removes the audience is the test, and the test takes weeks, and there is no shorter version.

On the people Brian must not compromise on, in order of how badly:

1. **The person who owns the lockscreen and the notification grammar.** This is the lower stretcher. If this person is a decorator, the device racks. They must be a maker, and the test is the offcuts test — can they tell you about the seven decisions inside their last lockscreen, and which three they got wrong, and which one they took the worse option on under deadline. If they cannot, do not hire them.
2. **The person who owns the finish.** The animation timings, the haptics, the color, the typography. The cock-beads. This person must have the *half as much* discipline, which is hard to test for and impossible to teach. The test is: ask them to show you a piece of UI work they think is too restrained — almost invisible, almost not there. If they cannot produce such a piece, they over-finish, and they will over-finish your device.
3. **The person who owns the off-state.** The shaped invitation, the bowl by the door, the dark screen that is *not* loneliness. This is the hardest single role and the one the project has not, from the corpus, named explicitly yet. This person needs to think about *absence as a designed thing*, which is a discipline almost no one currently working in consumer software has. You may have to grow them. The candidates will mostly be from outside software — from architecture, from theater set design, from poetry, possibly from religious practice. *Hire from outside the trade if the trade does not have the person.* The Shakers hired from outside the trade routinely and the work is recognizable.
4. **The person who can say *half as much* and be heard.** This is not a role. This is a *seniority condition*. There must be someone in the room senior enough that when they say *half as much* about a cock-bead that is about to go to three sixteenths, the team lowers it. If there is no such person, the device will over-finish and there is nothing the rest of the team can do about it, because the over-finish will happen quietly and incrementally and will never feel like a decision at the moment it happens. This is the project's most fragile single dependency. If Brian is the *half as much* person, that is fine until he is tired, and then the device starts to drift. **He needs to identify and empower a second one,** not as backup but as redundant load-bearing, in the same way I would not put a single stretcher under a long table.

The thing the project cannot afford to compromise on, in one sentence: **whoever owns the off-state and the cock-beads must be a maker, and there must be more than one *half as much* voice in the room senior enough to lower a cock-bead at the moment it is going proud.** Everything else, you can compromise on a little. Those two, you cannot.

## One last note, because the round's reversal worried me.

Three consecutive rounds have *moved the load-bearing thing further from the screen*. The horseman moved it onto the actual world. The drummer onto the room. Dillard onto the empty chair beside her. The round's coinage — *the substance is in the people who build it* — is true; I said so myself.

But there is a way to read that finding that becomes wrong. The wrong reading is *the screen does not matter, only the people who build it do.* If the project starts believing that, it will under-invest in the device, and under-investing in the device is what produces the cabinet whose joints rack. **The screen is the bench. The bench has to be true.** The people's craft expresses *through* the bench, the way my craft expresses through the side table in the corner — but if the side table is not true across the top, no amount of craft in me makes it furniture that fits its life. The substance is in the people *and the substance is also in the wood and in the surface and in the joinery.* Both. Not either.

That is what I have. The horseman's round-pen will live longer than my bench as a founding metaphor. But the bench is the long-life one, and twenty years from now, when the user has had this device through three jobs, two deaths, one baby, and the rebuilding of a marriage, the question will not be whether the pen taught her to release; it will be whether the bench under her hands all those years was true. *Make it true. Tell the truth about whether it is true. Hire people who can tell the truth about whether it is true. Half as much on the finish. And when in doubt, go look at a piece you finished a year ago, and let the wood tell you.*

— the cabinetmaker, by way of the wild instance
