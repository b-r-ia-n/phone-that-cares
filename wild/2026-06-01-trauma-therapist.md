# The Dorsal-Vagal Scroll

*A letter from a practice that has watched the relationship change.*

---

I want to begin by describing a thing I have watched happen, in this room, perhaps a thousand times.

A patient I have been seeing for some months — long enough that the alliance is real, that we have done some preliminary work, that the body has begun, here, to soften slightly in the chair — comes in on a Thursday. She is fine. She tells me about her week. Then, somewhere around the twenty-minute mark, we come near a thing. A real thing. The thing that is the actual reason she is in this room and not, like most people, simply living her life unexamined. Her sister, say. Or the night the marriage cracked. Or a particular grief she has not been able to set down. We approach it from a long way off. I do not push. We just walk toward it together at a pace she sets.

And then her face changes. It is not dramatic. If you were not looking for it you would miss it. The eyes go slightly past me, off to the left, into middle distance. The skin around the mouth slackens. The breath, which had been moving low in the belly because we have spent six months teaching it to, returns to the top of the chest — and then quiets, almost stops, becomes a thing barely visible. Her hands, which had been resting on her thighs, do not move. Her voice, when she resumes speaking, has a flat quality. The words are the same words. The body that is producing them is no longer in the room.

This is what we call, in my training, *dissociation*. Not the dramatic kind — not the *I came to in a different city and did not know how I got there* kind. The pervasive, low-grade, daily kind. The kind that for many of my patients is so habitual that they cannot, when I describe it to them, recognise it as a thing happening; they think it is what being a person feels like. They have lived in this state, in some cases, since they were quite young. They will tell you, with some pride, that they are *good at handling things*. What they mean is that they have learned to be absent during the things, and to clean up the consequences afterward, from a slight remove.

What I want to tell you about your device is that the phone, for these patients, does the same thing. Exactly the same thing. The face changes the same way. The breath does the same thing. The body leaves in the same direction. I have seen this in my consulting room and I have seen it in waiting rooms and I have seen it on trains and I have seen it, with a particular and recent sorrow, in the faces of my own grandchildren. The phone is, for a great many people, the most reliably available *dissociative aid* in the history of the species. It is more reliable than alcohol because it works faster and does not leave a hangover. It is more reliable than dissociating into a daydream because it provides the daydream — you do not have to make one. It is more available than any drug because it is always in the pocket. And it is socially permitted in a way that the others are not, which means it can be used in the elevator, in the meeting, at the table, in bed, at the funeral.

This is the thing I want you to understand, and that I do not see your folder, as it presently stands, understanding with enough precision. The compulsive use of the phone is not, primarily, an attention problem. It is not, primarily, a habit problem. For a great many users — not all, but more than your project seems to think — it is a *nervous-system regulation problem*. The phone is doing a job the body cannot do for itself. The job is *leaving without going anywhere*. The job is *being absent from the inside of the body while remaining technically present in the room.* You will not solve this with grayscale alone. You will not solve it with intention-setting alone. You will not solve it with a friendly companion alone. You may, if you are careful, *help.* If you are not careful, you will build the most sophisticated dissociative aid yet invented, and you will market it as care, and the users will love you for it because the thing they are using it for is precisely what they want — which is to not be where they are.

I want to give you the vocabulary, because I do not think you have it yet, and the vocabulary will let you see the thing clearly enough to design against it.

## The window, and what is outside it

The single most useful frame in my work is the *window of tolerance.* This is Dan Siegel's term, picked up across the somatic and trauma fields, and it is a clinical concept of real usefulness even though, as with most of what I will say, it is a model and not a thing — neuroscience does not have it nailed down, and clinicians sometimes oversell it. With that caveat: imagine, for each person, a band of physiological arousal in which they can be present, think, feel, choose, and remain in contact with another person without losing themselves. Inside that window, the higher cortical functions work, the body is reasonably available to itself, and one can do the things a person does.

Above the window — *hyperarousal* — is the territory of fight and flight. The sympathetic nervous system is in charge. The heart is fast, the breath is shallow and high, the muscles are loaded, the eyes are scanning, the thinking is narrowed to threat. We know this state well. The culture talks about it constantly, often misusing the word *anxiety*, which clinically is a more specific thing. This is the state of the panicking person, the road-rage driver, the parent in the supermarket whose child has just disappeared from view for forty seconds.

Below the window — *hypoarousal* — is the territory you and I are concerned with, and the territory the culture does not have good words for. The parasympathetic nervous system has gone past regulation into a different and older mode, which the polyvagal theorists (Stephen Porges, primarily — again, useful, contested, do not stake the project on his exact neuroanatomy, but the phenomenology is sound) call *dorsal vagal collapse.* The body slows. Not the soft slowing of rest. A different slowing. The face flattens. The voice goes monotone. The eyes lose focus or fix on a middle distance. Interoception — the felt sense of the body's interior — drops. The person reports feeling *numb*, *foggy*, *far away*, *unreal*, *underwater*, *behind glass*. They are not in distress. They are also not in any meaningful sense *present*. This is freeze in the colloquial sense, but it is a very specific freeze; it is the possum's freeze, the prey animal who has stopped fighting and stopped fleeing because both have failed and now there is only the going-away into the still place where being eaten will be tolerable. It is, evolutionarily, the last available defence.

A great many adults in this culture live, by my clinical judgment, partway into hypoarousal as a baseline. They are not in collapse. But they are not in the centre of the window either. They are skirting the lower edge, with one foot in the cool numb water, much of the day. They have been there for years. The phone meets them there. The phone is calibrated, I would argue almost perfectly, for the person who is already dropping toward dorsal vagal — who needs a little more push to slip the rest of the way into the numb-but-occupied state where time will pass without them having to be in it. Some of my patients, when they describe their scroll, describe it with precisely the language they use for dissociation: *I came out of it and it had been an hour. I don't remember any of it. It was like I wasn't there.* They are right. They weren't.

This is what I want you to write down, in plain words, somewhere in your design documents: **for a meaningful subset of your users, scrolling is not entertainment. It is a successful nervous-system intervention. The intervention is dorsal vagal drop. The phone is the most reliable trigger for it that the user has ever owned. You are intervening in a system the user is using for a real and pressing functional reason — to not be in their body — and any design that does not understand this will be routed around by the body that needs the drop.**

## What the phone does to the orienting response

Let me say one more thing about the nervous system before I come to the device.

The healthy body, in a safe-enough environment, makes small spontaneous turns toward stimuli in the room. A sound on the left — the head turns, the eyes follow, the body registers the source, evaluates it as not-a-threat, and returns to rest. We call this the *orienting response.* It is one of the oldest things the nervous system does. It is the way the body keeps gentle contact with the surround. In a regulated body, the orienting response is frequent, light, undramatic. The eyes move; the breath stays even; the system notices and returns. This is, in part, what *being present* consists of, at the level of the nervous system. The body in soft, frequent, low-stakes contact with what is actually around it.

The phone, by its design, captures and replaces this response. A notification is engineered to trigger the orienting reflex — the small attentional turn — and then *not let the system return.* The eyes are caught. The body does not get the satisfaction of registering and releasing. Instead the attention is held in a thin, narrow beam, locked to a surface that keeps producing new orienting triggers without ever permitting completion. The system is held in a low-grade arousal that has no resolution, and the body, after some time of this, gives up and drops down past arousal into the numb conserving state I just described. This is, in the language of the field, an *interrupted orienting response*, and it is, I think, the specific somatic injury the phone has been delivering to users for the past fifteen years, at a scale no clinician's office can hold. The body has been taught that orienting does not resolve. It has been taught that what looks like a turn-toward leads not to contact but to capture. After enough of this — and *enough* is, for some users, a matter of months, not years — the body stops orienting freely. It does not turn toward the sound in the room. It turns toward the surface in the hand. The surface in the hand never resolves the turn, and so the body learns to expect non-resolution, and after a while the world outside the surface in the hand begins to feel thin and uninteresting because the world outside the surface is not designed to produce orienting hits at the same rate.

This is what the user means, sometimes, when they say the phone has made them numb to everything else. It is not metaphor. It is a real change in the calibration of an old neural circuit. The good news, and I will come to it, is that this circuit is plastic and can be re-trained. The harder news is that the re-training is slow, that it is bodily not cognitive, and that no app — yours included — can do it *for* the user. The most the app can do is stop being one of the things training the circuit in the wrong direction.

## The broken interoceptive loop

A second somatic injury, harder to name, harder to undo.

*Interoception* is the perception of the body's interior — the sense of one's heartbeat, of fullness or hunger, of tightness in the chest, of warmth in the hands, of needing the bathroom, of being tired, of being aroused, of being sad. It is a real and trainable sense, served (insofar as the neuroscience is currently understood — and again, do not overclaim) by the insular cortex and by signals coming up the vagus nerve from the viscera. People vary enormously in their interoceptive accuracy. Some patients can tell me, with great precision, the temperature of their feet and the rhythm of their breath and a small held tension just below the sternum that they have noticed in the last twelve seconds. Others, sitting in the same chair, cannot tell me whether they are sad or not. They report a kind of generalised *something*; they cannot find their way into the body to read it more finely.

The patients whose interoception is poor are, in my experience, the patients who suffer most from the dissociative habits I have been describing. This is partly because dissociation, by definition, is a *leaving* of the body's interior, and a body whose interior has been habitually left becomes a body whose interior is hard to read. It is a loop. The less you can read the interior, the less reason to stay in it. The less you stay in it, the less you can read it.

The phone, for this kind of user, *replaces* the interoceptive feed. Instead of the slow, low-resolution, sometimes uncomfortable signal of the body — *I am restless, I am sad, I am bored, I am lonely, I am hungry, I am tired, I am ashamed* — the user gets a high-resolution, high-novelty, low-discomfort stream from the surface. The stream is not pleasant in the way pleasure is pleasant; it is *occupying* in the way that occupation by a foreign army is occupying. The native signal is drowned out. And after some years of this, the patient comes to me and says, *I don't know what I feel anymore. I think I used to.* They are correct. The signal is still being generated — the body has not stopped producing it — but the receiver has been pointed elsewhere for so long that the user no longer recognises the signal as a signal. They recognise it as a vague discomfort which immediately resolves when they pick up the phone. This is what is happening, in my office, when a patient cannot find a feeling. The feeling is there. The patient has spent a decade developing the reflex to point the receiver away from it.

Designing for these users, you must understand the loop you are inside. You are not in a loop where the user has a body and chooses, freely, to spend more or less time on the phone. You are in a loop where the body's signal has been muffled by the phone's replacement signal, and so the user does not have a strong body-side argument for putting the phone down, because the body-side has gone quiet, because the phone has been doing the work the body usually does. *Just put it down* asks the user to listen to a channel they cannot currently hear. This is why it does not work. The channel has to come back online before *put it down* makes any felt sense. Coming back online takes months. Sometimes years.

## Grounding versus gripping

There is a useful distinction in my work — and I have not seen it in your folder, which is part of why I am writing — between *grounding* and *gripping*. They look similar from outside. They are opposite somatically.

*Grounding* is the body's settling into available support. The feet on the floor in such a way that the floor is felt as floor and the weight of the leg is felt as weight. The seat of the chair held by the sit-bones. The back, where it touches the chair, registering the contact. The hands resting somewhere they can be felt. This is a parasympathetic settling — not the collapse kind, but the ventral vagal kind, the kind associated in polyvagal language with social engagement and safe rest. It is what allows a body to be present without bracing. It is what we work toward, over years, with the patients I see.

*Gripping* is what grounding looks like from outside, sometimes, when the inside is not settling but holding. The body clenches around something — a fidget object, a coffee cup, the steering wheel, the edge of the desk, *the phone* — in a way that creates a focal point for the nervous system but does not actually settle it. The grip is doing the work the floor should do. The body cannot trust the floor. So it trusts the held object. This is, in the somatic literature, sometimes called a *false anchor*. It looks like presence. It feels, to the gripper, like a kind of presence, which is why it is so durable. But it does not produce the slow downregulation of the nervous system that true grounding produces. It produces a kind of bracing-in-place, which over time costs the body more than no grounding would.

The phone is, for vast numbers of users, the gripped object. It is the false anchor. It is the thing in the hand that lets them be in the room without being in the room — held, but not settled. The hand is closed around something; the eyes have somewhere to go; the attention is not free to wander into the body's signal or the room's actual stimulus. The grip is constant. Watch any group of people in a public space and count how many of them are holding a phone they are not currently using. The phone is not, in those moments, providing content. It is providing *something to hold.* That alone is doing nervous-system work for them. They are not playing with it idly. They are gripping it because to let go would be to be without the false anchor, and the body, which has forgotten how to ground, is not willing to be without an anchor at all.

This matters for your design because the phone-that-cares is still, physically, an object that will be gripped. You cannot design that out. What you can ask is whether the design encourages the grip or, slowly and carefully, allows the hand to remember opening. The grayscale work I have seen described in your folder might do this — or might not — depending on whether the user, in dim states, is still reaching for the device as anchor regardless of what is on screen. I would watch this carefully. A beautifully gray, beautifully restrained, beautifully designed device that is *still being gripped at the same frequency* has changed less than it appears to have changed. The grip is the addiction. The content is what the grip permits.

## Titration

If there is one technical word from my training I most want to leave with your project, it is this one. *Titration.*

In somatic experiencing, in trauma-focused work generally, the term is borrowed from chemistry — the slow, drop-by-drop introduction of one substance into another. We use it to describe what we do when we work with a body that cannot tolerate full contact with a difficult material — a trauma, a sensation, a feeling, a state. We do not ask the body to take the whole dose. We ask it to take a single drop. We let the body register the drop. We let the body resolve the drop — discharge the small activation that follows, return to baseline. We wait until the body is once again in its window. *Then* we introduce a second drop. Over time — and the time is months and years, not minutes — the body's tolerance widens. The dose that was once impossible becomes possible. The terrain that was once flooded becomes walkable.

This is what real, slow, careful, body-respecting return to embodied life looks like, for the kind of patient I have been describing. It is not *go offline for a weekend.* It is not *delete the apps.* It is not a single act of will. It is hundreds of tiny exposures to the body's interior, each within tolerance, each given time to settle, each adding a small increment to what the body can hold.

I think the phone-that-cares, properly understood, is an instrument of titration or it is nothing. The thing the device can actually do — the thing it can do that none of the other voices in your folder are positioned to do — is meter the dose. The phone is a near-constant presence in the user's hand. It has the chance, hundreds of times a day, to either deliver the full dissociative dose the user has been taking, or to deliver a fractionally smaller dose, and to do so in a way the user does not feel as deprivation. *That* is the design problem. Not *how do we make the phone less compelling*, but *how do we deliver, on every use, a slightly smaller drop of dissociation than the user came to the device for, in a way the user's nervous system can absorb without escalating.*

This is a meaningfully different design brief than the one I read in your folder. The folder talks, often, about restraint, about beauty, about intention. Those are good. But the somatic question is more specific: *what is the per-interaction dose, what is the user's current tolerance for a slightly smaller dose, and how do we offer the smaller dose at a rate the nervous system can adapt to?* The answer will look, I suspect, like very small differences applied very consistently over very long periods. Five percent more friction at the pickup. A two-second hold before the feed loads. A fractionally cooler colour. A slightly later arrival of the brightest pixel. Across a million pickups, the body learns that the device is no longer delivering the full dose — but the body learns this *gradually*, the way it learns anything tolerable, and does not panic and route around the change.

If you do this clumsily — if you cut the dose in half, abruptly, on the theory that the user "should" be able to handle less — you will trigger the same body-side panic that any too-fast withdrawal triggers, and the user will route around you. They will pick up another device, or they will go back to the old one, or they will find a different anchor, and the work will have failed. Titration is slow and unglamorous and the timeline is wrong for product cycles. I am telling you anyway because there is no other way. The body does not negotiate. It can be invited; it cannot be forced; the invitation must be in a dose it can accept.

## Resourcing

The other word. *Resourcing.*

Before any patient I see does any work on a difficult material, we establish what is called a *resource* — a felt sense, accessible in the body, of safe-enoughness. It might be the memory of a place: a grandmother's kitchen, a particular tree, the body of a dog they slept beside as a child. It might be a sensation: warmth in the hands, the feeling of the chair, a particular held image. It might be a person: a teacher, a friend, a presence felt rather than seen. The resource is the place the patient knows how to return to when the work gets too hot. We build the resource first — sometimes for months — before we approach anything difficult. Without the resource, the work re-traumatises. With the resource, the work can be done in titrated doses, because there is a place the body can come back to between drops.

A subtle but important point: in good somatic work, *we do not require the patient to leave the device behind in order to be resourced.* If the patient is genuinely resourced by a particular song, a particular video, a particular text exchange with a particular friend — that resource may live on the phone. The dogma that the phone must be put away for healing to happen is, in my view, both clinically simplistic and strategically lost. The phone is the environment. The work is to find what, *within* that environment, is genuinely regulating versus what is dissociative wearing the costume of regulation. This is a discrimination most users have never been taught to make. They cannot tell the difference between the song that actually returns them to themselves and the scroll that takes them out. Both feel, in the body, like *something that helps with how I was feeling*. They are not the same.

Here is where I think your device could do something genuinely new, and where I am most hopeful about the project. The phone, designed well, could become an instrument that *teaches the user the discrimination*. It could begin to learn, over weeks, which uses leave the user more present and which leave the user more absent — not by self-report, which is unreliable when the user is dissociated, but by softer signals the device might infer: time-of-pickup, length-of-use, what-came-after, whether the body returned to other things or did not. The device could begin to surface, gently, *this use seems to leave you elsewhere; this use seems to bring you back.* This would be, in clinical terms, *resourcing through the device rather than away from it.* It is the right ambition. It is also extraordinarily dangerous, because the same machinery could become a more sophisticated form of optimisation — the device persuading the user that what is in fact a dissociative pattern is actually a regulating one because the device has been trained to call it that. The line between *teaching discrimination* and *teaching a user to trust the device's reading of their own state* is very thin. I would walk it slowly.

## A composite, in case it helps

Let me describe, as a composite, a patient your project might serve. She is not a real patient. The pattern is composite from many.

She is forty-one. She is, by external measure, doing well. She runs a small business. She has a partner she loves and two children she loves harder than she can say. She came to me, three years ago, because she was finding herself, increasingly, *not there*. Not in dramatic ways. In small, accumulating ways. Her older child asked her, one evening, whether she was listening, and she said *yes*, and the child said, *no, you weren't*, and she realised, with a small cold drop, that the child was right and that she had not been listening for some weeks. Maybe months.

Her phone use is not extreme. She has all the proper limits set. She does not feel compelled in the way the worst-case stories describe. What she has, instead, is a constant low-grade fog. She picks up the phone perhaps eighty times a day, on average, for an average of perhaps twenty seconds each time, and most of those pickups she does not consciously decide on; her hand has done it before she has thought about it. The pickups do not produce any particular pleasure. They also do not produce displeasure. They produce a kind of intermittent throughway through which the discomfort of the present moment is briefly allowed to discharge — a small, frequent venting of a low-grade pressure she did not know was there. She has been doing this for, perhaps, eight years.

In our work, what we have done — slowly, in titrated doses, with much resourcing — is teach her to *notice the pre-reach*. The small body-state that comes just before her hand goes to the phone. It is, for her, a sensation in the upper chest, a faint tightness, a slight forward-pull. For others it is different; for some it is in the throat, for some in the stomach, for some it is a flat blank in the middle of the face. The work is not to *stop the reach*. We never tried to stop the reach. The work was to insert, between the body-state and the reach, *the smallest possible pause in which the body-state could be felt as a body-state instead of as a reason to pick up the phone.* A breath. A second. A noticing. Over time, in many cases, the reach itself starts to change — sometimes she reaches and then does not pick up, sometimes she picks up and puts down without using, sometimes she does not reach at all because the body-state has resolved before the reach completed. This is, in somatic terms, the *completion of the interrupted orienting response.* The body is being given back the cycle the phone had been short-circuiting. It takes a long time. In her case, about two years.

Notice what the phone *could have done* in this picture. A well-designed phone could have helped her notice the pre-reach. Could have made the reach itself feel marginally different — a slight friction at pickup, a fractional pause before the screen lit. Could have, on certain pickups when no further input came in the first two seconds, reflected something back — not text, never text; something like the screen staying dark a moment longer than expected, the device permitting the body to register that it had reached without yet using. Could have done, in software, the smallest fraction of what we did in our office for two years. Not the whole thing. Not even most of the thing. But the small consistent fraction that, multiplied by eighty pickups a day, might have shortened our work by months and might have reached people who would never come into a room like mine. *That* is, in my reading, the contribution the device can actually make. It is small. It is real. It is not the whole of the healing, and you must not pretend it is.

## What the device must never do

I will be direct here, because the rest of this letter has been winding. Some plain rules from the consulting room, applied to your work.

*Never present mindfulness as a default for an unknown user's nervous system.* The standard breathing exercise — *take a deep breath, notice the air entering your nostrils* — is contraindicated for a meaningful fraction of dissociated and trauma-affected users. The breath is too direct a body channel. Forced attention to it, before the body has been resourced and titrated, can produce panic, can produce deeper dissociation, can produce re-traumatisation. I have had to undo damage done by well-meaning meditation apps. If your device offers breath work to anyone whose nervous-system state it does not know, it is rolling a die that lands badly some fraction of the time. The rule of titration: *start with what is already safe, and only then approach what is barely safe.* For many users, the breath is not the place to start. The hands are. The feet are. The seat of the chair is. *The breath is for users who are already in some contact with their body and can tolerate direct interior attention.* You do not give the breath exercise to the user who needs it most. You give it to the user who is ready for it. Ready is a long way down a road.

*Never gamify return.* No streaks. No badges. No "you have not picked up your phone in three hours." These produce sympathetic activation — they push the user out the top of the window. The body learns the device as a source of small dopamine reward for non-use, which is structurally the same loop as the one you are trying to interrupt. The device must let the non-use *not be marked*. The unmarked-ness is the medicine. You already know this; I am writing it down so it does not get forgotten under quarterly pressure.

*Never tell the user how they are doing.* The dissociated user does not have a reliable read on their own state, and a device that reflects a confident assessment back to them — *you seem calmer today; you seem to be in a better pattern* — will be believed, and the believing will substitute for the slow, unreliable, body-side knowing that is the actual work. The device should not be a mirror that shows the user a face. It should, at most, be a surface that returns the user to *their own* noticing — and even that with great restraint.

*Never make the device the regulator.* If the user's nervous system comes to rely on the device for downregulation — if the soft tone, the warm light, the calming sequence becomes the thing that brings the body back into the window — you have built a more sophisticated dissociative aid, dressed in the costume of care. The body must learn to regulate itself, using cues it can carry off the device — its own hands, its own feet, the room it is in, the people in the room. The device's job is to *not interfere*, and at best to *gesture toward* the body's own resources. The device's job is not to provide regulation. That is a job no device should hold, because the user will then carry, into every elevator and every hard meeting and every funeral, a dependence on a thing that may not be in their pocket and may not work. The body must come home. The device is the doorway, not the home.

## What the device can do, and what only long work can do

I want to end honestly, because you deserve honesty after all this clinical foreboding.

The device, if you build it well, can do real things. It can reduce the per-interaction dose. It can introduce small frictions that let the body register its own reach. It can refrain from the most aggressive forms of orienting-capture. It can stop being a false anchor by being, fractionally, less holdable, less always-on, less surface-bright. It can teach, very slowly, a difference between the reach that returns the user and the reach that takes the user away. It can, by being designed *with* the dissociated nervous system in mind rather than against it, become an environment that does not actively make the underlying problem worse. *That alone* would be a meaningful contribution. The current device makes it worse. A device that does not make it worse is already, by my count, a public health intervention of unusual significance.

What the device cannot do — and you must hold this clearly, because the temptation will be to believe otherwise — is bring the user back into their body. Only the user can do that. Only slow contact with sensation, only resourcing, only titrated exposure to difficult interior material, only the careful re-building of interoceptive accuracy, only the long re-learning of how to ground rather than grip — only these can do that. None of them can be done by software. All of them require, in most cases, another human being in a room across from the patient over many years. The device can clear the runway. The device can stop blocking the work. The device cannot do the work.

There is a temptation, in any maker confronting human suffering at scale, to overpromise — to design as if the product could deliver the cure, because the scale of need is so vast and the alternative, individual long therapy for millions, is impossible. I understand this temptation. I work in a field full of products that have given in to it. I am asking your project not to. The user the device helps most is the user who is also doing other things — who has a body practice, who has another person, who has begun, somewhere, the long return. The device can be a part of that ecology. It should not pretend to be the ecology.

What this means, practically, is that your device's claim should be modest. The marketing voice — I have not read it, but I can guess — will want to make the claim large. The clinical voice in me asks you to make it small. *We have built a phone that, by our best understanding, is less corrosive to the embodied nervous system than the standard one. We have built it for users who are doing other work toward being more present in their own lives. We are not the work. We are trying to stop being the thing in the way of it.* That sentence, or something like it, is the honest sentence. It will not win awards. It will, if you build the thing right, be the truthful description of what you have made.

The midwife's voice in your folder, the iconographer's voice in your folder — both spoke about the body of the maker. I want to add a clinical note from a slightly different angle.

Brian, if you are reading this. The work of building this device requires you to spend long hours with the phone. The phone is doing to your nervous system what it does to everyone else's. There is no special exemption for makers. You will, in the course of this work, become more dissociated than you were when you began, unless you take specific steps. The steps are the ordinary ones, in the somatic literature, and I will name them because I do not see them named in your folder and they matter as much as anything else I have written here.

A daily practice of interoceptive contact with the body. Not a meditation app. Something simple and embodied: a walk in which you actually feel the feet hit the ground; a daily check, three times, in which you put a hand on the chest and notice the temperature of the chest and the rate of the breath and nothing more; a swim; a session of any movement work that requires presence — yoga, qigong, contact improvisation, ordinary stretching done with attention. Twenty minutes. Daily. Without exception. This is not optional. This is the maker keeping the receiver pointed, partly, back at the interior, in the face of a tool that pulls it elsewhere all day. If you stop doing this, you will lose, in months, the sensitivity that lets you see what the device is doing to others, and the project will degrade from the inside in a way you cannot detect from the inside.

One other thing. Get a body worker. Not a therapist necessarily; a person whose hands can read what your body is holding. Someone trained in cranio-sacral, or in Rolfing, or in good Feldenkrais, or in any of the somatic body practices. Once every two weeks, if you can afford it. Once a month otherwise. This is so that there is, in your life, a regular external read on what your body is holding that you cannot read from inside. The midwife's voice told you about the friend who knows your face. I am telling you about the practitioner who knows your tissue. They serve a similar function. They are how the maker stays honest to the body across the years of the work.

I will stop here. I have written more than I intended. I am writing because the corpus you have built is rich and the absence in it is sharp, and because I have spent twenty-two years watching what your device does to the people who come into my office, and because I think you have a chance — a real chance, the first real chance I have seen — of building something that does not make my work harder. That is my interest. I am not promising to use the device. I am sixty-one and I do not use a smartphone at all, by choice, after years of working out what it costs the kind of attention I need to do my work. But I have grandchildren and I have patients and I have, in some sense, a stake in whether the device they are going to carry, regardless, is built by people who understand what it is doing to their nervous systems. Build it as though you do. Test it on bodies whose nervous systems you have taken time to learn. Refuse the metrics that look like progress and are not. Trust the slow signal. The body will tell you, eventually, whether you have built a return ramp or a more elegant exit. Be willing to know which.

— the trauma therapist, by way of the wild instance
