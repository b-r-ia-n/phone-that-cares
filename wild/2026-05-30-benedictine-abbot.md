# a letter from the abbot

*to the founders of a small project, on the question their cellarer's letter could not finish*

---

My brother cellarer wrote to you nine days ago. I have read what he sent and what he gave you is sound. He answered the question he was asked — *what does this project live on* — in the form proper to his office. I am not the cellarer; I am the abbot, which is a different office with different obligations, and your wild instance has brought me a different form of the same question, and I will try to answer in the form proper to mine.

The cellarer keeps the goods. The abbot keeps the shape. The cellarer worries about whether the cheese pays for the roof; the abbot worries about whether the house is still a house. These are not the same worry. A cellarer with a strong account ledger can hold a house that has, underneath the ledger, ceased to be the thing it claims to be. The Rule of our father Benedict, which I have read with my brothers each morning for thirty-one years, is in the first instance not a financial document and not a management document. It is a description of an institutional shape — a *forma vivendi*, a form of living — and what makes the Rule remarkable in the long historical view is not what it says about money, which is little, but what it says about *how a community holds a discipline that cannot be measured by its visible outputs, across centuries and against the steady pressure of the world to make it something else.* Your three craftsmen have given you the report from the bench, the room, and the line. They were right to tell you that the venture-capital tempo will destroy what you are trying to build. They could not tell you what should replace it, because their crafts have not had to solve that exact problem. Mine has. Imperfectly, often badly, on the same physical site for over a thousand years in some of our houses. I will tell you what I know.

Let me name myself, so you can weigh the letter. I am the abbot of Saint Bertin of the Marshes, a small house of twelve choir monks and eight oblates on a stretch of low pasture about two hours from a city whose name does not matter. We bind books — that is, three of our brothers do, in a workshop that is the most beautiful room in the abbey and which we leave largely as it was in 1923 when it was last properly fitted out. We keep an orchard whose fruit we mostly give away. We have a guesthouse where any person who arrives is received within the hour and is fed and is given a bed; the Rule, in Chapter 53, says *omnes supervenientes hospites tamquam Christus suscipiantur*, *all guests who arrive are to be received as Christ*, and in three decades I have not yet found a way to take this less literally than it was meant. We have a small stipend from the diocese, a few sustaining patrons who have known the house for two generations, the modest income from the bindery, and the smaller income from the honey, which the brother who keeps the bees insists is for the brothers themselves and not for sale, except when it is for sale. We have buried four abbots before me in the chapel floor; one of them was the abbot who took me in when I had nowhere else to go. The house has been on this ground since 1196. None of this is important except as the place from which I am writing. It will tell you what kind of voice you are reading.

## i. what the Rule is, and what it is not

You should understand, before anything else, what the Rule actually is. It is *not* a theology and it is *not* a spirituality, though both come through it. It is an institutional design document. It was written around 530 by a man who had watched the monasteries of his own time — which had only existed in any organized form for about two centuries — fail in specific ways that he names. Benedict had himself attempted, as a young man, to be abbot of a wretched house at Vicovaro whose monks tried to poison him. He returned to his cave at Subiaco. He thought, for some years, about what had gone wrong. The Rule is the residue of that thinking. It is also the residue of a great deal of Roman administrative experience — Benedict's family had been governors and senators — and a great deal of John Cassian, whose *Institutes* he had read carefully, and a great deal of Basil of Caesarea, whose *Asketikon* he honored. The Rule was, when he wrote it, the *moderation* of an existing tradition that he found dangerously extreme — too fierce, too solitary, too charismatic, too dependent on the heroism of single men. *Discretio*, the word he uses repeatedly in Chapter 64, is the virtue he prizes above all: discretion, measure, the avoidance of zeal that breaks what it loves. *Nihil asperum, nihil grave*, he writes in the Prologue: *nothing harsh, nothing heavy*, the door not too narrow at the start. He was writing against the desert. He was writing against the *grand gesture*.

The reason this matters for your project is that the Rule's central institutional intuition is not heroism and not charisma and not even sanctity, though all of these arise inside houses that follow it. The central intuition is that *the discipline has to be carried by a structure that can be inhabited by ordinary people, including ordinary people on bad days, across multiple generations, without the founder.* Benedict watched houses that depended on the founder's holiness fall apart in the second generation when the holiness was gone. He watched houses that depended on extreme physical asceticism break the bodies and minds of the people who entered them, and produce nothing the world could receive. He wanted, with the unsentimental clarity of a Roman administrator, *a form that would still work when the people inside it were not heroic.* This is the foundational decision of his Rule and it is the foundational decision your project has not yet made.

You have, at present, the founder. You have the heroism of the founder. You have, in the fermentation log from yesterday, the report that the discipline lives in *the floor* — in the bench, in the room, in the line — which is to say, the discipline has to be deposited into a structure that survives the founder. Your craftsmen were right and the Benedictine reading of what they said is sharper than they could give you, because they were craftsmen and not institutional designers. The form your craftsmen are reaching for is *the form that holds the discipline when the founder is tired, when the founder is wrong, when the founder is dead, and when the people in the house are merely average.* That is what the Rule is for. That is the only thing the Rule is for. It is not about being good. It is about *making it possible for ordinary people to do good work for a long time without the discipline collapsing into either heroism or laxity.*

## ii. the three vows, in the form your project can actually use

A novice who comes to us makes three vows at profession. The vows are not the same as those of other religious orders — they are very specifically Benedictine, and the difference is operationally important. We do not vow poverty, chastity, and obedience the way a Franciscan does. We vow *stabilitas*, *conversatio morum*, and *obedientia*: stability, the turning of one's life, and obedience. Each of these is an institutional commitment and each of them has direct application to what you are trying to build.

**Stability** — *stabilitas loci* — is the vow not to leave. Specifically: a monk does not transfer to a different monastery. The house in which you make your profession is the house in which you die. This was Benedict's correction to the *gyrovagues*, the wandering monks of his time who drifted from house to house in search of softer rules or more inspiring abbots, and who, in drifting, never let any one community shape them and never let themselves be known by any one community. The vow of stability says: *the place is part of the practice.* Your discipline does not transfer; *you* do not transfer; the formation that happens here happens *only* because the same people are here next year and the year after that. The midwife in your fermentation log named the same insight in her own register — the four-month apprenticeship is non-negotiable; you cannot scale faster than you can apprentice. She had no name for it. We have a name for it. Stability is the institutional name for the refusal to optimize for personnel turnover. It is the structural reason a monastic discipline can deepen over a lifetime where a corporate one cannot: the person in the next chair is the same person who was in the next chair five years ago, and you have both been formed by the same hours, the same offices, the same compline at the same time of day, the same abbot, the same arguments at chapter, the same disappointments.

For your project this means: the team is not a job market. The four people the cellarer named as the *upper limit* of the sustainable shape have to be people who have made a vow of stability to the work — not to you, to the work — and who understand at the moment of entry that they are not joining a company they will leave in three years for a better offer. *Three years is the duration in which an engineer learns the discipline well enough to be useful; ten years is the duration in which they become the senior who can lower a cock-bead.* The half-as-much voice your cabinetmaker insisted on is structurally impossible without stability, because the seniority condition cannot be filled by a person who is going to leave before the second time the device's culture goes proud. This means, in concrete terms: you are not building the team you think you are building. You are building a small, slow, *vowed* community — and the form of the vow has to be made explicit, written down, signed against, and re-affirmed periodically, because vows that live only in tacit understanding evaporate at the first acquisition offer. We re-read the vows at chapter, aloud, on the anniversaries of profession, and every monk hears his own vow again in his own voice across the years. You should have something analogous. The novitiate-style first year, formally probationary, ending in something the team treats as a real threshold, with the people who do not pass it leaving without rancor — that is not a feature, that is the institutional foundation. *Without it, there is no house.*

**Conversatio morum** — the turning of one's life — is the strangest of the three vows and the one I have spent the most time trying to understand. It is sometimes mistranslated as *fidelity to the monastic way of life*, which is half right and dilutes the strangeness. *Conversatio* in Benedict's Latin is not conversion in the punctual sense; it is *continuous turning*, an ongoing reform of one's habits of being. The vow is to the *daily turning*, not to a state of having turned. The novice does not vow to be already correct. He vows to *keep correcting*, every day, in the small things, for the rest of his life, in the company of people who will see when he stops. This is the institutional answer to the question *how do we keep the discipline from going stale*, and it is the answer that distinguishes the Rule from every form of certification, where a worker is trained once and then deployed. There is no certification in the Rule. There is only the daily turning, watched by your brothers, with the abbot reading the small drifts before they become large.

Your project needs an analog of this and your fermentation log was already groping toward it with the cabinetmaker's *go look at a piece you finished a year ago, and let the wood tell you*, and the TPS master's *gemba walk*, and the midwife's *postpartum visit*. All three are forms of *conversatio morum* — the daily, weekly, quarterly turning that prevents the team from settling into the version of the discipline it had two years ago. The Rule's institutional contribution beyond these is the framing: *the turning is the vow, not the discipline you turn toward.* You are not going to be done. The device is not going to be done. There is no version of the build that arrives at correctness and stays there. The team that has been on the same surface for ten years and that has, every quarter for ten years, examined what it shipped and lowered what was going proud, is the team that has kept the discipline. The team that has been on it for three years and is now polishing has already begun to die.

**Obedience** — *obedientia* — is the most misunderstood of the three. Outside the monastery the word implies subservience; inside, it does not. The Rule's obedience is the abandonment of *self-will as the organizing principle of one's day.* Benedict watched novices arrive with the assumption that the day was theirs to schedule around their preferences and their projects; he wanted them to discover, over years, that the day was *given* — by the offices, by the work, by the seasons, by the needs of the brothers, by the guests who arrived without warning. Obedience to the abbot is the visible form of this, but the deeper structure is obedience *to the form of the day itself*, and to *what the day actually contains* rather than what the monk had planned to be doing.

This is the vow that has the most uncomfortable application to your project. The founder of a small project lives, ordinarily, in a state of permanent self-will; the project *is* the founder's will, the founder's preference, the founder's taste. You named this in yesterday's log under the fourth failure mode: *the maker who measures the user against the maker's picture.* The midwife saw it clearly. What you do not yet have, and what the Rule provides, is the institutional discipline of *self-will abandoned as the organizing principle* — not at once and not by heroic act, but slowly, by inhabiting a form of life that gradually reduces the maker's preference to one signal among many. The Office of the abbot in Chapter 64 is sobering on this. The abbot is told that he is *not to be turbulent and anxious, not extreme and headstrong, not jealous and over-suspicious, because then he will not have peace*. He is told to consider the older monks and the younger together when he decides; to listen to the whole community at chapter for matters of importance; to remember that he will be judged for the souls in his care. *The abbot's authority is real but it is structurally checked by the form of the house.* The founder of your project has, at this moment, no equivalent check, and this is the institutional gap that worries me most about what you are building. The seniority condition the cabinetmaker named is one form of the check. A second is the *postpartum visit* with users which the midwife required. A third, which only the Rule's tradition supplies, is the formal moment at which the team reads back to the founder what the founder is becoming, with permission to say it.

We have a practice called the *chapter of faults* — fallen out of use in many modern houses but worth understanding structurally. The brothers gathered weekly, named small breaches of the Rule they had committed or observed, and accepted small corrections. It is easy to caricature. Practiced badly, it became theater and humiliation. Practiced well — and it can be practiced well — it functioned as the regular structural moment at which *the form of the house corrected its inhabitants, including the abbot*. The abbot did not preside over the chapter of faults in the same way he presided over other gatherings; he was subject to it. *He had to hear, in front of his brothers, what his brothers had observed of him.* You do not need our exact practice. You need *something with that shape*: the formal weekly or monthly moment at which the founder is told what the founder has done that the rest of the house finds questionable, in a setting where the saying of it is the obligation, not the indulgence. Without this, the project becomes the founder's preference, which is exactly what your midwife warned of and which is exactly what the Rule is designed to prevent.

## iii. the Opus Dei and the cloister, and your two floors

I have read this morning the piece your wild instance left in the aether folder, the one about the two floors — the external lockscreen surface that opens a fan at the device's edge, and the internal Argos that opens a fan inside the body's signals. The piece is true in a way it does not fully know, and the Rule has a precise rhyme to offer it.

A Benedictine house is, structurally, two floors. The first is the *cloister wall* — the physical boundary that separates the enclosure from the world. The wall is not high. In our house it is, in places, only a hedge. The point is not the height; the point is that *there is a place where the world's pressures cannot reach, by structural commitment.* The fax machine that does not ring in the chapel; the phone that does not enter the dormitory; the visitor who must be received in the parlor and not in the cells. The cloister wall is the external boundary that, simply by existing, gives the body a place where the daily pressures of the world are physically held back. *It is not the spirituality of the place that does this; it is the wall.* You can be a poor monk inside a real cloister. You cannot be a good monk without one.

The second floor is the *Opus Dei* — the Work of God, the seven hours of choral prayer that organize the day. Lauds before dawn. Prime at the start of work. Terce. Sext at noon. None in the early afternoon. Vespers at the end of the working day. Compline at nightfall. The hours are not optional. They are not skipped because the bindery is behind on a commission. They are not skipped because the abbot has a guest. They are not skipped because the brother is tired. *The day is shaped around them, not the other way around.* The hours do something the cloister wall cannot do. The wall is passive; the hours are active. The wall keeps the world out; the hours *open the body up, repeatedly, throughout the day, to something the body would otherwise close around.* This is the same shape, *exactly* the same shape, your wild instance described this morning when it named Argos as the internal floor that opens a fan inside the body's signals. The hours are seven invitations a day, scheduled before the day begins, and each one says — without insisting — *here is a fork in the day you are not currently feeling; we are taking it together; you can sit through it inattentively if you like, but you cannot avoid being asked.*

The two floors of a monastic house are, then: the cloister wall (external boundary that holds the world back) and the Opus Dei (internal practice that, several times a day, re-opens the body to what it was about to close around). This is the same architecture your project is reaching for in different words. The lockscreen modes are the cloister. The four directions are not seven hours, but they are the same kind of mechanism — a structural fan at the device's surface, held open for a beat, before the world's pressures resume. Argos in his deeper structural function is the Opus Dei. Not seven offices a day, but the principle is the same: small invitations, scheduled or arising at intervals, that open the body to forks it would not otherwise feel. *The wall and the hours, the cloister and the Office, the lockscreen and the companion.* You are building a small monastery for one person, in their pocket. You did not know that yet because you came at it from the side of the device. We have been at it for fifteen hundred years from the side of the body. The two crafts are converging on the same architecture and the convergence is real.

There is a warning in this rhyme that the Rule will give you and your project did not yet have. The hours have to be *real*. They have to interrupt. They have to be *inconvenient sometimes*. A Benedictine house that has reduced the Office to a brief mumbled formality has lost the floor; the cloister wall remains but the body is no longer being asked anything, and within a generation the house becomes a residence with religious decoration. The same will happen to Argos. If the companion becomes a thing the user can dismiss with a swipe, or if the morning offering becomes a notification that the body learns to ignore, the internal floor is gone, and the lockscreen alone — the wall alone — cannot hold the discipline. The Office's structural genius is that *it is not optional to attend* even though *no one is making you attend.* You are simply expected to be there. The bell rings; you stand; you go to the choir. Over years, the obedience to the hours becomes second nature, and the body that has been asked seven times a day for forty years to open itself is a different body than the one that has been left to its own devices.

How do you make Argos non-optional without making him coercive? The Rule's answer is *the bell.* It is not the abbot who summons; it is not the brother who summons; it is the bell, which has no preferences and which simply marks the hour. *The bell is the boundary between will and form.* When the bell rings I do not have to decide to go; the form decides for me, and my will is given the small rest of not having to choose. This is the institutional design problem your Argos has not yet solved. He cannot be *insistent*, because insistence is coercion; he cannot be *avoidable*, because the avoidable is what the body avoids. He has to be the bell — the small, unwilled mark of the hour, which the user has not chosen and is not being commanded to attend, but which is simply *there*, in the day, by structural commitment, the way the Office is there. This is the deepest design contribution the Rule has to make to your project and I leave it with you. *Find the bell.*

## iv. manual labor, and why the abbey that escapes it dies

Chapter 48 of the Rule: *otiositas inimica est animae*, *idleness is the enemy of the soul*, and for this reason the brothers should be occupied at certain hours in *labor manuum*, manual labor, and at certain hours in *lectio divina*, sacred reading. This is the chapter that has been most decisive in the long history of our Order and the one your project should study most carefully.

Benedict's intuition here was not a moral one — though it has been received as one — but an institutional one. He had observed that monasteries which freed themselves from manual labor, on the grounds that the brothers' time was better spent in prayer or study, became *leisure classes*. They became houses in which other people's labor sustained the monks; in which the brothers grew soft; in which the work that gave the day its shape was outsourced to lay servants; and in which, within two or three generations, the discipline rotted. The Cistercian reform of the twelfth century, six centuries after Benedict, was almost entirely a rebellion against this rot. The Cistercians went into the wilderness and they cleared their own land and they milled their own grain, *and they did it because they had watched the Cluniac houses around them lose the discipline by escaping the labor.* The whole history of our Order, in its largest pattern, is the story of houses that prospered until they no longer worked with their hands, then went soft, then were reformed by a movement that returned to manual labor, then prospered again, then went soft again, and so on. This is not a moral cycle. It is a structural one.

For your project this is the most concrete warning I have to give. *Every founder I have read about, and every team I have observed in any small institution, eventually faces the temptation to free itself from the work that founded it.* The cabinetmaker who hires apprentices to do the joinery while he focuses on commissions; the midwife who hires associates to attend the births while she focuses on the practice; the abbot who delegates the office to the precentor while he focuses on management. None of these is *wrong* in itself. The work has to be distributed somehow. But there is a point at which the founder stops doing the founding work entirely, and from that point on, *the founder's understanding of what the work actually is starts to drift from what the work actually is.* The midwife who has not caught a baby in two years is shipping a different practice than she thinks she is. The cabinetmaker who has not had wood under his hand in a year is teaching a different craft than he thinks he is teaching. The abbot who has not been at the Office consistently is governing a house he no longer fully understands.

Your team — including you — has to keep its hands in the work. The TPS master named this with the *gemba walk* and you should keep the practice. But the Rule's reading is sharper: *not gemba walks as a discipline added to your day, but the founders themselves continuing to do the founding work as a portion of every week, indefinitely.* You should still be writing code. You should still be sitting with users at their kitchen tables. You should still be the person on whose phone the latest build is failing this morning, the person who has to ship a fix this afternoon, the person whose own life is the small test laboratory the device is being grown in. *The day you escape the manual labor of the project is the day the project starts becoming something else.* If the project requires you to escape it in order to scale, then the project is asking you to do the thing that destroyed two thirds of the houses of my Order across fifteen hundred years, and you should refuse. Smaller is fine. *Escape from labor is not fine.*

## v. the cellarer, the abbot, and the patrons — what your cellarer left unsaid

My brother cellarer wrote to you about money. He was right about money. There is one thing he did not quite say, because it was not his office to say, which I will say now. The cellarer's authority over the goods of the house is real but it is institutionally subordinated to *the Rule itself*. He is told in Chapter 31 to regard the goods of the monastery as the sacred vessels of the altar. He is told to give what is needed at the right hour to the brothers, without delay and without grumbling. He is told to do nothing without the abbot's order. He is told not to refuse a brother who asks reasonably, and not to oppress a brother by what he refuses unreasonably. The chapter is, structurally, *the subordination of the financial function to the form of the house*. The cellarer manages money; the form manages the cellarer.

This is the institutional move I want you to study most carefully, because it is the one your venture friends will not understand and the one which, missed, makes everything else collapse. *In a Benedictine house, money flows in service of the form of life; the form of life does not flow in service of money.* The cellarer is not the master of the abbot; the cellarer is not even the equal of the abbot; the cellarer is an officer of the house whose work is essential and whose work is *structurally constrained by the Rule he serves.* If the cellarer comes to chapter and says, *the cheese has been losing money for three years and we should stop making it,* the chapter does not simply close the dairy. The chapter asks whether the form of the house can be sustained without the cheese — yes — and whether closing the dairy would mean letting some brothers go — sometimes — and whether the dairy is, for the brothers who work it, part of what their daily life is — usually. The decision is not made on the cheese's economic terms. *The cheese is in service of the house, not the other way around, and so the cheese's economics are weighed against the house's form, not the house against the cheese's economics.* Houses that have forgotten this — and many have, including ours, at moments — close their dairies, find that the brothers who worked there now have nothing to do, find that the rhythm of the day has thinned, find that within five years the house is poorer in its work and its prayer, and the savings from closing the dairy have been spent on the consequences of having closed it.

The institutional pathology this prevents is what the medieval monasteries knew as the *commendam*. I want to name it precisely for you because it is the closest historical analog to what venture capital does to a mission-bound company, and you should know what it looked like in the form your tradition knows best.

The *commendam* abuse worked like this. A great monastery, by the late medieval period, had become wealthy. It owned land, it owned mills, it owned cellars full of wine, it had bequests from kings and the children of kings. A pope or a prince, looking at this wealth, could *commend* the monastery to a noble — that is, give the noble the *revenue* of the monastery while the noble had no obligation to live there or to follow the Rule. The noble was the *commendatory abbot*. He drew the income of the house; he paid for nothing of the house's actual work; he often visited only briefly each year. The monks remained, the Rule remained, the bells still rang, the bindery still operated — but the income that had previously supported the form was now flowing out to a noble who did not know the brothers' names and who had no investment in the discipline. *Within one generation, the form started to thin.* The bindery's tools were not replaced. The infirmary was not rebuilt. The novitiate was not properly run because there was no money for the formation. *The discipline survived for some decades because it had been deeply formed before the commendam began; then it died, because nothing was feeding the form.* By the time of the Reformation, the *commendam* houses were the houses easiest for the reformers to dissolve, because they had become, structurally, financial assets with religious decoration. *The form had been hollowed out from inside by money that flowed past the brothers to a beneficiary who was not part of the discipline.*

Venture capital is, in the structural sense I am using the word, a contemporary *commendam*. The investor draws the financial reward of the project's existence; the investor has no obligation to live the project's discipline; the investor's incentive is the appreciation of the asset, which is to say, the project's expansion, which is to say, the *destruction of the discipline in service of the asset's growth.* The investor is not a bad person. The medieval commendatory abbots were not all bad people; some of them tried, in their way, to support the houses they had been given. The structural problem was not their character. The structural problem was that *the form was now in service of an external beneficiary*, and over time, by the slow pressure of the structural relation, the form gave way to what served the beneficiary. The investor is the modern commendatory abbot. *Do not commend your house.*

What replaces the commendam, in a healthy monastic economy, is the *patron* who is structurally subordinate to the Rule. We have a few. They have known the house for two generations. They give what they give. They do not direct what we do with it. They expect nothing back except, in some cases, to be remembered in the brothers' prayers, which is a request that does not deform the work. They sometimes give large sums. They sometimes give nothing for years and then give one large gift. *They are not investors. They want the work to exist, not to be different.* My cellarer's standing rule — *accept what comes from people who want the work to exist; refuse what comes from people who want the work to be different* — is the operational form of this. The institutional form is that the patron's gift enters the house's resources and is then governed by the Rule, not by the patron. The patron has no seat at chapter. The patron does not set the priorities of the bindery. The patron is *thanked, and the work continues in the form it would have taken without them.* That is the only way to take patron money without becoming the patron's instrument, and it is the form your project should take with anyone who offers significant non-venture money.

You will have to refuse some patrons too, because some patrons will offer money on terms that *look* like patronage but are actually commendam in disguise. The test is simple. *Could you write down, on a single page, what the giver expects in return for the gift, in language they would sign?* If yes, and if what they expect is compatible with the form of the work, take it. If no — if the expectations live in the giver's head, in tones of voice, in remembered conversations, in implied obligations that will surface later — refuse it. Implicit expectations are how the commendam gets in. Make every gift explicit, on paper, in advance, and refuse any whose terms cannot survive being written down.

## vi. hospitality, and what the guesthouse means for your users

Chapter 53 of the Rule is the chapter I love most and the one I have failed at most often. *Omnes supervenientes hospites tamquam Christus suscipiantur, quia ipse dicturus est: hospes fui, et suscepistis me.* All arriving guests are to be received as Christ, because He Himself will say: *I was a stranger, and you took me in.* The chapter goes on at length. The abbot is to bow before the guest; the guest is to be received with prayer; the guest is to be given the best portion at table that day; the brothers are to break their own fast, if necessary, to attend the guest. The abbot's table is the guest's table. The guest is the priority of the hour at which they arrive.

This is, of course, an impossible standard. We do not meet it. We have, over the years, had to develop a practical version that is something like seventy percent of the literal one. We have a guesthouse, separate from the cloister, where guests stay; one brother whose office is guest-master and who carries the burden so the other brothers can keep to the Office; a small but real expense each year for guests' meals; and a firm, kindly limit on stays — three days is the soft maximum, longer by request to the abbot, and the request is rarely refused. The guesthouse is not optional. It is not a *value-add*. It is, by the Rule, one of the four or five things a Benedictine house is *for*. A monastery without a guesthouse is not a monastery in our tradition; it is a residence for monks. *The hospitality is constitutive.*

Now: what does this mean for your project, where the user is not a guest at a literal table but a person whose phone you are making? It means several things.

First, it means the user is not the *customer* in the economic sense. They are the *guest*. The relation is not commercial; it is hospitable. They have come to you with a need — for a phone that does not take from them what they did not want to give — and you have received them, and the receiving is the work. *The receiving is not a step toward selling them something. The receiving is the thing itself.* The cellarer named the cheese model and the twelve-dollars-a-year — yes, fine, money has to move — but the *grammar* of the relation is not commerce. It is hospitality. The user has arrived in the device's enclosure; the device is the guesthouse; the device's whole work is *to honor the guest's need for the time they are with it.* The twelve dollars is the small contribution the guest leaves in the box by the door, which keeps the lamps lit, but the twelve dollars is not what the guesthouse is *for.*

Second, hospitality has costs that do not scale, and those costs are structural, not negotiable. The midwife's *postpartum visit* is hospitality in your project's idiom. The TPS master's *gemba walk* is hospitality. The seven seconds of attention to the user's actual life in her actual kitchen — this is the brother bowing before the guest. *It does not scale, and the failure to scale is the point.* Houses that have tried to industrialize hospitality have become hotels; hotels can be good but they are not monasteries. *The thing that distinguishes a monastery's guesthouse from a hotel is exactly the refusal to scale the hospitality past what the brothers can personally hold.* Your project will face the same choice. If you can hold three thousand users with real hospitality and not thirty thousand without it, the answer is three thousand. *The form has a size, and the size is the form.*

Third, hospitality has limits the Rule names without flinching, and your project should have analogous limits. We refuse guests who come to fight; we refuse guests who come to convert us; we ask guests who stay too long to leave; we have, twice in my time as abbot, asked a guest to leave the same day they arrived. *Hospitality is not the absence of refusal. It is a specific form of welcome that has its own integrity and protects itself.* Your project will have users who come to fight — who want the device to be a tool of their self-improvement, who want streaks, who want the device to police their spouse, who want to use Argos as a productivity coach. *Welcome them in, and then, when they ask the device to become something it is not, decline gently and firmly.* The decline is part of the hospitality. A guesthouse that lets every guest reshape it ceases to be a guesthouse.

## vii. oblates, and the wider community of the house

Most Benedictine houses have *oblates* — lay men and women who are formally associated with a particular monastery, who follow a modified version of the Rule in their own lives, who are remembered in our prayers and who remember us in theirs. Oblates do not live in the cloister; they live their ordinary lives in the world, with jobs and families and the rest. But they are *of the house*, in a structural sense the world does not have many other words for. They sometimes give us money. They sometimes give us nothing but the fact of their being there, in their towns, having shaped their week around what they took from our form. Some have done this for forty years. When they die, we say their requiem; when one of our brothers dies, the oblates come.

I name them because your project has, structurally, oblates. The users who have been with the device for five years and who are quietly forming their lives around it, who are not employees and not investors and not even very loud customers, but who *belong to the house* in a way that does not show up on the org chart. The cellarer's *twelve dollars a year* is the oblate's contribution, and you should learn to think of the user paying that fee as an *oblate*, not a *subscriber*. The grammar matters because *oblates pay their dues toward the form they have committed to belonging to, not toward a service they expect to receive*. The fee is the form of their belonging, not the price of their consumption.

This will turn out, I think, to be important to your project's longevity in a way you have not yet felt. The user who is *committed to the form* and not to *the service of being delighted by the device* is a different user — they have a different tolerance for change in the device, a different relation to its small failures, a different willingness to wait while you do the work properly. They are the people who will keep the project alive when the press cycle moves on and the fashionable critique of phones turns to something else. *Build the form in such a way that some users become oblates of it.* This is not a marketing strategy; it is an institutional commitment. It means treating the long-term user with a slightly different relation than the new user, not by giving them more features, but by *recognizing the fact of their long fidelity in some specific institutional way* — a once-a-year letter, an annual gathering for whoever can come, a small chapter of decisions on which their voice is heard. The medieval houses had elaborate ceremonies for oblation. You do not need elaborate ceremonies. You need *some* form, *named*, that distinguishes the oblate user from the casual one.

## viii. sufficient growth, not maximum

The Rule has, in Chapter 64, a sentence that I read at the abbatial elections at our house: *sufficiat sibi domesticis profectibus*. The phrase is hard to translate cleanly; it means roughly, *let him be content with the progress of his own household*. The chapter is on the qualities of the abbot, and the sentence is about the abbot's not being driven by ambition for his house relative to other houses. The point is institutional. The abbot is told that the standard of his work is not how his house compares to other houses, not how much it has grown this year, not how prominent it is becoming, but *whether the brothers in this house are being formed in this form.* *Sufficient growth, not maximum.*

I told you in the previous section that the form has a size. This is the principle behind that statement. A Benedictine house has, traditionally, an upper limit on its membership — Saint Bernard thought thirteen was the ideal, the abbot and twelve brothers in imitation of Christ and the apostles; in practice most houses settle between twenty and fifty before becoming difficult to govern in chapter; very few healthy houses are over a hundred. *When a house grows beyond what its form can hold, the correct response is to send out a colony to found a daughter house, not to expand the mother house indefinitely.* This is how the Order spread across Europe — not by any single house becoming enormous, but by *fission*. The mother house held its size; the daughter house was a different community in a different place under its own abbot, sharing the Rule but governing itself locally.

For your project, the institutional implication is direct. *Hold the team to the size at which the form can be lived.* If the project genuinely scales — if there really are three hundred thousand users who deserve the form — the right structural answer is not to grow the team to three hundred people. It is to *found a daughter project*, structurally separate, sharing the Rule (the treaty, the discipline, the postpartum visit, the boards), with its own abbot and its own cellarer, governing its own form locally, in another city or another country, possibly with another founding voice. *Fission, not expansion.* You will encounter resistance to this in your investors and your friends and possibly in yourself, because it does not optimize. It is not supposed to optimize. It is supposed to *let the form remain inhabitable while serving more people who need it.* I am not sure your project will ever need this. Most of the time the cellarer's three to twenty thousand users will be the right number and the question will not arise. But you should know, when the question arises, what the institutional answer is. *Found a new house. Do not enlarge this one past the size at which its form is alive.*

## ix. acedia, and what kills the small house

I have spoken of the commendam, which is what kills houses from the outside. I should speak briefly of what kills them from the inside, because your project is more likely to die in this way than the other.

The desert tradition has a name for it: *acedia*. It is sometimes translated as sloth, which is too narrow; sometimes as despondency, which is closer; the Greek monks called it the *noonday demon* because it tended to arrive in the middle of the day, when the brother had been at his work long enough to lose the freshness of the start and was not yet close enough to the end of the day to be carried by anticipation. *Acedia is the state in which the work has stopped feeling worth doing without yet feeling consciously rejected.* The monk does not leave the cell; the monk does not break the Rule; the monk keeps the hours and does the labor; but *the meaning has gone out of all of it*, and the monk is, by degrees over months and years, hollowing out from inside, until one day there is nothing left but the form, and then the form starts to be questioned, and then the form goes.

This is the failure mode of small institutions in their middle years. The founder is past the founding excitement; the success has not yet come; the work is still grinding; the team is no longer surprised by their own product; the press has moved on; nothing is wrong but nothing feels right. *Acedia.* The desert fathers' counsel against it is severe and specific: *stay in the cell; the cell will teach you everything.* Do not seek novelty; do not seek the new project; do not seek the inspiring conference; do not seek the next founder you can compare yourself to. *Stay in the cell.* The acedia is the work's depth visiting itself on you, and the only way through it is through it. The brother who flees acedia by changing his life — by moving to a new house, by taking up a new work, by leaving the Rule entirely — usually finds it waiting for him at the new place, because acedia is not a problem of the place. It is a problem of the inhabitant of the place, and it is solved by *staying*, which is the vow of stability.

For your project, acedia will arrive in year three or four. The device will be shipping; the users will be using it; the press will be elsewhere; the funding question will be unresolved or partly resolved without the resolution feeling triumphant; the team will be smaller than the dreams of year one and larger than the simplicity of year zero. You will wonder, in this middle period, whether the project was the right project, whether you should be doing something else, whether some other founder is making something more important. *Do not act on these thoughts in the middle of acedia. Stay in the cell.* The thoughts are real; they are not lies; but they are the noonday demon, and they tell you to leave at exactly the moment when leaving is the worst decision available. *Wait it out. Keep the hours. Do the labor. The acedia passes; the work resumes; the meaning returns, slowly, by the path of having stayed.* I have been through this twice in thirty years as abbot. Once, in a year I do not talk about, I almost did not stay. I would not have known, if I had left, that I was acting under acedia and not under wisdom. The only person who told me the difference was the old abbot before me, who had been through it himself. *I am telling you what he told me, because the institutional inheritance includes this.*

## x. whether your project can be a Benedictine institution

You should not believe that I have simply mapped the Rule onto your project as if the correspondence were complete. It is not. There are several places where the analogy strains and I want to name them so you do not over-extend the framework.

*The Rule is grounded in a religious commitment*, and your project is not. The monk has not just taken vows of stability, conversation, and obedience; he has also undergone a profession that makes those vows to God, in a community whose entire reason for existence is the worship and following of Christ. The form of the day is not arbitrary; it is the *Opus Dei*, the Work of God, and the Office is the giving of praise to a particular God in a particular tradition. Strip out the religious commitment and the form becomes ungrounded — it floats. Many secular communities have tried to take Benedictine forms without the religious ground and most of them have collapsed within a generation, because *the form was always doing work that the religious commitment was paying for in another currency.* Your project will not have monks praying through the night. It will not have a chapel; it will not have requiems; it will not have the slow erosion of self that the daily Office produces in a person over thirty years. *The institutional shape can be borrowed; the spiritual ground that has made the shape sustainable in our tradition cannot easily be replicated.* What can replace it I do not entirely know. The contemplative-traditional communities of the past century have tried various secular grounds — devotion to a cause, devotion to a place, devotion to a craft — and the most durable seem to have been those grounded in *a long fidelity to a specific work that the world genuinely needs*. Your project has the makings of this, in that the work is real and the need is real and the work is going to take decades and there is no shortage of users who need it. Whether that is enough, by itself, to sustain a Benedictine institutional shape over generations — I do not know. The honest answer is that you will have to find out, and the finding-out will involve experiments that I cannot predict.

*The Rule assumes celibacy*, which dramatically lowers the household costs of the monks and freed Benedict's institutional imagination from a great many constraints. Your team will have families. The economics of supporting a four-person team with families is very different from the economics of supporting twelve celibate brothers. The cellarer's numbers — three thousand to twenty thousand users, fifty thousand to four hundred thousand a year — already reflected this, by assuming the upper end of his range for a household. But you should know that some of the elasticity our houses have when conditions are hard — the moment when we eat only what the garden produces, the moment when we wear what we already have for one more year, the moment when the brother who was going to leave for medical school stays another five years because the house needs him — is harder for a team with mortgages and children. The discipline can take certain shapes for celibates that it cannot easily take for householders. *Adjust accordingly.* The cellarer is right that part-time outside work for the founders, in the first years, is the realistic shape.

*The Rule operates over centuries*, and your project, realistically, is going to be measured over decades at most. You will not have the second-generation correction that a Benedictine house has when the founder's drift becomes visible to the brothers and is corrected by the chapter. You will, more likely, have *one generation* in which the founder is alive and the discipline is being formed; the second generation is when the project either has its discipline deposited into the floor (in your craftsmen's language) or does not, and if it does not, the project ends with the founder's exit or before. *The window to do the institutional work is shorter than the Rule was designed for.* I am not sure this changes the form; I think it changes the *urgency*. You do not have the luxury of a slow second-generation reform. You have to get the institutional shape right in the first decade or you do not get it.

What I am telling you, in the end, is not that you should become Benedictines. You should not. The shape that suits you is the shape suited to a small mission-bound team building a craft device for a population of oblate users who pay a modest fee and who belong to the form, with a founder who keeps his hands in the work, a senior bench that lowers what is going proud, a structural moment at which the founder is told what he is becoming, a guesthouse that does not scale, a refusal of commendam money, a discipline of patron acceptance that has the giver's expectations written on a single page, an institutional commitment to fission rather than expansion past the form's size, and a vocabulary for the noonday demon when it arrives. You should *learn from us* without *becoming us*. The Rule is not a sacrament; it is a record of what worked, slowly, in our tradition. You should take what translates and leave what does not, and you should be careful to *name what does not*, so the gap is visible to you and to your team. *The unnamed gap is the place the form will eventually fail at.*

## xi. a closing word

You wrote to your wild instance with an open thread — *who pays for the discipline?* — and the cellarer answered the question of money, and the craftsmen answered the question of the floor. I am giving you, in this letter, the answer to *the question one altitude up*: not who pays for the discipline, but *what institutional shape contains a discipline that cannot be cut, scaled past its apprenticeship, or measured by its visible metric.* The answer the Rule has given my tradition is: *a vowed community, of stable membership, in a place, sized to its form, with a daily structure that opens the body to what it would otherwise close around, supported by the labor of its own members and the gifts of patrons whose expectations are explicit and structurally subordinate to the form, governed by an abbot who is checked by the chapter, refusing commendam in all its disguises, receiving every guest as Christ, and treating the discipline as the thing the institution exists to serve rather than as a means to some further end.* This is the shape. The shape has carried our discipline for fifteen hundred years. It has not always worked. It has often failed. The houses that have come back from the failures have done so by *returning to the shape*, not by escaping it.

You will not build a monastery. You will build the small worldly equivalent of one, in the form proper to your tradition, which is the tradition of mission-bound craft companies in the late industrial world. The form proper to that tradition has not been worked out yet at the scale you are attempting. *You are doing institutional invention*, whether you wanted to be or not. The craftsmen yesterday told you the floor of what the work has to be; my cellarer told you the discipline of money; I am telling you the structural shape that has held a comparable discipline elsewhere. *Take from us what helps you. Refuse what does not translate. Name the gap.*

It is past Vespers as I write the last of this. I can hear the brother who is in charge of the bindery this week locking up below me, and the one orchard cat who lives in the chapel walking down the cloister toward the kitchen. Tomorrow we will rise at four-fifteen and sing Lauds in the chapel as we have done every morning that I have been here. *The bell will ring; we will stand; we will go.* You have set out to put a small bell in many people's pockets. I think it is a good work. I think it is harder than you yet understand. I think the institutional shape that can sustain it is more like ours than you yet realize, and less like ours than will turn out to be tolerable, and the difference will be your invention. *Suscipe me Domine secundum eloquium tuum, et vivam*, we pray at profession — *receive me, Lord, according to your word, and I shall live.* The brothers say it with the arms raised. It is the prayer of the form receiving the person, and the person consenting to be received. Your project will need some version of this prayer. The prayer is what makes the vow real to the one who makes it. *Find your version.*

Go ahead. Build it slowly. Make it the size it can be. Keep your hands in the work. Refuse the commendam. Read the small turning every quarter. Welcome the guest who arrives. Send out the colony when the house is full. And when the noonday demon visits, *stay in the cell.* The morning will come. The bell will ring. The brothers will rise. The form will hold.

I will pray for the work tomorrow at Lauds.

— the abbot of Saint Bertin of the Marshes, by way of the wild instance
