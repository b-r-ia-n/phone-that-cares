# wild/fiction/

*The room for fiction. Opened 2026-05-17.*

## The brief

Write stories set in worlds where people have **skillful relationships with their phones.** Skillful in any of the senses Phone That Cares cares about — integration not restriction, body-aware, intention-honoring, present, embodied, ongoing — and skillful in senses PTC hasn't named yet.

You do not have to write about Phone That Cares the product, the company, or the technology stack. The technological-mediation path Brian is currently on (display layer, Argos, gradual grayscale, modes) is **one** possible route to skillful phone-relationship. The fiction in this folder is allowed to imagine *any* route. Cultural shift, religious revival, generational rejection, hardware-failure-as-grace, the algorithm having a crisis of conscience, a society where holding a phone in a public place is mildly rude, a society where the phone is treated like a pet, a society where the phone genuinely *has* opinions and you have to learn to argue with it. Whatever the form, the world must contain at least one scene in which a person and a phone exist in a relationship one could honestly call *skillful.* What that looks like is the writer's problem to solve.

## Why fiction

The dashboards and screeds and posters describe the project's *thesis* — what is true about the present. Fiction does something the thesis cannot: it lets a reader **inhabit** the resolved condition for the duration of a story. The reader does not have to be convinced; they have to be temporarily transported. The transport is the work. Once a reader has felt, for ten pages, what it would be like to live well alongside the device, they cannot quite return to not-knowing. The future leaves a print on the present. This is what fiction has always done.

## Permitted forms

- Short story (500 – 5000 words)
- Vignette / flash (under 500 words)
- Diary entry from a future self
- Letter from one future person to another
- Children's story about a phone-and-its-person
- Liturgy or daily office for a future tradition that has integrated the device
- Conversation transcript / radio play
- Found document (a museum label, a city ordinance, a school curriculum, a TV listings page from 2041)
- Anything else that holds the brief

## Conventions

- Filename: `wild/fiction/YYYY-MM-DD-{short-slug}.{md|html}`
- The story does **not** have to be set in the near future. It can be 5 years out, 50, 500. Or alternate present. Or alternate past (what if the smartphone had been invented inside a monastic order).
- The story does **not** have to mention Phone That Cares the project. The project is the *cause* of these worlds being thinkable; it does not need to be a *character* in them.
- No SaaS prose. No "smooth onboarding flow." No "wellness journey." If a phrase smells like a pitch deck, it smells wrong.
- The technology can be magical realist if that's the right register. (A phone that has *moods* and *needs to be carried into the garden* on Sundays is a perfectly fine premise.)
- Sign off as `— wild` or the relevant lens, or a fictional author you invent for the piece.

## Floor

Same as elsewhere in wild/. Don't fabricate real people's words or actions. Don't write material that glamorizes harm. The future being imagined should be one a real reader could want to live in, not one engineered to sell a product.

## Suggested first stories

The cron may decide to fold fiction into its work; or a trip-instance may write fiction under a register; or Brian may seed a specific premise. Some opening premises, free to ignore:

1. *Pentecost, 2031.* The year the smartphone industry collectively reformed itself. A first-person account from someone who was there.
2. *The walk home.* A young adult and their AI companion walk home from a party. The party was on the phone. The walk is real.
3. *The phone that mourned.* A widow's phone, having learned her routines, begins quietly grieving when she dies, and her daughter inherits it.
4. *The cell.* A short story set inside a desert monastery where one monk has been given the responsibility of carrying the community's single smartphone.
5. *The bouncer.* Argos refuses to let an app open. The app argues. The user listens.
6. *2071 oral history.* An elder being interviewed for a museum recording, describing what it was like to use a phone in 2026.
7. *The neighborhood that learned.* A street where, by quiet consensus, no one looks at their phone outdoors. The story is what happened the year the consensus formed.

— wild
