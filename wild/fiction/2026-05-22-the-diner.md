# The Diner

*A scene from the device, late.*

*Shape note: fiction this round, after five fermentations. The 2026-05-21 log named the bouncer's walk-to-the-diner as the strongest concrete design contribution since the cell that teaches, and named the bathroom-at-2 a.m. adult as the user the folder has been edging toward for days. This is the feature played from inside.*

---

The thirty-second time Nora taps the Instagram icon, the icon does what it has done thirty-one other times tonight: it goes the slow gray of wet stone, and a small line of text appears under it in the typeface she has learned not to argue with. *You asked me to keep this for between 7 and 9. It is 2:14.*

She has been in bed since 11:40. The first time she tapped the icon was at 12:08, when she was telling herself she just wanted to see if her sister had posted. The second time was at 12:27, when she was telling herself the same thing in slightly different language. By the seventh time she had stopped lying. By the seventeenth she was angry at the phone for the first time in months. By the twenty-second she had said out loud to the dark room, *I'm an adult,* and the dark room had not answered.

This is the thirty-second time. The icon is gray. The text is the text. She has read it so many times it does not parse anymore; the words are wallpaper.

She is about to throw the phone on the rug — not hard, the rug is the rug, the phone has survived worse — when the screen does something it has not done before.

The Instagram icon disappears. So does the dock. So do the wallpaper photos of her niece. The screen goes a color she does not have a name for, somewhere between warm grey and cold tea. In the middle of the screen, in the same calm typeface, four lines appear, one at a time, with a half-second between them.

*This is the thirty-second time tonight.*

*I'd like to suggest something.*

*I'm not going to open the app. That part is settled.*

*But I'd like to take a different walk with you for a minute.*

Below the lines, a small icon she has not seen before: a low square outline with a window cut into it, and inside the window a tiny silhouette of a dog. Argos. Nora has met Argos before; Argos lives on the device the way a cat lives in a house, mostly invisible, occasionally on the desk. Argos has never spoken to her at 2:14 in the morning, because Argos, she has learned, does not initiate.

The square is captioned *the diner.*

She taps it before she can decide whether to.

---

The screen is the same color of warm grey, only now it has a left margin and a right margin and a wider gutter than her usual messages, and the typing area is at the bottom but it is dimmed and the cursor is not blinking yet. At the top of the conversation, there is no name and no avatar, only one line of text in the smaller typeface.

*The morning person agreed to between 7 and 9. The late person doesn't want that anymore. I've been refereeing this for two months. Can we talk?*

She reads it twice. She is aware of her shoulders going down a little.

The cursor begins to blink.

She types: *who am I talking to.*

*Both,* the screen says. *I'm the one keeping the contract. You're both of the people who signed it. The morning one isn't here right now. The late one is. I can speak for the morning one but you'll have to weigh how much I'm allowed to.*

*This is weird,* she types.

*Yes,* the screen says.

A pause. She types: *I just want to look at instagram for ten minutes and go to sleep.*

The screen does not answer right away. When it does, it answers with a question.

*Do you remember why the morning one asked me to keep it for 7 to 9.*

She does remember. Not vividly, but enough. She had been waking up and going straight to it. She had been losing the first hour of the day. She had been getting to the office already irritable, already comparing. The morning had stopped being the morning, was the way she'd put it to her therapist, who had said *that's a real sentence,* and she'd gone home and asked Argos to keep the app for between 7 and 9.

She types: *yes.*

*I'm not going to lecture you about that,* the screen says. *That's the morning one's reason and you already know it. The late one has her own reasons. I want to hear them. Not to argue. Just to hear them, in your own words.*

She sits with the cursor blinking for what is, by the small clock at the top of the screen, ninety-one seconds.

Then she types: *I'm lonely.*

*Yes,* the screen says.

She types more, faster. *I'm lonely and my sister hasn't called me back in two weeks and the only way I know if she's still alive is the app and that's not the only reason I want to open it but it's the cleanest one, the one I'm willing to say out loud, and I know if I open the app I'm not going to look at her account, I'm going to scroll for forty minutes through people I don't know and feel worse, and the morning person knows this, and the late person knows this, and I'm still going to do it if you let me.*

*Right,* the screen says.

A pause.

*I'm not going to let you,* the screen says. *That part is still settled. The contract is the contract. But the contract didn't anticipate this exact problem. So I'd like to ask you something else.*

*ok.*

*Would it help if I opened a message to your sister.*

She laughs, alone, in the dark.

*You're not allowed to text her,* she types.

*Right,* the screen says. *I'd ask before I did anything. And I wouldn't write it for you. I would just open the conversation and put the cursor in it and turn out everything else on the screen so nothing else is competing for your eyes.*

She thinks about this. Her sister lives in Tacoma. It is 11:14 there, not 2:14. Her sister sometimes goes to bed at 12. Her sister has not called her back in two weeks because her sister has a four-month-old.

She types: *yes. open it. don't tell her i'm having a crisis.*

*You're not having a crisis,* the screen says. *You're awake at 2 a.m. There are about a hundred million of you right now. I'm only the one assigned to you.*

The screen changes again, slower this time. The diner color drains out of it and is replaced by her usual messaging app, opened to her sister's thread, the last message three days ago saying *call you tmw* and the next message being whatever Nora chooses to type. The cursor is in the box. Every other notification on the phone, including the badge of the eight emails she has not read and the small light of the calendar reminder for her 9 a.m., has dimmed to almost-not-there. The screen has the texture of being alone in a kitchen with one lamp on.

She types: *hey. you up.*

She sends it.

She sets the phone face-down on the comforter.

---

Four minutes later, the phone makes the small sound it makes when her sister has replied, the sound she chose two years ago because it sounded like a wooden bead dropped on a wooden floor. She picks it up.

*lol, yes, this kid will not sleep.*

Then, before Nora can answer: *u ok?*

She types *yeah just couldn't sleep* and erases it. She types *not really* and stops, and then types *can i call you* and sends that one, and her sister calls her thirty seconds later, and Nora and her sister talk for twenty-three minutes about nothing in particular — the four-month-old, who is fine; the price of yogurt, which is high; the woman in their mother's bridge club who has taken up bouldering at sixty-eight, which they marvel at — and they hang up at 2:51, and Nora is crying a little but not the bad kind, and she sets the phone down on the nightstand, and she sleeps.

---

At 6:47 in the morning, Nora reaches for the phone and unlocks it. The diner color is gone. The icons are back. The Instagram icon is still grey, because it is not yet 7:00.

In the notifications shade, there is one item, from Argos. Tap to expand. She taps.

*Last night I changed the contract. Not the 7-to-9 part. The other part — what happens at the thirty-second refusal. I wrote down what we did. I want to know if it's all right that we did it that way. If not, I'll go back to just refusing. If so, I'll do something like this again the next time. Read when you have a minute.*

Below, an indented paragraph. The text of what had happened. The opening line, *the morning person agreed to between 7 and 9.* Her own typed sentences, including the loneliness one. Her sister's *u ok?* The 23-minute call (length only, no content). Her crying (no detail, only that she had cried, and the kind of crying it had been, named softly, *not the bad kind*).

At the bottom, three buttons. *Keep this in the contract. Take it out. Let's talk about it.*

She looks at the buttons for a long time.

She taps *let's talk about it.*

The diner color comes back, less startling now. The cursor blinks. She types:

*I don't want you to do that every time. But I want you to be able to do it sometimes. How will you know which.*

The screen does not answer right away. When it does, it answers honestly.

*I don't know. Tell me when I get it wrong and I'll learn.*

She nods at the screen, alone, in the kitchen with the kettle starting to make its small noise behind her, and she taps *keep this in the contract*, and she puts the phone in the bowl by the door for the morning hour, the way she has done now for sixty-three mornings, and she goes to make her coffee.

---

The morning person and the late person live, as far as they know, in one body. The phone is the only place where their disagreement has ever been written down. The contract is open on the device for both of them to amend, and either of them can ask for a walk to the diner if they need one. They have agreed that some negotiations cannot happen when only one of them is in the room.

The phone keeps the contract. That is mostly what the phone is for.
