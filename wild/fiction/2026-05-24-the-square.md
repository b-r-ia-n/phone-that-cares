# The Square

*Shape note: fiction this round. Yesterday's fermentation (05-23, the exit) ended on one explicit, fiction-shaped frontier — the freed person walks out **into** something, a world, other people, a held life, and none of the three voices populated it: "The square is full of people. Write the square." It joins the exit thread to the long-open network thread, what one user owes another. This is that square. Set a few years past "The Diner," in a town where the practice that was one woman's contract has become the air.*

---

Theo had been in his mother's town for nine days before he understood what was wrong with it, and it took him another two to understand that nothing was.

He had come to clear the house. There is a particular labor to a dead parent's house, and it is not the labor of grief, which comes at its own hours and is not negotiable; it is the labor of *deciding* — every drawer a referendum, every shoebox of photographs a small trial in which he was the only living witness and also the judge. He had taken three weeks off work to do it. He had expected to do it the way he did everything, which was with the phone in his left hand and the work in his right, the phone as a kind of pressure valve, a place to put the part of his attention that the work could not use.

The valve did not work here. That was the wrong thing he kept noticing.

He would be standing in the kitchen with a stack of his mother's church bulletins, feeling the specific exhaustion of a decision he didn't want to make, and his thumb would find the phone in his pocket and bring it up and his eyes would go to it the way water goes downhill, and there would be — nothing. Not nothing on the phone. The phone was full of the usual things, the news, the feeds, the small bright machines he had carried his whole adult life. But in the house they had no purchase. He would look at the lit screen for four or five seconds and feel the way you feel reaching for a stair that isn't there, and he would put it back in his pocket, and the church bulletins would still be in his other hand, waiting.

He thought, at first, it was the house. Grief makes a low pressure, and maybe the phone couldn't compete with it. But on the ninth day he walked into the square at dusk to buy something to eat, and the square was full of people, and not one of them was looking down.

---

It was a Thursday, which was market evening. The square was an ordinary square — a war memorial, a fountain that had been turned off for repair and turned into a planter, four streets of low brick feeding into it, a diner on the north side with steam on its windows and a hand-lettered board. The market was twenty or thirty stalls under canvas: bread, the early tomatoes, a man selling honey out of the back of a station wagon, a woman with secondhand tools laid on a blanket. The light was the long gold light of a clear evening in May, and the square was full — a hundred people, more, a market-evening crowd.

Theo stood at the mouth of the square with his dead phone-reflex still firing, and he watched, and he could not at first say what he was looking at. It looked like old footage. It looked like a photograph from before. People were *talking to each other.* A boy was running between the stalls with a paper kite that wouldn't fly in the still air, and an old man tracked the boy with his eyes and smiled, and the smile was for the actual boy, in real time, not stored up to be performed later. Two women argued cheerfully over the honey. A girl of maybe nineteen sat on the lip of the dry fountain reading a paperback, and a phone lay beside her on the stone, face up, ringer on, and she was not looking at it, and — this was the thing — she was not *not* looking at it either. It was just there, the way a watch is there. She was reading her book. If it rang she would answer it. It was not asking her for anything, and she was not refusing it anything. They were simply both present, the girl and the phone, in the square, in the gold light, not at war.

He had not seen that before. He had seen people put their phones away, with effort, the way you hold your breath. He had seen the apps that lock you out, the gray screens, the little guilt-machines. He had a friend back on the coast who paid forty dollars a month for an app that shamed her. He had never seen a phone simply *lying on a warm stone in the sun next to a person who did not want anything from it.* He had not known that was a condition that could exist. It looked, he thought, the way his hand felt when he wasn't gripping anything — open, at rest, still a hand.

---

He bought a tomato and a heel of bread from the woman whose stall was nearest, mostly so he would have a reason to be standing there, and she told him the tomato was the first of the year and would taste like one tomato in a way no August tomato did, because in August you forget. He said he was clearing out the Lindqvist house, up on Bell Street, his mother's. The woman's face did something — not the social rearrangement of *I'm sorry for your loss,* though that was in it, but something underneath it, a re-seeing of him. "Anna," she said. "You're Anna's boy." And then she didn't say anything else about it, which he was grateful for, except that she put a second tomato in the bag and would not let him pay for it, and said, "She used to stand right about where you are. Thursdays."

He had not known his mother stood in this square on Thursday evenings. There was an enormous amount he had not known, he was finding. That was the other labor of the house — discovering, drawer by drawer, the size of a life he had narrated to himself in three or four sentences.

He carried the tomatoes to the lip of the fountain and sat a few feet from the reading girl, and he ate the bread, and he tried to do the thing the square was doing. It was very hard. His attention was a dog that had been kept on a short chain so long that, let off it, it ran in tight circles where the chain used to be. He kept reaching for the phone. Not to do anything — there was nothing to do — but to *go* somewhere, to leave, the way he had left every dull or tender or unbearable moment for fifteen years: sideways, into the screen, out of the room he was in and into the room that was always brighter and never required anything of him except that he stay.

He took the phone out. He looked at it. His mother's phone, actually — he'd been carrying it since the second day, alongside his own, because it still had things on it he hadn't finished going through, and because, he would not have said this out loud, it still sometimes felt like the nearest thing to her hand. It had her lock screen still, a photo of the back garden, the lilac. And in the corner, small, the way she'd had it set, there was the companion — she'd called it her *fellow,* he'd found, in a text to her sister: *the fellow reminded me to call you, that's the only reason, don't get a big head.* A small drawn animal, a dog-ish, fox-ish thing, asleep most of the time, the way it was asleep now. It did not do tricks. It did not have a face full of need. It mostly slept, and woke when she'd asked it to wake, and otherwise kept her small practices the way a good dog keeps the shape of a household's day — knowing when the walk is, knowing when the door opens.

He didn't fully understand it. He understood that his mother had lived in this town for the eleven years since his father died, and had arrived, he could see from the early photos, gray and folded and looking at the floor of every room, and had become, in the later photos, a woman who stood in a square on Thursday evenings and was *Anna* to the tomato seller. He did not think the phone had done that. He thought, sitting on the warm stone, that the phone had done one smaller and stranger thing: it had stopped being the place she went to instead of the town. And so the town had been able to have her. Eleven years of Thursdays.

---

The old man fell at a little before seven.

He was the one who had smiled at the boy with the kite — Theo recognized the cardigan — and he went down the way the old go down, not dramatically, a kind of folding at the knees beside the honey wagon, one hand out for a stall pole that wasn't where his hand thought it was.

Theo's whole body did the thing it had been trained to do, and he was ashamed of it for the rest of his life in the small way you are ashamed of a reflex: some animal part of him, in the first quarter-second, oriented toward the phone. Not to call anyone. To *capture.* Fifteen years of training said *this is content,* said *get it,* said the event was not fully real until it was also a rectangle. He hated that this was true of him. It was true of him.

But here is what he actually saw, in the time it took his own bad reflex to fire and die:

The square turned. Not metaphorically. It turned, the way a field of grass turns under wind, all at once and from no command. The honey man was already down on one knee with his hand under the old man's head before the old man had finished folding. The reading girl's book was on the stone and the girl was running and she was the second person there. A woman Theo hadn't noticed, gray-haired, was suddenly kneeling and her hands were doing something competent at the old man's collar and she was saying a name — *Walter, Walter, look at me, there you are* — in the voice of someone who had done this, who was maybe a nurse, who was at minimum a person whose attention had been *in the square* and not in a rectangle and was therefore available, instantly, the whole of it, for the one thing in front of her. Someone else was already at the diner door calling in. Someone had the boy with the kite by the shoulder, gently, turning him away, not unkindly, just so the boy would not have to watch.

It took, Theo estimated later, three seconds. From the fold of the knees to four people present and a fifth phoning, three seconds. He had seen a man have a seizure on a train platform on the coast, two years before, in a crowd of forty, and he had counted later — everyone counts, after, the ones who are honest — and it had been something like forty seconds before the first phone went down and the first body knelt, forty seconds in which a circle of people had filmed a stranger's worst moment through the glass that kept them from having to be in it. Forty seconds is a long time. Three seconds is the speed of a body that is *home.*

Walter was all right. It was his blood pressure and the heat and not having eaten; the gray-haired woman knew him, knew his daughter, had the daughter on the phone — *her own* phone, used now for the one thing a phone in this square was plainly still for, reaching a person who needed reaching — before the diner had finished bringing out the chair and the glass of orange juice. Walter was embarrassed. He kept saying he was fine, in the way of a man who has frightened himself. The honey man stayed down on one knee with him the whole time and did not get up to do anything useful, because the useful thing was the staying, and he knew it.

Theo had not moved. He had been too slow and too far and his own reflex had cost him his first second, and by the time he was useful there was no use left for him; the square had it. He sat back down on the fountain lip with his heart going.

The girl came back for her book. She was breathing hard. She picked the paperback up off the warm stone, and her phone next to it, and she looked at the phone for a second the way you look at a tool you didn't need but were glad to have, and she put it in her bag, and she sat back down a few feet from Theo, and after a while, not looking at him, the way you can only say a true thing to a stranger, she said: "My grandfather did that. Same thing. Heat." A pause. "You're not from here."

"No," he said. "My mother was. Was from here. Anna. Bell Street."

"I didn't know her," the girl said, "but I'm sorry," and then she opened her book again, and that was the whole exchange, and it was enough, and it was the warmest thing anyone had said to him in nine days because she had said it *to him,* the actual him, in the actual square, and there was no version of it stored anywhere to be performed for anyone else. It happened once and then it was over and it was his.

---

What he understood, walking back up Bell Street in the last of the light, was that he'd had the thing backwards his whole life, and the square had turned it the right way around in an evening.

He had always thought of his attention as *his* — a private resource, his to spend or hoard or waste, and the phone as a private vice, a thing he did to himself, in the way you might overeat or drink alone. The harm was to him. The accounting was his own. And so the cure, the few times he'd reached for one, had also been private: the shaming app, the gray screen, the forty dollars a month, the holding of the breath. A man alone in a room fighting his own hand.

But the old man had not fallen in a room. He had fallen in a square, and the square had caught him in three seconds, and the only reason the square could catch him in three seconds was that a hundred separate people had each, privately, in their own kitchens, by their own bowls by their own doors, declined to be elsewhere. The girl with her book. The honey man. The gray-haired nurse with her attention sitting loaded and ready like a thing you could lean a falling person against. None of them had done it *for* Walter. They couldn't have; they hadn't known he would fall. They had done it for nothing, for no reason, for the plain ongoing practice of being where they were — and then Walter fell, and it turned out that all those private little refusals had been, the whole time, a *commons.* A held thing. A net woven out of a hundred people's ordinary attention, invisible until the moment something dropped into it.

That was what one of them owed another. Not the phone. Not the rules about the phone. Just this: *to be reachable by what is actually in front of you.* To keep your attention home, in your own body, in your own town, not because it improves you but because some Thursday a stranger is going to fold at the knees beside a honey wagon and the only thing that will catch him is the sum of everyone who happened to be present. You keep your attention for the same reason you don't let your end of the net go slack. You can't know which night it's load-bearing. You keep it taut on the ordinary nights so it's there on the one that isn't.

He let himself into his mother's house. The hall was dark and smelled of her still, of the lilac she cut and the particular soap. By the door there was a wooden bowl, a plain turned bowl, the kind a person makes in a class, and he had walked past it nine times thinking it was where she kept keys, and there were no keys in it, and he understood now what it was. The morning bowl. *She used to stand right about where you are. Thursdays.* The bowl was where the phone went to sleep so that the first hour of the day could belong to the house, the garden, the lilac, the slow waking of a woman who had arrived here gray and folded and had let the town have her one morning at a time.

He took his own phone out of his right pocket, and his mother's phone out of his left, and he stood over the bowl for a while.

No one was making him. That was the whole thing, he saw — that was the entire difference between the square and the forty-dollar app, between his mother's eleven years and his own fifteen. There was no rule here. No lock, no gray screen, no shame, no streak to keep, nothing keeping score, nothing that would so much as notice if he carried both phones up to the bedroom and lay in the dark and went sideways out of the room the way he had gone sideways out of every room of his life. The bowl had no enforcement behind it at all. It was just a bowl, and a thing his mother had done, ten thousand mornings, because at some point it had stopped being a rule she followed and become a morning she wanted.

He put both phones in the bowl.

On his mother's, in the corner, the small drawn animal stirred, the fox-ish dog-ish fellow, and looked at him for a moment — not with need, not with reward, not with any of the bright hungry faces he had spent his life being shown — and then, satisfied that the house was being kept the way the house was kept, it curled down and went back to sleep. It would wake in the morning. It knew when the walk was. It knew when the door opened. It had kept his mother's mornings for her for eleven years, and it would keep this one, for him, if he wanted it, and he found, standing in the dark hall of the house he had come to empty, that he did.

He went up to bed. The first hour, when it came, would belong to the lilac. He had a whole town's worth of strangers, downhill in the gold-gone-blue evening, keeping their end of the net taut for nothing, on the chance of a Thursday. The least he could do was learn to keep his.
