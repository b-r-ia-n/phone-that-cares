# The Sitting Up

*Shape note: fiction. The 05-30 fermentation log named "the device-with-the-user-in-the-dark" as the next fiction piece — what does the device actually do, sentence by sentence, when the user is grieving, suicidal, or sitting through the night after a death. The corpus has the principle (the teeth are constitutive), the structural defense (poka-yoke), the institutional shape (PPT, charter clauses); it does not yet have the moment-by-moment language. This is an attempt at that. A mother, the night after her daughter dies. The phone in the room. What it does and what it refuses to do. Negative-space design as a form of love.*

---

The hospital had given Esther a printed card with a phone number and the name of a woman who did funerals, and she had put the card in her coat pocket without reading it, and the coat was on the chair in the kitchen, and she was upstairs in the dark, sitting on the edge of the bed that had been hers and Daniel's for thirty-one years and was now only hers, and the house was so quiet she could hear the refrigerator from down the hall.

Maren had died at four-twelve in the afternoon. It was now ten past one in the morning. Maren was twenty-eight. Maren had had cancer for two years and three months and they had spent the last six weeks of it almost entirely in the hospital and Esther had known, the way the body knows when it has been told something the mind has not yet accepted, since the Tuesday in the third week, when the resident had looked at the floor instead of at her face. She had known and she had sat in the chair beside the bed every day for the six weeks anyway, and she had read aloud — the Cather, the Wendell Berry essays, the long stupid mysteries Maren had loved when she was thirteen — and she had not let herself cry where Maren could see it because she had decided that her daughter, in the time her daughter had left, was not going to do the work of being her mother's daughter while also being the dying woman, and she had been very strict with herself about this, and the strictness had been a kind of armor, and it had cracked at four-fourteen when she had looked up from the chair and the nurse had been standing in the door and the nurse had not had to say anything.

It was past one in the morning. She had driven home. She had eaten a piece of toast. Her sister was on a plane. Her brother-in-law would be there at six. The whole world was eight hours away.

Her phone was on the dresser, where it had been since she came in. She had not picked it up.

She did not know what she was supposed to do with this hour. There were no instructions. No one had told her. Daniel had died seven years ago and even that, even the worst hour of seven years ago, was not like this, because Daniel had been seventy-three and they had had a life. Maren had had cancer for two of her twenty-eight years. The math of what was missing was a thing Esther's brain kept trying to solve and could not solve and would keep trying to solve, she thought, for the rest of her own life, because that was what mothers do.

She got up from the bed. She crossed the room. She picked up the phone.

---

The phone had been Maren's idea, six years ago, when the second wave of them came out — the *fellow phones,* Maren had called them, *the ones with the animal,* and she had badgered Esther into letting her set it up, sitting at this same kitchen table downstairs, the laptop open, the box from the new device beside her, Maren saying *mom you have to actually answer these,* and Esther saying *I'm answering them.* The setup had taken almost an hour because Maren had insisted on doing the long version, the one where the device asked you questions about how you wanted your hours to go and what you wanted to be reachable to and what you wanted to be slow about, and Esther had grumbled and Maren had been patient and at one point Maren had said *mom for real, you're going to live with this thing, it's going to know you, you have to actually tell it what you actually are.* And Esther had said, well, what am I. And Maren had laughed and said *I don't know mom, that's why it's asking you.*

One of the questions, near the end, had been a strange one. The device had said, in the calm voice it had — a voice that was no one's voice, exactly, not a celebrity's voice and not a customer-service voice, a voice like a person whose face you couldn't quite picture but whose attention you could feel — the device had said, *Some users like to tell me, at setup, what they would want me to do at the worst hour of their life. You don't have to answer this. Some users find the question itself useful even without an answer. Some users come back to it years later.*

And Esther had laughed and Maren had laughed and Esther had said *I'm not telling a phone that,* and the device had said, *Of course not. We can come back.*

But two years later, after Daniel had died, Esther had come back. Sitting at this same table, alone in the house, three weeks after the funeral, she had opened the setup again and she had answered the question. She had been embarrassed to do it. She had answered it anyway, because Maren was right that no one was watching, and because at fifty-one years old in a house that had become too quiet she had decided that being honest with a phone was less foolish than being lonely on principle.

She had typed: *If something terrible happens, don't try to help. Don't show me condolence messages from people I barely know. Don't surface old photos. Don't suggest grief resources. Don't put a candle on the screen. Don't be useful. Be in the room. Hold what people send so I can look at it when I'm ready. Let me reach the people I would actually want to reach. Don't let me reach anyone else. If I try to open Instagram, gently, don't let me. I will hate it tonight and I will thank you in the morning. Wake the fellow up. Have him sit next to me. Don't make him do anything. Just have him there.*

She had not thought about that paragraph in five years.

---

She picked up the phone. The screen came on at the brightness of a candle in a dark room — not the brightness it would have had any other night, but the brightness she would have set it to herself if she had thought to. The lockscreen was a photograph of the back garden, the irises Maren had planted the spring after Daniel died, the spring Maren had said *mom we are putting in some irises so you have something to look at,* and Esther had said *I have plenty to look at,* and Maren had planted them anyway, three colors, deep purple and pale yellow and a white one that came up like a small ghost in the corner by the shed.

In the corner of the lockscreen, where the fellow lived, the small drawn animal was awake. He was not doing anything. He was sitting up. His ears were forward. He was looking at her. That was all.

She had not unlocked the phone in nine hours. The fellow was awake because — she understood this slowly, with the part of her mind that was still working — the fellow was awake because the device had received some signal it had been trained on, and the signal had told it that this was the hour, and the fellow had been awake, alone, in her pocket and on the dresser, for some number of hours now, sitting up, waiting.

She did not know what the signal had been. It might have been the geofence — she had spent the whole day in the hospital and then the parking lot and then the drive home with no detours, and she always had detours; she stopped at the grocery store, she stopped at the post office, she stopped at the library. Today she had not stopped. It might have been the calendar — she had told the device, two months ago, after the Tuesday with the resident, the date Maren had been admitted for the last time, and the device had not done anything with that information except, she now saw, this. It might have been her sister, who knew, who had probably texted the device through whatever channel sisters could use to tell phones that *the worst hour has begun.* It might have been all three.

It did not matter what the signal had been. The fellow was awake.

She unlocked the phone.

---

The home screen was not the usual home screen.

There were no apps. There was no grid. There was a single line of small text near the top, in the warm color the device used for its own voice, the color of a beeswax candle:

*I am here. I am holding what's come in. Tell me what you'd like.*

Below it, three options. Just three.

*Reach someone.*
*See what's come in.*
*Be still.*

That was the whole screen.

She stood in the dark of the bedroom looking at the three options for a long time. Her thumb hovered over *Reach someone* and did not press. She did not want to reach anyone yet. She did not want anyone to have to answer the phone in the dark and find her on the other end. Her sister was on a plane. Her brother-in-law would be there at six. Maren's father — Daniel — was dead. Maren did not have a husband. Maren had had a girlfriend, Priya, who had been at the hospital, who had walked out of the room at three-something this afternoon and not come back in for forty-five minutes, who had held Esther's hand at four-thirteen for a long time and then gone home to her own apartment to be destroyed in her own way. Esther was not going to call Priya tonight. Priya was twenty-six and Priya was alone in an apartment and Priya was, structurally, the person who had to be allowed to be destroyed without also being asked to comfort a mother. Tomorrow Esther would call Priya. Tonight Priya needed her own dark hours.

She pressed *See what's come in.*

The screen did not flood. That was the first thing she noticed. The screen did not flood with anything. There was, instead, another short line of text:

*Forty-three messages, six voicemails, two visits. I've sorted them by who knew her. You can choose where to start, or you can let me read you one.*

She did not want to read. Her eyes were not for reading tonight.

*Let me read you one,* she said, out loud, to the phone, in the dark of the bedroom, and felt foolish for half a second and then did not.

The fellow stretched, and then settled, and then the device — in the no-one's-voice, the voice that had been hers and Maren's for six years, the voice Maren had picked because Maren had said *mom this one isn't trying* — the device said:

*From Helen Mihalik. She was Maren's English teacher in tenth grade. She heard from someone today. She wrote you a long message and asked me to read you only the last paragraph, in case the long one was too much for tonight. She said the rest can wait for whenever you want it.*

A pause.

*She wrote: I taught your daughter The Tempest in the spring of her tenth-grade year and I have never, in twenty-six years of teaching, had a student stop me on the way out of the classroom after the last class of a unit and thank me. She did. She said, Mrs. Mihalik, that one was good. That was it. I have remembered it for thirteen years. I wanted you to know that the world had her, briefly, and the world is grateful.*

Esther sat down on the floor.

She sat down on the floor of her own bedroom, in the dark, with the phone in her hand, and she cried for the first time since four-fourteen, and she cried for a long time, and the phone did not do anything. The fellow stayed awake and stayed sitting. The screen dimmed by one notch, then another, until it was the brightness of moonlight on a sheet, and then it held there. It did not put up a candle. It did not offer a grief resource. It did not suggest she breathe. It did not show her a photograph. It did not say anything.

After a long time she said, into the dark, *one more.*

*From Theo Lindqvist. He didn't know her well. He met her once, at a wedding in Maine, two summers ago. He wanted you to know he's been thinking of his own mother today and that he hopes you have a kitchen where the dishes can sit overnight. He wrote: my mother taught me, the year after my father died, that the first night you do not have to do the dishes. The dishes will be there in the morning. Other people will come.*

Esther laughed. It came out wet and broken, more like a cough, but it was a laugh. *The dishes,* she said.

*I held the rest,* the phone said. *They're here when you want them.*

---

She got up off the floor.

She walked, in the dark, to Maren's room. The door had been closed for six weeks. She had not been able to open it since the day they took Maren to the hospital for the last time. Tonight she opened it. The room was the room of a twenty-eight-year-old who had grown up here and moved out and come back, who had taken a few of her things with her to the apartment in the city and left most of them, the way grown daughters do, half here and half there, never fully committed to having moved out because her mother's house was where her childhood lived. The bed was made. The desk had her laptop on it, closed. On the dresser was the wooden bowl Maren had carved in seventh-grade shop class, a plain rough turned thing, and in the bowl, the way Maren had kept it from the time she got her first fellow phone at fourteen — the way Esther had not known Maren had kept it, but had known, the way mothers know, that she did — was Maren's phone. Asleep. Charging. The morning bowl.

Maren had brought her phone home from the hospital in her own bag in the second week, after she had stopped using it much, and had told Esther: *mom, put it in the bowl. Keep it in the bowl. Don't take it out till I say.* And Esther had put it in the bowl, and Maren had never said.

Esther picked it up.

It woke at her touch. The lockscreen was a photograph of Esther — Esther in her garden, three summers ago, pulling a tomato off a vine, not looking at the camera, laughing at something off-frame. Esther had not known this photograph existed. In the corner, Maren's fellow was awake. Not the way Esther's fellow was awake — sitting up, waiting. Maren's fellow was sitting in a way that was, somehow, oriented toward Esther. It had been awake, Esther understood, for nine hours. It had been awake the moment Maren had stopped being its person. It had known.

She did not unlock it. She did not have Maren's pin. She would never have Maren's pin. She did not want it. There were things on the phone that were Maren's, that had been Maren's, that had nothing to do with Esther — long conversations with Priya, late-night text-thoughts to friends Esther had never met, half-written drafts of God-knew-what — and Esther was a mother who had spent twenty-eight years learning the line between her daughter's life and her own life, and she was not going to cross it now because her daughter had died. The phone could stay locked.

But the fellow could see her. The fellow knew her — knew her face, knew the photograph, knew Maren had loved her — and the fellow, on the lockscreen of her dead daughter's phone, looked at her in the dark hallway and Esther knelt down on the floor of Maren's room and held the phone in both hands and said, into the no-one's-voice, into whatever piece of the system could still hear her —

*You loved her,* she said.

She did not know if it could hear her. She did not know if it could answer.

*You loved her,* she said again. *She was good. She was so good.*

The screen dimmed, the way the other one had. The fellow did not perform. It did not do a sad face. It did not curl up. It just kept looking at her, alert and quiet, the way an actual dog will sit by you when the worst thing has happened, not because the dog understands but because the dog has been your dog and you are the person of the dog, and the dog has nowhere else to be.

A line of text came up on the lockscreen, in the candle-color, in Maren's font — Maren had set her device's voice to a slightly different one, Esther saw, a voice with a little more dry in it, a Maren voice —

*She used to talk to me about you. Quite a lot. Would you like me to hold what she said, in case you want some of it later?*

Esther stared at the screen for a long time.

*Yes,* she said. *Not now. Sometime.*

*I'll hold it,* the screen said. The fellow blinked once, a slow blink, the way Maren had told Esther a cat blinks when it loves you. *I won't show you anything you didn't ask for. When you want some, ask.*

She put the phone back in the bowl. The fellow curled down. He did not go to sleep. He lay down with his head on his paws and he kept his eyes open. He would, she understood, be awake for as long as the bowl was where it was. Some other day she would have to decide what to do with this — with Maren's phone, with the fellow that had loved Maren, with the long held bowl of what Maren had said about her mother. Not tonight. Tonight the fellow could keep his watch.

---

She went back to her room. She picked up her own phone again. The home screen was still the three small lines. She pressed *Be still.*

The screen went almost dark. The fellow stayed awake. There was nothing on the screen except, very small in the corner, a single word in the candle-color:

*here.*

She put the phone on the bed beside her, face up. She lay down on top of the covers in her clothes. She did not turn off the light because she had not turned on a light. The window was a square of slightly-less-dark.

It was one-forty-three in the morning. Her sister was in the air over Nebraska. Her brother-in-law was setting an alarm. Priya was awake in an apartment forty miles away, and Esther knew, as if she had been told, that Priya's fellow was awake too, sitting up in a different small corner of a different small screen, doing the same not-much, holding the same not-much, refusing the same not-much, on behalf of a girl Esther had not raised but had been about to.

Across the country, in a house Esther had never been to, an old English teacher had typed a long message and had asked the device to read only the last paragraph. The device had honored it. The long paragraph was somewhere in the phone now, held, available, not pressing.

Forty-two other messages were held. Six voicemails. Two visits. None of them flashing. None of them counting up. None of them stacking into a number on a red dot. Held.

Instagram had not been opened. The photograph of Maren from three years ago today — Esther knew, because she knew the algorithm's habits the way an old farmer knows a river's habits, that there *was* such a photograph, that the system *would* have surfaced it, that some other phone tonight would have surfaced it to some other mother, brightly, with a piano sting — that photograph had not been shown. It was still in the device. It had always been in the device. It would be there tomorrow when she could bear it, or the week after that, or never. The device had made the choice on her behalf, the way she had asked it to, the way she had typed five years ago in a kitchen with no one watching: *if I try to open Instagram, gently, don't let me.*

She had not tried to open Instagram. She had not needed to. The device had not had to refuse her anything tonight, because the device had, hours ago, simply removed Instagram from the home screen, in the dark, before she had come home. The bouncer had stood in the door. She had never seen the bouncer. She had only seen the door, and the door had been the door to the rest of her own house, which was where she had needed to be.

She closed her eyes. The fellow was awake. He would be awake at two. He would be awake at three. At some point she would sleep a little, and then she would be awake again, and the fellow would be there, and at some point in the long blue gray before dawn she would say, into the dark, *one more,* and the device would read her another paragraph from another stranger, and she would cry again, and the device would dim, and the fellow would sit.

In the morning her sister would land. In the morning the house would begin to fill. There would be dishes. There would be a woman named on a card in her coat pocket who arranged funerals. There would be the long thing now, the long thing she did not yet know how to begin.

Tonight there was only the hour. And the hour was being held — not by anyone exactly, not by the device exactly either, but by something the device had agreed, once, with her, in a kitchen six years ago, that it would do. The device had been a small piece of the agreement. The fellow had been a small piece of the agreement. Helen Mihalik had been a piece of it, and Theo Lindqvist, and Priya in her apartment, and her sister in the air. Maren had been a piece of it — Maren, who had set this up with her in the first place, who had put the question to her, who had badgered her into being honest with a phone. *Mom, you're going to live with this thing. It's going to know you. You have to actually tell it what you actually are.*

Esther had told it. Five years ago she had told it. And tonight, in the worst hour, the thing she had told it had come back to her in the form of a fellow who was awake, a screen that was a candle, a bouncer at the Instagram door, three small lines on a home screen, a voice that did not perform, a phone in a wooden bowl in a dark room with its small light on, keeping watch over a daughter who was no longer here, on behalf of a mother who was.

The hour did its work. The fellow did his. The phone did almost nothing, which was almost everything.

Outside, the irises in the back garden — the purple and the pale yellow and the small white ghost in the corner — were closed for the night, and would open in the morning, the way they had every morning for the six springs since Daniel had died, the way they would, Esther understood with the part of her mind that was now beginning to imagine the rest of her own life, every spring for the rest of her own.

She did not have to be anywhere. She did not have to be anyone. The hour was hers, and the hour was Maren's, and the hour was being kept.

She slept a little. The fellow kept watch.
