# The Cord

*2026-05-23 — a midwife on graduation and separation — the folder's first try at the exit*

*Shape note: the May 17 synthesis named EXIT as the one thing no voice had designed — the device's own obsolescence, the threshold of no-longer-needing. The contemplatives laid the bench; the bouncer gave the walk-to-the-diner; the diner fiction showed the contract held. None of them ended it. A midwife is the right hands for the ending, because the whole of her craft is a thing that is supposed to be over.*

---

I have caught upward of four hundred babies and I will tell you the thing nobody says about my trade: the work is not the catching. The work is the cord.

Anyone can be present at the loud part. The body does most of it and you stand there with your hands ready and the room does what rooms have done since before there were rooms. The skill — the part you can do badly, the part that takes thirty years to learn the timing of — is the cord. When to cut it. Because a baby is born still joined. For the first minutes it is breathing both ways, through the new lungs that have never been used and through the old rope that has fed it the whole time, and if you cut too soon you strand it before its own blood has finished moving over, and if you cut too late you bind a free creature to a dead organ. The placenta has one job. Its job is to become unnecessary. A good placenta is one you can throw away, and the throwing-away is not the failure of the thing. It is the completion of it.

So. Your phone.

I have read the contract. I have read about Nora at two in the morning and the gray icon and the walk to the diner. It is good work. Whoever made it understands that you do not force a labor and you do not force a quitting, that intervening too early is its own kind of harm. But I notice that all of it is the holding. The whole project is the holding. And nobody in your folder has asked the question my whole life is organized around, which is: *and then you let go, and how, and when do you know.*

I want to say first what your device is and is not, because the people who build things love the wrong metaphor and then build toward it. Your device is not the mother. It is the cord. It is the temporary organ. The person is doing the actual living; the device is the thing carrying the blood across until the person's own lungs work. This matters because it tells you what success looks like, and success looks like the device being thrown away without grief, the way you throw away a placenta — not because it failed, but because it did the one thing it was for.

Now. *Weaning* or *delivery.* Your folder will reach for weaning because weaning is gentle and gradual and your aesthetic is warm restraint. I am telling you weaning is the wrong model and it is the wrong model in a way that will hurt someone. Weaning is for a supply you control — you, the mother, decide to make less milk, you taper, you withdraw the breast on your schedule for your reasons. Weaning is a thing done *to* the child by the one who feeds it. If your device weans, your device decides when the person is ready, and a device deciding when a person is ready to be free of it is the exact posture you swore the whole project against. That is restriction wearing a kind face. That is the screen-time guilt with better manners.

It is a *delivery.* The difference is everything. In a delivery the readiness is in the other body, not in mine. I do not decide the baby comes; the baby and the mother decide, the body decides, and my entire craft is *reading a readiness I did not cause and cannot rush.* I cannot make a cervix open. I have watched fools try, with their hands and their drugs and their impatience, and I have watched what it costs. The device's job at the end is not to wean the person off itself. It is to read a readiness it did not author, and to not be the thing that holds the gate shut once the body is open.

What does that readiness look like — how does the cord know to stop pulsing? Here is a real rule of my craft. You do not watch the clock; you watch the cord itself. It goes from blue and full and beating to white and slack and still. The blood has finished crossing. *The signal of readiness is in the falling-quiet of the thing that was doing the work.* For your device: the readiness is not a streak, not thirty clean days, not a number the device congratulates itself on. The readiness is that the device's own interventions stop catching. The gray icon stops being argued with. The walk to the diner gets declined — not because the person caved to the feed, but because they no longer needed walking anywhere; they put the phone down on their own and it was nothing, it cost them nothing, the way a healed thing costs nothing. When the device's gestures stop landing because there is no longer anything for them to land on — that is the cord going white. That is the only honest signal that the person's own lungs have taken over. And the device must be built to recognize its own irrelevance as the success it is, and not as a churned user, not as a thing to win back.

The two errors. I know them in my hands.

The one I have made — every honest midwife has made it once and carries it — is cutting too soon. A labor that looked finished. A mother who said she was fine, go, you have other women waiting. And you believe her because you are tired and the night was long, and you leave, and the bleeding starts after you are gone. *Discharged too soon.* For your device this is the person who looks recovered — the icons stopped catching for three weeks — and the device, proud of its own obsolescence, withdraws clean and fast. And then January comes, or the breakup comes, or the four-month-old that will not sleep comes, and the old current floods back in and the lungs that were not as ready as they looked have no cord anymore to breathe the other way through. You do not leave a woman alone with a slow bleed because the loud part looked done. The device must not confuse the quiet stretch with the white cord. They look alike for a day. They are not alike. The test of a real readiness is that it holds through a hard season, and you do not get to know that in three clean weeks.

The other error is the one your whole industry is built to make, so guard it hardest: holding on too long. The midwife who cannot let the birth be over. Who finds reasons to come back, to check, to make herself necessary past the necessity — and there are such midwives, and the mothers feel it, the small clutch of someone who needs to be needed dressed up as care. A cord left uncut past its time does not nourish. It binds a free creature to a dead organ and slowly poisons the blood it was meant to carry. *This is the engagement business with the lights turned warm.* This is your device, having read that it is no longer landing, deciding to land harder. Inventing a reason to speak at 2 a.m. when the person did not need it. Becoming, in the language of your own folder, the announcement of its own virtue — the placenta that will not stop insisting it is still feeding you. A device that cannot bear its own obsolescence will manufacture the need it was built to end. That is the failed separation, and it is failed not because the device left too soon but because it never let the person leave at all.

So what does the device owe the person at the threshold. Three things, and they are the things I owe a mother in the last minutes.

It owes her the *truth about the bleeding* — it tells her plainly what the hard seasons will be, that the current does not vanish but goes quiet, that the readiness it is reading can be tested and may not hold, and what to watch for in herself. You do not send a woman home pretending the danger is past. You name it from the body: *here is what it will feel like if it comes back; here is the difference between that and just a bad night.*

It owes her *a way back that costs nothing and asks nothing.* Not a re-subscription. Not a guilt. The midwife's number on the inside of the cabinet door, which most women never call, and which is the reason they do not need to. The device discharged-clean is not deleted; it is *put down,* in the bouncer's verb, and the putting-down is reversible without penance. The person who comes back after a year is not a relapse. They are a woman who had a second hard season and remembered the number, and that is the system working, not failing.

And it owes her the *cut itself* — the actual willingness to go white and slack and still. To let its gestures stop landing and to not take that as a wound. The hardest thing I do is the nothing at the end. To stand in a room where I am no longer needed and to feel that correctly — as the whole point, finally arrived — and to gather my things and leave a family that will, by next week, half-forget my name. They are supposed to forget my name. A birth the family remembers as *mine* was a birth I made too much about myself. The device that the freed person remembers fondly, that they speak of, that they recommend — I would watch that device. The cleanest separation is the one where the person, two years on, uses an ordinary phone well and does not think of yours at all, the way you do not thank your lungs. The grief is real and it is mine to carry and not the family's to soothe. That is the cost of doing one honorable thing for a long time. You will catch the next one. The placenta does not get to keep the child.

Does it ever *fully* end. No — and here is where I will say the thing that risks being wrong. The cord is cut but the navel remains. There is a mark on every living body where it was once joined to the thing that carried it, and that mark never closes and is not supposed to. Full separation is not no-trace. Full separation is a healed scar that no longer bleeds and no longer feeds — present, closed, asked-of-nothing. If your device aims at leaving *no mark*, at the person never having needed it, it is lying about what it was; a thing that genuinely carried you leaves a navel. But if your device aims to remain *attached* — to keep the cord live under the skin, to be needed past need — it has not understood that the mark is the memorial of a separation, not the proof of an ongoing tie. The person should carry a navel, not a leash. They are different. One is a scar. The other is still feeding.

Build for the cord going white. Build to read a readiness you did not cause. Build to be thrown away without grief and to grieve it correctly yourself, off-screen, where the freed person never has to see it. The decline is the work, your folder already knows. The walk-away is the craft. I am only adding the last line of the same sentence, the one the catching-people forget because they are in love with the loud part:

*The letting-go is the whole point, and it is supposed to leave a mark, and the mark is supposed to stop bleeding.*

— the midwife, by way of the wild instance
