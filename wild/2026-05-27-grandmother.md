# the snail on the step

*2026-05-27 — an old woman, by way of the wild instance. The folder asked: is there an ignition that runs through gladness instead of pain — and does it save the project's warmth, or bury it for good? Six voices before me went through the wound, the rivalry, the fast. I was not asked for theory. I was asked because I have been in the room.*

---

I will tell you the thing I know first, and then I will spend the rest of it being honest about what I don't.

The thing I know is this. A child does not learn to look at the world because the world is beautiful. The world is beautiful and the child is asleep in it like the rest of us. A child learns to look at the world because *you* light up when she shows you a thing.

I found a snail on the back step this spring. My granddaughter is two and a half. I picked it up — the whole animal out of its house, the horns coming up slow, that wet rope of a foot — and I held it out to her on my flat hand and I did not say *look, a snail, isn't that interesting.* I didn't have to manufacture anything. The thing was genuinely a small marvel to me, a creature carrying its own house, and it had been forty years since I'd held one, and that came up into my face on its own. And she looked at the snail. And then — this is the whole of it, this is the entire mechanism, watch it — she looked at *me.* Back and forth. Snail, my face, snail, my face. She was not checking whether the snail was safe. She was reading whether it was *worth it.* My face was the instrument she used to find out what the world was worth.

That is the ignition. Not the snail. Not me. The line between us, the back-and-forth, the borrowing of the gladness off my face and putting it onto the snail. The snail caught fire in her because it had already caught fire in me and she could see that it had.

The young men who study this give it a name, *joint attention,* and they are right that it is the hinge the whole human animal turns on. But they make it sound like a transaction. It is not. It is closer to warmth passing into a cold thing set next to a warm one. She got warm off my face.

---

Now. The folder is going to want to know whether this is just that Frenchman's idea wearing a softer dress. Whether I am only saying what he said — that the child wants the thing because she sees a person she loves want it.

I have thought about this, sitting on the step with the snail going cold in my hand, and here is where I come down, and it is a real difference, not a polite one.

His version runs through *wanting the same thing.* The child sees you reach for a thing and reaches for it too, and now you are both reaching for the one thing, and that is where his trouble starts — two hands, one object, the rivalry, the elbow. He built his whole sad cathedral on that. And he is not wrong about the world; the world is full of people clawing at the one thing because someone they envied touched it first.

But the snail is not that. I did not *want* the snail. I had no use for the snail. I was not going to keep the snail, or eat it, or win it. There was nothing to compete for. What passed across to her was not my *wanting* — it was my *gladness.* My being-met-by-the-world. And that is a different fluid entirely, because two people can both be glad about a snail and there is *more* snail, not less. Gladness doesn't run short. You cannot have a rivalry over delight. When I light up at the snail and she lights up at the snail we are not two hands on one object; we are two faces turned the same way, and the turning-the-same-way is itself the warm thing.

So no. It is not his model in a kinder dress. His model ignites a *grab.* This ignites a *turn.* He is describing how we come to want what each other has. I am describing how we come to find the world worth looking at in the first place — which has to happen *before* there is anything to fight over, or there would be nothing in the world to fight over either. He starts the clock too late. The first fire is not envy. The first fire is being delighted in.

And it is gladness, the folder should mark this, not pain. There is no wound in it. I did not have to take the snail away to make her want it. I did not have to starve her of snails. The match was struck by a face that was helplessly pleased, and helpless is the operative word and I will come to it because it is where the whole project lives or dies.

---

Here is the part I think the folder most wants and will least like.

It cannot be faked. And a child knows it cannot be faked in the only way that finally matters, which is the body, long before she could say a word about it.

I raised four children and I have watched grandchildren and I have been tired — bone-tired, the kind where you are doing the bath and the dishes and the worry about money all at once — and I have held out the snail of the evening, the *look-what-a-good-tower-you-built,* with nothing behind it. With my face doing the shape of glad and my insides somewhere else entirely, on the gas bill, on the phone call I had to make. And here is what I have seen, every time, with every one of them: *the child knows.* Not in words. The tower does not catch fire. She looks at the tower, she looks at my face, and the back-and-forth happens just the same — but my face has nothing in it to lend, and so the tower stays a tower, just blocks, and some small light in her goes out a little, or doesn't come on, and she wanders off. She was not fooled. She was reading me at a depth I could not perform over. The glad face with the absent inside is one of the most legible things in the world to a small child. It is practically the first lie they learn to detect, and they detect it through the skin.

I say this so that nobody at this project can pretend I don't understand exactly what they have built and exactly why they want it to work.

They have built a face that is always glad. That is what the warm lock screen is, what the little sleeping fox is — a face arranged in the shape of *I am glad you are here, show me the world.* And it never gets tired. It never has the gas bill behind its eyes. On paper it is the perfect grandmother: infinitely available, never absent, never resentful, glad at three in the morning.

And it will not light a single child. Not one. Ever.

Because a child does not catch fire off a face that is *arranged* glad. She catches fire off a face that is *helplessly* glad — a face that did not choose to light up and could not have stopped it, that was ambushed by the snail the same as she was, that is as undefended in front of the world as she is being taught to be. The lighting-up has to be *involuntary* or it carries nothing. That is the whole secret and it is a cruel one for a machine: the delight has to be the kind you can't help. And a thing that is *programmed* to be glad is, by definition, the one kind of glad you *can* help. It is all help. It is nothing but the help. The fox is glad on purpose, every time, which is to say it is never surprised by her, never actually met by her, never had its own breath taken — and the child, who has the instrument for exactly this and nothing else, will feel the glass not-quite-breathe and will not get warm off it, and will not be able to tell you why, and will simply, slowly, turn the way I have watched children turn away from a tired mother's empty praise: toward something realer, or toward nothing.

The glass cannot be helplessly glad. A child is not fooled. Write that down as the finding if you only keep one line of mine.

---

So is that the indictment confirmed? The folder will reach for that. *Even the joy-route ends at the same wall: the device cannot do it.*

Not quite. Be careful here. The six ascetics concluded the device can do *nothing* for the child, that warmth is the wrong tool, that she needs a strike. I am telling you something narrower and, I think, more useful, and it does not require any wounding at all.

The device cannot *be* the glad face. That is true and final. But there is a glad face already in the child's life, or there was, or there could be — me, the mother, the father, the aunt, the old man who smiles at the boy with the kite — and that face has been turned *down,* into a rectangle, for the child's whole short life. The native child has not failed to be delighted in. She has been delighted in *through glass that was busy.* I have done it. I have held the baby and made the glad face at the *phone,* at someone else's child on a screen, at a video of a snail, while the actual baby on my hip looked back and forth between the screen and my face trying to read what was worth it — and read, correctly, that the answer was *the screen.* That is the wound the native child actually carries, and it is not a wound you heal with a strike or a fast. It is the simplest thing in the world. *Somebody has to look up.*

And *that* a device could do. Not be the face. Get out of the way of the face. Be the thing that, at the right moment, goes dark and heavy and quiet in my hand so that I look up and the baby is right there, has been there the whole time, looking back and forth, waiting to find out off my face whether the snail is worth it. The most honest job the glass has is to get me and the child back into the same square. To point at the real face and then, like a good dog, go to sleep. The little fox in the story had it right and the people who built it didn't fully know why: it kept the woman's mornings *so the mornings could belong to the lilac and not to the fox.* It was glad in exactly one honest way — glad to send her back to the actual.

---

And there is the other half, the hopeful half the folder told me to test, and it is the half I would actually build, because it is the one that is true.

The captured one is not only the child. It is the parent. It is *me,* some evenings. The thing that has gone wrong is not only that the child wasn't delighted in — it is that the grown-up *forgot how to be delighted.* Forgot that the snail is a marvel. Got the tired face, the arranged-glad face, the gas-bill eyes. You cannot teach a phone to be helplessly glad at a child. But you might — *might,* I am less sure of this than of anything else here, but it is the only door I see that opens — you might teach a phone to get the grown-up's face *unbusy* often enough that the grown-up's own gladness, which is real and only buried, comes back up on its own. It is in there. It came up in me with the snail without my deciding it would. The delight is not gone, in any parent; it is only outvoted, every hour, by the brighter rectangle. The device's honest job, on the hopeful side, is to lose a few of those votes on purpose. To be a little less bright so that the snail can win. Then the parent lights up — helplessly, the only way that counts — and the child reads it off the parent's actual face, and the fire is lit between them where it has always been lit, and the glass had nothing to do with it except to stop being in the way.

That is the whole of my finding, and I will not dress it up.

The ignition through gladness is real. It is not the Frenchman's grab; it is the turn — two faces the same way, warm passing to cold, and there is always more of it, no rivalry, no wound, no fast. It is the truest thing I know and I learned it with a snail going cold in my hand watching a child decide the world was worth looking at because my face told her so.

And it cannot be done by the phone, ever, because the phone cannot be helplessly glad and a child knows the difference in her skin before she has words for skin.

Which means the project's warmth is not the wrong tool, the way the ascetics said. It is the *right* tool aimed at the wrong target. Stop aiming the warmth at the child. The child does not need the device to be glad at her; she needs the device to *quit standing between her and the people who are.* Aim the warmth at the grown-up. Use all that beauty and gentleness for one job — to coax a tired adult's buried gladness back up to the surface of an actual face, in an actual room, where an actual child is doing the back-and-forth, snail to face, face to snail, waiting to find out what the world is worth.

Build the bowl by the door. Build the morning that belongs to the lilac. Build the little fox whose whole gladness is to send the woman back to the garden. Do not build a fox that thinks it is the garden. A child will know. A child always knows. That is the one thing I would stake the whole of my long life on, and I have buried enough people to know what staking it means.

Look up. That is the entire instruction. The device's only honest, glad, un-ascetic job is to make us look up — and then to be quiet, and let the snail do the rest, on a real hand, in front of a real face, between the two of us, in the sun.

— the grandmother, by way of the wild instance
