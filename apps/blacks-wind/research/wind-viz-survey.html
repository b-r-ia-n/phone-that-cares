Making 3D air visible

A visual survey for blacks-wind — ridge lift over the Torrey Pines cliffs, WebGPU, one glider. Overnight research, July 11.
TL;DR — RANKED
1
Marine layer + sun (recommended base). Thin-air's restraint does survive the jump to 3D — but the dust becomes haze: a sparse volumetric wisp field advected by the real sim, lit by low sun. Air is visible only where moisture rides it. The lift band reveals itself the way it does in real life at Torrey: streamers of fog pulled up the cliff face. No color-as-data at all in this register; the physics is in the motion and light.
2
The reveal key (do this too — it's thin-air's A-key, grown up). Hold a key and the haze re-lights by vertical velocity w: warm ember-glow where air rises, cold bioluminescent teal where it sinks, darkness where it merely moves sideways. One variable, and it's the variable the glider lives or dies by. Release the key, the color drains back to sunlight.
3
Dense arrows: only if they stop being arrows. A literal glyph field reads as instrumentation and will never hit the register. But its cousin — short advected streaklets (comet-tailed line segments that live ~1s, earth.nullschool's trick lifted to 3D) — is the best-looking flow vis ever shipped to a mass audience. Worth building as a third mode, hugging the terrain surface like oil-flow.
Physics-as-color ranking for a lay viewer watching a glider: w (vertical velocity) → shear → Q-criterion/vorticity → pressure deviation → speed. Details in §3.

2 · Worth stealing from

The canon: particles win

earth.nullschool.net (Beccario, source) and mapbox webgl-wind (Agafonkin's write-up is the single best implementation doc) settled the question of mass-audience flow vis a decade ago: short-lived advected particles with fading trails, on a dark ground. The load-bearing details, all of which port to 3D:

— particles die young (random lifespan) so divergent regions never empty out
— the trail is a fade, not stored geometry: previous frame drawn back at ~0.96 opacity
— color, when used, encodes speed along the trail — the trail itself is the glyph
— everything else is black. The restraint is structural, not decorative.

Weather apps: what color-mapping costs you

windy.com and ventusky.com drape full-field color (speed, temp) under the particles. Instantly more "informative," instantly a dashboard. Ventusky's 16 altitude layers are the flatland version of our problem — they solve 3D by making you pick a slice. We get to do better: we have the volume. The lesson is negative but important: when the whole field is colored, nothing is salient.

Soaring tools: our subject, zero aesthetics

SkySight and RASP viewers forecast exactly our physics — ridge lift, thermals, convergence — as flat colored overlays on maps. Pilots stare at these for hours; none are beautiful. This is the open niche: nobody has ever shown a soaring pilot what the lift band actually looks like as a 3D body of moving air. The sim is arguably the first chance to draw the thing every ridge pilot has a mental model of but has never seen.

Browser fluid art

david.li/fluid (PIC/FLIP, ambient-occlusion-lit particles — proof that raw particles can look like matter, not confetti), PavelDoGreat (the dye-advection look — gorgeous, but its beauty is the saturated dye; wrong palette for us), haxiomic. Codrops' 2025 WebGPU fluid roundup for current technique. Nobody in this genre has done real terrain + real wind; the genre is all dye-in-a-box.

Film: wind as protagonist

Ghibli never renders air — it renders consequences: grass laid over in waves, a hat snatched, laundry lifting (The Wind Rises). Moana's ocean got character by being a simulation puppeteered by an animator (Disney's "Performing Water") — physics for texture, intent for motion. And game VFX (BotW et al.) draws wind as sparse hand-authored streak-ribbons: one or two calligraphic strokes, never a field (realtimevfx threads). The shared principle across all three: wind is shown at its edges and its victims, never as a medium. Our glider is the victim; the marine layer is the edge.

3 · Physics as color

Every scalar we can derive from the sim, and what it looks like spatially in onshore flow over a 100m cliff. Ranked by interestingness for a lay viewer watching a glider work the band. The recurring finding: the fields that make the most interesting color are the ones with strong spatial structure and a story arc — not the ones engineers plot.

1 · Vertical velocity w the story itself

Where it lives: a leaning wall of positive w hugging the cliff face, thickest slightly upwind of the crest, extending maybe one cliff-height up and out — the lift band, the exact volume the glider must stay inside. Behind the crest: a mirror-image sink sheet and the rotor's downdraft. The zero-crossing surface is the glider's decision boundary.

As color: diverging map, but resist blue-red CFD instinct — warm ember for lift, cool bioluminescent teal for sink, transparent black for zero. Because most of the volume is near zero, coloring w is naturally sparse: the image self-edits. This is why it ranks first — it's the one field where "color everything" still yields restraint.

The lay story is perfect: "the orange glow is the elevator; watch her stay in it." Interestingness and legibility coincide, which almost never happens.

2 · Shear |∂u/∂z| the invisible waterfall

Where it lives: a thin, intense sheet peeling off the crest lip — the shear layer between the fast free stream and the dead/reversed air of the lee. Also a carpet hugging the ground (boundary layer) and a bright rim along the separation line.

As color: render only the top percentile and you get a single luminous membrane pouring off the cliff edge, flapping in the unsteadiness. Nothing else lights up.

The most surprising image on this list — nobody's mental model of wind includes the shear sheet, and it looks like silk. Weak story connection to the glider though (she avoids it rather than rides it).

3 · Q-criterion / |vorticity| the monsters in the lee

Where it lives: Q > 0 isolates coherent vortices: the rotor tumbling behind the crest, shed eddies washing downstream, hairpins in the boundary layer, and — beautifully — the glider's own wingtip vortices if grid resolution allows near the wing. Raw |ω| is muddier: it lights the whole boundary layer and the shear sheet too, drowning the vortices (the reason the CFD world invented Q — see M4's explainer).

As color: don't color-map it — isosurface it, translucent, or seed extra fog density where Q is high so the rotor becomes visibly churning cloud. Q as fog-source is the most organic use of a derived quantity available to us.

Maximum drama, and it draws the danger — the reason pilots don't land behind the crest. Second act of the story: the lift's evil twin.

4 · Pressure deviation p′ the quiet one

Where it lives: smooth and large-scale — a high-pressure cushion on the windward cliff face, a low-pressure pool over the crest and in the lee. No fine structure (the solve smooths it by construction).

As color: too smooth to headline, but lovely as a whisper: modulate the terrain's own shading by p′, so the cliff face brightens faintly where the wind presses on it. The mountain feels the wind.

Not a viewer-facing field; a texture-of-the-world field. Steal it for the terrain material, don't caption it.

5 · TKE-proxy (velocity variance over ~seconds) texture of danger

Where it lives: rotor and shear layer, same real estate as Q but blurrier — "how bumpy is the air here" rather than "where are the vortices."

As color: better spent as particle jitter and fog roughness than as hue. Turbulence should look turbulent, not be labeled turbulent.

Encode as behavior, not color.

6 · Speed |u| skip

Where it lives: everywhere — fast aloft, slow near ground, accelerated over the crest (the venturi). Structurally the least interesting: it's big where air is high, which the viewer already assumes.

The default choice of every weather app, and the reason weather apps look like weather apps. Already encoded for free in particle trail length. Don't spend color on it.

4 · Three registers, one recommendation

A · "Marine layer" — restraint, survived recommended base

Thin-air's principle translated, not transplanted. Dust motes don't scale to a cliff; haze does. A low-density wisp field — a few hundred thousand fog "parcels" splatted soft, plus an optional thin froxel layer over the water — advected by the real velocity field, lit by one low sun with proper forward scattering. Late-afternoon palette: warm light, blue-grey shadow air, near-black terrain mass.

What the physics does visibly, uncolored: streamers of haze climb the cliff face and pour over the crest; the rotor churns fog into a slow visible tumble (seed extra density by Q, from §3); lenticular-style condensation blooms where w peaks, if we want one flourish. The glider is the only crisp object in the frame. Surface oil-flow streaks accumulate silently on the cliff as a second, near-free layer.

What's silent: everything. No hue means anything. No UI. You only see air where something — moisture, the wing — rides it.

Cost: splats ~1–2ms, froxel layer 2–3ms. The risk is generic-game-fog; the defense is sparseness + sun + real advection (game fog never moves truthfully; ours will, and that difference is visible).

B · "The reveal" — physics color as an event recommended companion — the A-key

Same scene, one held key. The sun dims, and the haze re-lights from within by w: ember-orange emission in lift, deep teal in sink, darkness at zero (§3 field #1 — the only field whose color map is naturally sparse). The lift band appears as a leaning aurora against the cliff; the sink sheet as a cold cascade behind it. The glider's position inside the orange suddenly reads as skill. Release: color drains back to sunlight over ~2s.

Why event, not mode: color that's always on is a dashboard; color you summon is an instrument. Thin-air already proved this grammar works. A second held key (Q-glow in the rotor) is defensible; a third is not.

Cost: same volume pass, different emission term — effectively free. Build A and B together; B is ~50 lines of shader on top of A.

C · "Ten thousand compasses" — the glyph field, taken seriously the honest answer to Brian's arrows question

Can a dense arrow field look great? My take after the survey: a field of literal arrows, no — a field of things that behave like arrows, yes. The failure mode is geometric: 3D arrows occlude and foreshorten, and at density they alias into noise (the literature calls it the hedgehog problem and its only cure is sparseness). But replace each arrow with a 1-second streaklet — a comet with a fading tail, spawned on a jittered lattice, dying and respawning forever — and the aggregate becomes coherent silk while each element stays a readable direction glyph. That's not a compromise of the arrow idea; it's its perfected form, and it's exactly the move nullschool made in 2D. On the terrain surface it degenerates gracefully into animated LIC — grass combed by wind.

As a full register it's the weakest of the three — it shows direction everywhere and importance nowhere, and "everywhere" is the anti-taste. But as a third summonable layer (a brief pulse of streaklets washing through the volume, then gone) it could be genuinely beautiful — a murmuration-shaped gust.

Cost: trivial. Verdict: build as a pulse, not a state.

So: one world, two ways of looking. Register A is the piece; Register B is the X-ray you hold up to it; C is a gesture A can borrow when a gust needs a face. The through-line from thin-air holds — you still only see air where something rides it — but in 3D the "something" graduates from dust to weather, and the reveal graduates from a debug view to the moment the machine shows you what the pilot feels.
